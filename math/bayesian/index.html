Bayesian estimation: beliefs that update

Maximum likelihood gives one best guess. The Bayesian approach keeps a whole distribution of plausible values, the posterior, and updates it as data arrive. From that one object you can read a best guess, an error bar, and a prediction that knows how unsure it is.

What is Bayesian estimation, in plain words?

The idea

Treat the unknown parameter \(\theta\) as something you are uncertain about, and describe that uncertainty with a probability distribution. Before the data, that is the prior \(p(\theta)\). The data enter through the same likelihood \(p(\text{data}\mid\theta)\) that maximum likelihood uses. Bayes' theorem combines them into the posterior:

$$p(\theta\mid\text{data})=\frac{p(\text{data}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(\text{data})}\;\propto\;\text{likelihood}\times\text{prior}.$$

How it works

  1. Choose a prior: what values of \(\theta\) are plausible before you look.
  2. Write the likelihood, exactly as for MLE.
  3. Multiply and rescale so it integrates to 1: the posterior.
  4. Summarise it: its peak (MAP), its mean, and an interval holding 95% of it (a credible interval).

An everyday picture

A new restaurant has one review: 5 stars. Another has 400 reviews averaging 4.6. Which is better? Maximum likelihood says the first: its average is higher. Most people pick the second, because one review is weak evidence and most restaurants are not perfect. That instinct, "start from what is typical, move as far as the evidence justifies", is Bayesian estimation.

Good at
  • Small data: the prior stops wild estimates
  • Honest uncertainty, including for predictions
  • Updating one observation at a time
Watch out for
  • The prior is a choice, and with little data it matters
  • The posterior rarely has a formula: sampling or approximation is needed

1Prior × likelihood = posterior, on a coin

The unknown is the coin's heads probability \(\theta\). For a prior on a number between 0 and 1, the natural choice is a Beta distribution, \(p(\theta)\propto\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}\). Read \(a-1\) and \(b-1\) as heads and tails you imagine having seen already. With \(h\) heads and \(t\) tails, the likelihood is \(\theta^{h}(1-\theta)^{t}\), so

  1. \(p(\theta\mid h,t)\propto\theta^{h}(1-\theta)^{t}\cdot\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}=\theta^{a+h-1}(1-\theta)^{b+t-1}\)
    Multiplying adds the exponents: real counts plus imaginary counts.
  2. \(\theta\mid\text{data}\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a+h,\;b+t)\)
    The posterior is again a Beta. A prior with this property is called conjugate: updating is just adding counts.
  3. \(\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}}=\dfrac{h}{h+t},\quad\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}=\dfrac{a+h-1}{a+b+h+t-2},\quad\mathbb E[\theta\mid\text{data}]=\dfrac{a+h}{a+b+h+t}\)
    Three point estimates. The two Bayesian ones pull the MLE towards the prior's centre, more when data are scarce.

Start with 3 heads and 0 tails. MLE says \(\theta=1\). Each prior gives a more sensible answer. Then raise both counts to 60 and 40: all the priors agree. With enough data, the data win.

Going deeper: which single number? And credible versus confidence intervals

Point estimates are decisions. If a wrong guess costs \((\hat\theta-\theta)^2\), the best guess on average under the posterior is its mean. If it costs \(|\hat\theta-\theta|\), the median. If you are only rewarded for being exactly right (0–1 loss), the mode, which is MAP. For a symmetric posterior they coincide. For a skewed one (few flips, near 0 or 1) they differ, and the mean is usually the safest.

Credible interval. "Given the data and the prior, \(\theta\) lies in \([0.41,0.78]\) with probability 95%." That is the sentence people want a confidence interval to mean, and it is true for a credible interval. A confidence interval instead promises that the procedure captures the fixed true value in 95% of repeated experiments. With a flat prior and plenty of data the two intervals are almost the same numbers; with little data or a strong prior they differ.

2Estimating a mean: the posterior is a weighted compromise

Measurements with known noise \(\sigma=2\) of an unknown mean \(\mu\) (true value: dashed). Grey: prior. Orange: what the data alone say. Purple: the posterior. The bar shows how much weight each side gets.

Each measurement is \(x_i\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\). Put a Normal prior on the unknown mean, \(\mu\sim\mathcal N(m_0,s_0^2)\). Gaussians multiply into a Gaussian, and the cleanest way to say how is with precision, one over variance, which measures how much information something carries:

  1. \(\dfrac{1}{s_n^2}=\dfrac{1}{s_0^2}+\dfrac{n}{\sigma^2}\)
    Information adds up: the prior's precision plus \(1/\sigma^2\) from each measurement.
  2. \(m_n=s_n^2\Big(\dfrac{m_0}{s_0^2}+\dfrac{n\bar x}{\sigma^2}\Big)=w\,\bar x+(1-w)\,m_0,\quad w=\dfrac{n/\sigma^2}{n/\sigma^2+1/s_0^2}\)
    The posterior mean is a weighted average of the data mean and the prior mean, weighted by their precisions.
  3. \(n\to\infty:\;w\to1,\;m_n\to\bar x,\;s_n\to0\)
    With enough data, the prior stops mattering and the estimate becomes the MLE.

Pulling an estimate towards a fixed point is called shrinkage. It trades a little bias for much less variance, and on average lands closer to the truth, which is the same trade that regularisation makes.

Algorithm: Bayesian estimate of a mean (Normal prior, known noise)
  1. input prior mean \(m_0\) and spread \(s_0\), noise \(\sigma\)
  2. for each new measurement \(x\) do
  3. \(\tau\leftarrow1/s^2+1/\sigma^2\) precision goes up by one measurement's worth
  4. \(m\leftarrow\big(m/s^2+x/\sigma^2\big)/\tau\) move towards \(x\), by its share of the precision
  5. \(s\leftarrow1/\sqrt\tau\) today's posterior is tomorrow's prior
  6. return posterior \(\mathcal N(m,s^2)\); 95% credible interval \(m\pm1.96\,s\)

3Bayesian linear regression: MAP is ridge, and you get error bars

A polynomial fit to noisy points. Grey dashed: the maximum-likelihood fit. Purple: the posterior mean. Shaded: the 95% band for a new observation, which widens where there is no data.

The model is \(y=w^\top\phi(x)+\varepsilon\), with features \(\phi(x)=(1,x,x^2,\dots)\) and Gaussian noise of precision \(\beta\). Put a Gaussian prior on the weights, \(w\sim\mathcal N(0,\alpha^{-1}I)\): "weights are probably small". Take the log of the posterior:

  1. \(\log p(w\mid\mathcal D)=-\dfrac{\beta}{2}\sum_i\big(y^{(i)}-w^\top\phi(x^{(i)})\big)^2-\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\|w\|^2+\text{const}\)
    Log-likelihood (squared errors) plus log-prior (squared weights).
  2. \(\hat w_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\min_w\;\|y-\Phi w\|^2+\lambda\|w\|^2,\quad\lambda=\alpha/\beta\)
    Maximising the posterior is ridge regression. The penalty is the prior, and \(\lambda\) is noise variance divided by prior variance.
  3. \(w\mid\mathcal D\sim\mathcal N(m_N,S_N),\quad S_N=(\alpha I+\beta\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1},\quad m_N=\beta S_N\Phi^\top y\)
    The whole posterior is Gaussian, so we also know how unsure each weight is.
  4. \(y_*\mid x_*,\mathcal D\sim\mathcal N\big(m_N^\top\phi_*,\;\tfrac1\beta+\phi_*^\top S_N\phi_*\big)\)
    The posterior predictive: noise we can never remove, plus uncertainty about \(w\), which is large far from the data.
Algorithm: Bayesian linear regression
  1. input features \(\Phi\) (n × d), targets \(y\), prior precision \(\alpha\), noise precision \(\beta\)
  2. \(S_N\leftarrow(\alpha I+\beta\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\) posterior covariance of the weights
  3. \(m_N\leftarrow\beta S_N\Phi^\top y\) posterior mean = ridge solution with λ = α/β
  4. for each new input \(x_*\) do
  5. mean \(\leftarrow m_N^\top\phi(x_*)\); variance \(\leftarrow1/\beta+\phi(x_*)^\top S_N\phi(x_*)\)
  6. return predictions with error bars mean \(\pm1.96\sqrt{\text{variance}}\)

At degree 9 with 10 points, the MLE wiggles through every point. The posterior mean stays calm. Turn \(\alpha\) down to \(10^{-6}\) and it starts to wiggle too: a prior that is too weak gives no protection.

4No formula? Draw samples: Metropolis–Hastings

A coin with a two-part prior, "probably a fair coin, but possibly a trick one", has no closed-form posterior. A random walker proposes moves and accepts or rejects them. Top: its path. Bottom: where it spent its time, against the exact posterior (computed on a grid, for checking).

Conjugate priors are the lucky case. Change the prior to something more honest, such as a mixture, \(p(\theta)=0.7\cdot\operatorname{Beta}(40,40)+0.3\cdot\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)\), and the posterior \(\propto\theta^h(1-\theta)^t p(\theta)\) has a shape but no clean formula: in particular, we cannot compute the constant \(p(\text{data})\) that makes it integrate to 1 in a model with many parameters.

Markov chain Monte Carlo gets around this. It never needs the constant, only ratios of the posterior at two points, where the constant cancels. The walker proposes a nearby value. If it is more plausible, it moves. If it is less plausible, it moves only sometimes, with probability equal to the ratio. Run long enough, the time it spends in each region is proportional to the posterior there.

Algorithm: Metropolis–Hastings sampling
  1. input unnormalised posterior \(\tilde p(\theta)=p(\text{data}\mid\theta)p(\theta)\), step size \(s\), start \(\theta\)
  2. for \(t=1\) to \(T\) do
  3. \(\theta'\leftarrow\theta+s\cdot\mathcal N(0,1)\) propose a nearby value
  4. \(r\leftarrow\tilde p(\theta')/\tilde p(\theta)\) the unknown constant cancels
  5. if \(u\sim U(0,1)<r\) then \(\theta\leftarrow\theta'\) uphill: always; downhill: sometimes
  6. record \(\theta\) a rejected move records the old value again
  7. return the recorded values after a burn-in: a sample from the posterior
Going deeper: tuning, diagnostics and modern samplers

Step size. Try 0.005: almost every move is accepted, but the walker crawls and the histogram is lumpy for a long time. Try 0.6: most proposals land in implausible places and are rejected, so the walker sits still. A common target is accepting roughly a quarter to a half of the proposals.

Why it works. The acceptance rule makes the chain satisfy detailed balance: \(\tilde p(\theta)q(\theta'\mid\theta)\min(1,r)=\tilde p(\theta')q(\theta\mid\theta')\min(1,1/r)\). A chain that balances every pair of moves this way has the posterior as its long-run distribution.

In practice. Discard an initial burn-in, run several chains from different starts and check they agree (\(\hat R\approx1\)), and report the effective sample size, since successive values are correlated. Libraries such as PyMC and Stan use Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (NUTS), which uses gradients of \(\log\tilde p\) to make long, well-aimed moves, and scales to thousands of parameters. When even that is too slow, variational inference fits a simple distribution to the posterior by optimisation instead; that is what a VAE does.

6The same thing in Python

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# 1. conjugate coin: Beta(2, 2) prior, 7 heads and 3 tails
post = stats.beta(2 + 7, 2 + 3)
post.mean(), post.interval(0.95)          # posterior mean and 95% credible interval
(2 + 7 - 1) / (2 + 2 + 10 - 2)                  # MAP

# 3. Bayesian linear regression: learns alpha and beta from the data too
from sklearn.linear_model import BayesianRidge
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 10); y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + 0.2 * np.random.randn(10)
X = np.vander(x, 10, increasing=True)
m = BayesianRidge().fit(X, y)
mean, std = m.predict(X, return_std=True)   # predictions with error bars

# 4. a mixture prior with no formula: sample the posterior with PyMC (NUTS)
import pymc as pm
with pm.Model():
    theta = pm.Mixture("theta", w=[0.7, 0.3],
                       comp_dists=[pm.Beta.dist(40, 40), pm.Beta.dist(1, 1)])
    pm.Binomial("heads", n=20, p=theta, observed=15)
    trace = pm.sample(2000, chains=4)
pm.summary(trace)                            # mean, credible interval, r_hat, effective sample size
