Probability and Bayes' theorem

Machine learning is learning under uncertainty. Labels are noisy, data is a sample, and a classifier should say "80% sure" rather than just "yes". Probability is the language for this. Bayes' theorem is the one rule that turns evidence into updated beliefs.

What is probability, in plain words?

The idea

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how likely something is. One way to read it: if you could repeat the situation many times, it is the fraction of times the thing happens. Another: it is a degree of belief, which should change when evidence arrives. Both readings obey the same rules, and both are used in ML.

The central question of this page is the email-filter question. You see the word "winner" in an email. How likely is it to be spam? You know how often spam contains "winner", but that is the reverse question. Bayes' theorem turns one into the other.

How it works

  1. List what can happen (the sample space) and assign probabilities that add to 1.
  2. An event is a set of outcomes; its probability is the sum over those outcomes.
  3. Learning that \(B\) happened shrinks the world to \(B\): that is conditional probability \(P(A\mid B)\).
  4. Bayes' theorem flips the condition: \(P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A)P(A)/P(B)\).

An everyday picture

A weather forecast says 30% rain. Then you look out of the window and see dark clouds. Your belief goes up, not to 100%, but up. How far up depends on how often clouds come with rain, how often they come without it, and the 30% you started with. That everyday reasoning, made exact, is Bayes' theorem.

Good at
  • Saying how sure a prediction is
  • Combining prior knowledge with data
  • Giving loss functions a meaning (log-likelihood)
Watch out for
  • Confusing \(P(A\mid B)\) with \(P(B\mid A)\)
  • Ignoring base rates: rare events stay fairly rare after weak evidence

1Probability by counting: two dice

Roll two fair dice. The sample space \(\Omega\) is all 36 ordered pairs \((d_1,d_2)\), each with probability \(1/36\). An event is any set of outcomes, and when all outcomes are equally likely,

$$P(A)=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}=\frac{\text{outcomes in }A}{36}.$$

Now suppose someone tells you event \(B\) happened. Every outcome outside \(B\) is ruled out, so the world shrinks to \(B\). Among those outcomes, the ones where \(A\) also happens are \(A\cap B\). That gives the definition of conditional probability:

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} .$$

Rearranged, it is the product rule: \(P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)\). If learning \(B\) does not change the chance of \(A\), so \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\), the events are independent, and then \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\). Try "first die is even" with "second die is 6" (independent) and "sum is 7" with "sum is at least 10" (not independent).

Going deeper: the axioms and the rules that follow

Kolmogorov's three axioms: \(P(A)\ge0\); \(P(\Omega)=1\); and for disjoint events \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\) (also for countably many). Everything else is derived:

  • Complement: \(P(A^c)=1-P(A)\), since \(A\) and \(A^c\) are disjoint and fill \(\Omega\).
  • Inclusion–exclusion: \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\). The overlap was counted twice.
  • Law of total probability: if \(B_1,\dots,B_k\) split \(\Omega\) into disjoint pieces, \(P(A)=\sum_iP(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)\). This is the denominator of Bayes' theorem.
  • Chain rule: \(P(A_1\cap\dots\cap A_n)=P(A_1)P(A_2\mid A_1)\cdots P(A_n\mid A_1,\dots,A_{n-1})\). A language model is this rule applied to words.

For infinite sample spaces (the real line) we cannot assign a probability to every point, so we assign it to intervals through a density, and "events" are restricted to a well-behaved family of sets (a σ-algebra). For ML purposes, the rules above are all you need.

2Random variables and their distributions

Bars or curve: the distribution. Grey histogram: \(n\) random draws from it. The dashed line is the mean; the band is ±1 standard deviation.

A random variable is a number attached to each outcome, like "the sum of the two dice". Its distribution says how probability is spread over its values. Discrete variables have a probability mass function \(p(x)=P(X=x)\). Continuous ones have a density \(p(x)\), where probability is area: \(P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^bp(x)\,dx\).

Two numbers summarise a distribution. The expectation (mean) is the probability-weighted average, the balance point of the bars. The variance is the expected squared distance from the mean, a measure of spread:

$$\mathbb E[X]=\sum_xx\,p(x)\quad\text{or}\quad\int x\,p(x)\,dx,$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E\big[(X-\mathbb E X)^2\big]=\mathbb E[X^2]-(\mathbb EX)^2 .$$
Distribution

As you draw more samples, the sample mean settles on \(\mathbb E[X]\). That is the law of large numbers, and it is why averaging a loss over a big training set estimates the expected loss on new data.

3Two variables at once: the Gaussian, covariance and the covariance matrix

The Gaussian (normal) distribution

The bell curve \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is described by two numbers: the mean \(\mu\) (where the peak sits) and the standard deviation \(\sigma\) (how wide it is). About 68% of draws fall within \(\pm1\sigma\) of the mean, 95% within \(\pm2\sigma\) and 99.7% within \(\pm3\sigma\). It appears everywhere because sums of many small independent effects are approximately Gaussian, whatever the effects look like: that is the central limit theorem. Measurement noise, averages, and the errors in linear regression are all modelled this way.

The standard normal is the one with \(\mu=0,\ \sigma=1\), written \(\mathcal N(0,1)\). Any Gaussian value can be turned into a standard one by its z-score,

$$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},$$

which answers "how many standard deviations above the mean is this?". Applying it to every feature, using the training mean and standard deviation, is called standardising; it puts features measured in metres and in years on the same footing, which gradient descent, PCA, SVMs and regularisation all need. Press Standardised to see it: each axis is rescaled to spread 1, and only the correlation is left.

Covariance: do two features move together?

One number per feature is not enough once we have two. Height and weight are each spread out, but they are also related: tall people tend to be heavier. The covariance measures this:

  1. \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big]\)
    Multiply how far each variable is from its own mean, and average.
  2. \(>0\): both above or both below together
    Both deviations have the same sign, so the product is positive. The cloud tilts up.
  3. \(<0\): one up when the other is down
    The cloud tilts down. Near 0: no linear trend.
  4. \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,X)=\operatorname{Var}(X)\)
    A variable's covariance with itself is its variance.

Covariance has awkward units (cm × kg), so its size is hard to read. Dividing by both standard deviations gives the unit-free correlation, \(\rho=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)\), always between \(-1\) and \(1\). \(\pm1\) means the points lie exactly on a line. Correlation is the covariance of the z-scores, which is why the Standardised view keeps the tilt but loses the stretch.

Two variables are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other: \(p(x,y)=p(x)\,p(y)\). Independent variables have zero covariance, but zero covariance does not imply independence: if \(Y=X^2\) with \(X\) symmetric around 0, \(Y\) is completely determined by \(X\) yet their covariance is 0. Covariance only sees straight-line relationships.

The covariance matrix \(\Sigma\)

With \(d\) features there is a covariance for every pair. Arrange them in a \(d\times d\) table: entry \((j,k)\) is \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_j,x_k)\). That is the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), and it is the multi-feature version of the variance:

$$\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1^2&\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\\ \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2&\sigma_2^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \hat\Sigma=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(x^{(i)}-\bar x)(x^{(i)}-\bar x)^\top .$$

Read it like this. The diagonal holds each feature's own variance. The off-diagonal entries hold how pairs move together. It is symmetric, because \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_j,x_k)=\operatorname{Cov}(x_k,x_j)\). And it is positive semi-definite: for any weights \(w\), the combined feature \(w^\top x\) has variance \(w^\top\Sigma w\), and a variance cannot be negative.

Geometrically, \(\Sigma\) is the shape of the cloud. Its eigenvectors point along the axes of the ellipse, and each eigenvalue is the variance of the data along its eigenvector. The longest axis is the direction in which the data varies most: that is the first principal component, and finding these axes is exactly what PCA does. Set \(\rho=0\) and the arrows line up with the feature axes: \(\Sigma\) is diagonal.

The multivariate Gaussian

The bell curve in \(d\) dimensions is \(\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)\), with density

$$p(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\det(\Sigma)^{1/2}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\Big).$$

The exponent is the squared Mahalanobis distance: distance from the mean measured in standard deviations, along the ellipse's own axes. Points on one ellipse are equally likely. The \(\det\Sigma\) in front normalises for the volume of the ellipse. LDA and GDA fit one of these per class; a diagonal \(\Sigma\) means the features are treated as independent, which is the naive Bayes assumption; and drawing samples, as this plot does, uses the Cholesky factor: \(x=\mu+Lz\) with \(z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\).

Going deeper: why divide by \(n-1\), and what correlation cannot tell you

The sample mean \(\bar x\) is fitted to the same data, so the points are on average a little closer to \(\bar x\) than to the true \(\mu\). Dividing by \(n\) underestimates the variance by a factor \((n-1)/n\); dividing by \(n-1\) (Bessel's correction) removes the bias. One "degree of freedom" was spent estimating the mean.

Correlation measures straight-line association only, and it is easily moved by a few outliers. Four very different data sets (Anscombe's quartet) share the same means, variances and correlation. And correlation is not causation: ice-cream sales and drownings are correlated because both rise in summer.

With more features than examples (\(d>n\)), \(\hat\Sigma\) has rank at most \(n-1\), so it is singular and \(\Sigma^{-1}\) does not exist. Shrinking it towards a diagonal matrix, \((1-\alpha)\hat\Sigma+\alpha\,\mathrm{diag}(\hat\Sigma)\), is the usual fix.

Algorithm: sample covariance and correlation matrices
  1. input data matrix \(X\) (\(n\) rows = examples, \(d\) columns = features)
  2. \(\bar x_j\leftarrow\frac1n\sum_i X_{ij}\) for each column \(j\) feature means
  3. \(\tilde X\leftarrow X-\mathbf 1\bar x^\top\) centre: subtract each column's mean
  4. \(\hat\Sigma\leftarrow\frac{1}{n-1}\tilde X^\top\tilde X\) entry (j,k) averages products of deviations
  5. \(R_{jk}\leftarrow\hat\Sigma_{jk}/\sqrt{\hat\Sigma_{jj}\hat\Sigma_{kk}}\) correlation matrix: ones on the diagonal
  6. return \(\hat\Sigma\), \(R\)

4Bayes' theorem: a medical test

1,000 people. Filled squares have the disease; outlined squares tested positive. Of everyone who tested positive, how many are actually ill?

ill, test +ill, test −healthy, test + (false alarm)healthy, test −

A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 90% of real cases (its sensitivity) and correctly clears 95% of healthy people (its specificity). You test positive. Most people guess you are about 90% likely to be ill. Let us compute it.

  1. \(P(D\cap +)=P(+\mid D)P(D)=P(D\mid +)P(+)\)
    The product rule, written in both orders.
  2. \(P(D\mid +)=\dfrac{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+)}\)
    Divide by \(P(+)\). This is Bayes' theorem: posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence.
  3. \(P(+)=P(+\mid D)P(D)+P(+\mid \bar D)P(\bar D)\)
    Law of total probability: a positive comes from an ill person or from a false alarm.

With the starting numbers (1%, 90%, 95%), a positive result means only about a 15% chance of disease. The squares show why: there are so many healthy people that even a 5% false-alarm rate produces more false positives than the disease produces true ones. The prior (the base rate) matters as much as the test. A second, independent positive test would use 15% as its new prior.

5Learning from data: how biased is this coin?

The curve is your belief about the coin's chance of heads, \(\theta\). Flip and watch it sharpen. The dashed line is the true \(\theta\), which the learner does not know.

Now the unknown is a number, the coin's heads probability \(\theta\). Bayes' theorem works the same way, with densities:

$$\underbrace{p(\theta\mid\text{data})}_{\text{posterior}}\;\propto\;\underbrace{p(\text{data}\mid\theta)}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}} .$$

With \(h\) heads and \(t\) tails, the likelihood is \(\theta^h(1-\theta)^t\). Take as the prior a Beta distribution, \(p(\theta)\propto\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}\). You can think of it as \(a-1\) imaginary heads and \(b-1\) imaginary tails seen beforehand. Multiply:

$$p(\theta\mid\text{data})\propto\theta^{a+h-1}(1-\theta)^{b+t-1}=\text{Beta}(a+h,\;b+t).$$

Learning is just adding counts. Three estimates of \(\theta\) fall out. The maximum-likelihood estimate \(h/(h+t)\) ignores the prior. The MAP estimate is the posterior's peak, \((a+h-1)/(a+b+h+t-2)\). The posterior mean is \((a+h)/(a+b+h+t)\). With \(a=b=2\), MAP is "add one fake head and one fake tail", which is Laplace smoothing in naive Bayes. With little data the prior keeps you from saying "\(\theta=1\)" after three heads. With lots of data all three agree.

More on priors, MAP, credible intervals and sampling the posterior when there is no formula: Bayesian estimation.

Algorithm: Bayesian updating of a coin
  1. input prior counts \(a, b\) Beta(1,1) is "no idea": flat
  2. for each new flip \(x\in\{\text{H},\text{T}\}\) do
  3. if \(x=\text{H}\) then \(a\leftarrow a+1\) else \(b\leftarrow b+1\) today's posterior is tomorrow's prior
  4. mean \(\leftarrow a/(a+b)\); MAP \(\leftarrow (a-1)/(a+b-2)\)
  5. uncertainty \(\leftarrow\sqrt{ab/((a+b)^2(a+b+1))}\) shrinks like 1/√n
  6. return Beta\((a,b)\)
Going deeper: conjugate priors, and MAP as regularisation

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior has the same form as the prior, as Beta does for coin flips. Other pairs: Dirichlet for categorical counts (word frequencies), Gaussian for a Gaussian mean, Gamma for a Poisson rate. With a conjugate prior, Bayesian learning is closed-form bookkeeping. Without one, we approximate the posterior by sampling (MCMC) or by fitting a simpler distribution (variational inference, as in VAEs).

Take logs of Bayes' theorem for a model with weights \(w\): \(\log p(w\mid D)=\log p(D\mid w)+\log p(w)+\text{const}\). Maximising it means "fit the data, but pay a price for unlikely weights". A Gaussian prior \(p(w)\propto e^{-\|w\|^2/2\tau^2}\) adds \(-\|w\|^2/2\tau^2\), which is exactly the L2 (ridge) penalty. A Laplace prior gives L1 (lasso). See regularisation.

6Where probability shows up in ML

Classifiers output probabilities

Logistic regression models \(P(y=1\mid x)\) with a sigmoid; neural networks use softmax over classes. The threshold you choose is a decision on top of a probability.

Loss = negative log-likelihood

Cross-entropy loss is \(-\log P(\text{true label}\mid x)\). Squared error is the negative log-likelihood under Gaussian noise. Minimising a loss is maximising a likelihood (see maximum likelihood).

Generative classifiers

GDA and naive Bayes model \(p(x\mid y)\) and \(P(y)\), then classify with Bayes' theorem. Naive Bayes assumes features are independent given the class.

Language models

A language model is the chain rule: \(P(w_1\dots w_n)=\prod_tP(w_t\mid w_{<t})\). Transformers predict each factor; temperature reshapes it.

RAG marginalises

RAG treats the retrieved passage \(z\) as a hidden variable and sums it out: \(p(y\mid x)=\sum_zp(z\mid x)\,p(y\mid x,z)\), the law of total probability.

Uncertainty and calibration

A well-calibrated model that says 70% is right about 70% of the time. Ensembles, dropout at test time and Bayesian methods all try to get the uncertainty right, not just the answer.

7The same thing in Python (NumPy / SciPy)

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d = rng.integers(1, 7, size=(100_000, 2)); s = d.sum(1)
(s == 7).mean()                              # ≈ 6/36: probability by simulation
((s == 7) & (d[:, 0] % 2 == 0)).mean() / (d[:, 0] % 2 == 0).mean()   # P(A | B)
X = stats.binom(n=10, p=0.3); X.mean(), X.var(), X.pmf(3)
prior, sens, spec = 0.01, 0.90, 0.95
p_pos = sens * prior + (1 - spec) * (1 - prior)
sens * prior / p_pos                          # P(ill | +) ≈ 0.154
a, b = 2, 2; flips = rng.random(50) < 0.7
post = stats.beta(a + flips.sum(), b + (~flips).sum())
post.mean(), post.interval(0.95)              # estimate and 95% credible interval
