Projections and least squares

Very often we want a point we cannot reach, and must settle for the closest point we can. In linear algebra that closest point is a projection, and finding it is one short derivation. The same derivation, written with data, is linear regression.

What is a projection, in plain words?

The idea

On the previous page, \(Ax=b\) had no solution when \(b\) lay off the line that \(A\) squashed everything onto. Real data is like that all the time. With 47 houses and a model with 2 parameters, we have 47 equations and 2 unknowns. No straight line passes through every point, so \(X\theta=y\) has no exact solution.

The sensible fallback is: find the reachable point \(p\) that is closest to \(b\). That point is the projection of \(b\), and the key fact about it is simple: the error \(b-p\) is perpendicular to everything we could reach.

How it works

  1. Decide what is reachable: a line through \(a\), or the span of the columns of \(A\).
  2. Write the closest point as \(p=A\hat x\) for some unknown \(\hat x\).
  3. Demand that the error \(e=b-A\hat x\) is orthogonal to every column: \(A^\top e=0\).
  4. Solve \(A^\top A\hat x=A^\top b\). This is the normal equation of least squares.

An everyday picture

The shadow of a pole at noon. The sun is straight overhead, so light falls perpendicular to the ground and the shadow is the closest point on the ground to every point of the pole. A low evening sun casts a longer, slanted shadow. That shadow is still a projection, but not the closest one.

1Projecting onto a line

Drag \(a\) (purple, sets the line) and \(b\) (orange). The green point is the projection \(p\); the dashed segment is the error \(e=b-p\).

The points on the line through \(a\) are the multiples \(t\,a\). We want the \(t\) that brings \(t\,a\) closest to \(b\). Here are two ways to find it, and they agree.

  1. \(f(t)=\|b-t\,a\|^2=b\cdot b-2t\,a\cdot b+t^2\,a\cdot a\)
    The squared distance from \(b\) to a point on the line: a parabola in \(t\).
  2. \(f'(t)=-2\,a\cdot b+2t\,a\cdot a=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat t=\dfrac{a\cdot b}{a\cdot a}\)
    Calculus: set the derivative to zero at the bottom of the parabola.
  3. \(a\cdot(b-\hat t\,a)=a\cdot b-\hat t\,a\cdot a=0\)
    Geometry: the same \(\hat t\) makes the error perpendicular to \(a\). The closest point is where the error is orthogonal.
Going deeper: why "orthogonal" really means "closest"

Let \(p\) be the point on the line with \(b-p\perp a\), and let \(q\) be any other point on the line. Then \(p-q\) is a multiple of \(a\), so it is perpendicular to \(b-p\), and Pythagoras gives

$$\|b-q\|^2=\|b-p\|^2+\|p-q\|^2\ \ge\ \|b-p\|^2 ,$$

with equality only when \(q=p\). So the orthogonal foot is the unique closest point. The same argument works for any subspace, in any dimension, and for any inner product. That is why projection theorems hold even for spaces of functions (for example, Fourier series are projections).

$$p=\frac{a^\top b}{a^\top a}\,a=\underbrace{\frac{a\,a^\top}{a^\top a}}_{P}\,b .$$

The matrix \(P\) does the projecting. Projecting twice changes nothing (the shadow of a shadow is itself), so \(P^2=P\). It is also symmetric, \(P^\top=P\). Those two properties characterise orthogonal projections.

2Projecting onto a plane

Drag to rotate. The plane is everything \(A\hat x\) can reach. Use the sliders to move \(b\).

Now let the reachable set be a plane through the origin, spanned by two columns \(a_1, a_2\) of a \(3\times2\) matrix \(A\). A point on the plane is \(A\hat x=\hat x_1a_1+\hat x_2a_2\). The same principle applies: the error must be perpendicular to the plane, which means perpendicular to both columns.

  1. \(a_1^\top(b-A\hat x)=0,\quad a_2^\top(b-A\hat x)=0\)
    The error is orthogonal to each direction in the plane.
  2. \(A^\top(b-A\hat x)=0\)
    Stack the two equations: the rows of \(A^\top\) are the columns of \(A\).
  3. \(A^\top A\,\hat x=A^\top b\;\Rightarrow\;\hat x=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top b\)
    These are the normal equations. \(A^\top A\) is invertible exactly when the columns are independent.
$$p=A\hat x=\underbrace{A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top}_{P}\,b .$$

When \(A\) is a single column \(a\), this collapses back to the formula from section 1, because \(a^\top a\) is just a number.

Going deeper: the anatomy of a projection matrix

Symmetric: \(P^\top=(A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top)^\top=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top=P\), using that \(A^\top A\) is symmetric. Idempotent: \(P^2=A(A^\top A)^{-1}(A^\top A)(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top=P\).

Conversely, any symmetric \(P\) with \(P^2=P\) is an orthogonal projection onto its column space. Its eigenvalues can only be 0 or 1: if \(Pv=\lambda v\) then \(\lambda v=P^2v=\lambda^2v\). Directions inside the subspace have eigenvalue 1 and are kept; directions perpendicular to it have eigenvalue 0 and are removed. \(I-P\) is also a projection, onto the orthogonal complement, and every vector splits uniquely as \(b=Pb+(I-P)b\).

Since the trace is the sum of eigenvalues, \(\operatorname{tr}P=\operatorname{rank}A\). In regression this is the number of parameters, also called the model's degrees of freedom, and it is why the noise variance is estimated with \(n-p\) in the denominator.

3Least squares is a projection

Drag the points. The solid line is the least-squares fit; the dashed line is your own guess, set by the sliders. Vertical segments are residuals.

Here is the payoff. Fit \(h_\theta(x)=\theta_0+\theta_1x\) to \(n\) points. Stack the inputs into a design matrix with a column of ones (for \(\theta_0\)) and a column of \(x\)'s:

$$X=\begin{bmatrix}1&x^{(1)}\\ \vdots&\vdots\\ 1&x^{(n)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{bmatrix}y^{(1)}\\ \vdots\\ y^{(n)}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^n .$$

Every possible line gives a vector of predictions \(X\theta\), and all of those vectors form a 2-D plane (the column space) inside \(\mathbb R^n\). The target \(y\) is almost never on that plane. Minimising the squared error \(\|y-X\theta\|^2\) means finding the point of the plane nearest to \(y\). That is a projection, so section 2 gives the answer immediately:

$$X^\top X\,\hat\theta=X^\top y,\qquad \hat y=X\hat\theta=Py .$$

The condition \(X^\top e=0\) has a concrete meaning. Its first row says \(\sum_i e^{(i)}=0\): the residuals balance out. Its second says \(\sum_i x^{(i)}e^{(i)}=0\): the residuals are uncorrelated with \(x\). Move the sliders away from the fit and both sums stop being zero.

Going deeper: the calculus route, and why squared error

Andrew Ng's notes derive the same equations with matrix calculus. Write \(J(\theta)=\tfrac12\|X\theta-y\|^2=\tfrac12(X\theta-y)^\top(X\theta-y)\). Expanding, \(J=\tfrac12\theta^\top X^\top X\theta-\theta^\top X^\top y+\tfrac12y^\top y\), and the gradient is

$$\nabla_\theta J=X^\top X\theta-X^\top y .$$

Setting it to zero gives the normal equations. \(J\) is a convex bowl because its Hessian \(X^\top X\) is positive semi-definite (\(v^\top X^\top Xv=\|Xv\|^2\ge0\)), so this stationary point is the global minimum. Geometry and calculus give the same answer.

Why squared error rather than absolute error? If \(y^{(i)}=\theta^\top x^{(i)}+\varepsilon^{(i)}\) with independent Gaussian noise \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\), the log-likelihood is \(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y^{(i)}-\theta^\top x^{(i)})^2\) plus a constant. Maximising likelihood is then exactly least squares (see maximum likelihood). The Gauss–Markov theorem adds that, even without assuming Gaussian noise, least squares has the smallest variance among unbiased linear estimators, as long as the noise has zero mean, constant variance and no correlation.

Algorithm: least squares, two ways
  1. input design matrix \(X\) (\(n\times p\), first column all ones), targets \(y\)
  2. Route A: normal equations (simple, less stable)
  3. \(G \leftarrow X^\top X\); \(g \leftarrow X^\top y\)
  4. solve \(G\theta = g\) (Cholesky, since \(G\) is symmetric positive definite)
  5. Route B: QR (what libraries do)
  6. \(Q, R \leftarrow \text{QR}(X)\); solve \(R\theta = Q^\top y\) by back substitution
  7. \(\hat y \leftarrow X\theta\); \(e \leftarrow y - \hat y\) check: Xᵀe ≈ 0
  8. return \(\theta\), \(\hat y\), \(e\)

See the linear regression page for the same fit with gradient descent, standard errors and R².

4Orthonormal bases make projection easy

The formula \(A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\) has an inverse in the middle, which is the expensive and fragile part. It disappears if the columns are orthonormal: unit length and mutually perpendicular. Call such a matrix \(Q\). Then \(Q^\top Q=I\), and

$$P=QQ^\top,\qquad p=\sum_k (q_k\cdot b)\,q_k .$$

In words: the coordinate of \(b\) along each \(q_k\) is just a dot product, and the projection is the sum of those pieces. No system of equations to solve. This is why so many algorithms first build an orthonormal basis. Gram–Schmidt does it by taking each new column and subtracting its projections onto the previous ones; written as matrices, that is the QR factorisation on the decompositions page.

Worked example

Let \(q_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)\), \(q_2=(0,0,1)\) and \(b=(3,1,2)\).

\(q_1\cdot b=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(3+1)=2\sqrt2\), \(q_2\cdot b=2\).

\(p=2\sqrt2\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)+2\,(0,0,1)=(2,2,2)\).

Check: \(e=b-p=(1,-1,0)\), and \(e\cdot q_1=0\), \(e\cdot q_2=0\). The error is perpendicular to the plane, as promised.

5Where projections show up in ML

Linear regression

The fitted values are \(\hat y=Py\) and the residuals are \((I-P)y\). The "hat matrix" \(P\) is the projection; its diagonal entries (leverages) tell you how much each point pulls on its own fit.

PCA

PCA projects data onto the \(k\) orthonormal directions that keep the most variance. Because those directions are orthonormal, the projection is \(QQ^\top x\), and the reconstruction error is what is left over.

Fisher's LDA

LDA projects onto one direction \(w\) chosen so that the two classes' shadows are far apart and narrow.

Removing a direction

To remove an unwanted direction \(u\) from word vectors (say a bias direction), replace \(x\) by \(x-(u\cdot x)u\): subtract the projection.

Ridge regression

Ridge replaces \(X^\top X\) by \(X^\top X+\lambda I\). It is no longer a pure projection: it shrinks the fit towards zero, trading a little bias for a lot less variance. See regularisation.

Attention outputs

An attention head's output is a weighted sum of value vectors, so it always lies in their span. Each head can only write to its own small subspace of the residual stream.

6The same thing in Python (NumPy)

import numpy as np
a, b = np.array([2., 1.]), np.array([1., 3.])
p = (a @ b) / (a @ a) * a               # projection onto the line through a
P = np.outer(a, a) / (a @ a); np.allclose(P @ P, P)   # idempotent
x = np.array([1., 2., 3., 4.]); y = np.array([1.2, 1.9, 3.2, 3.8])
X = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(x), x])
theta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)          # normal equations
theta, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)   # same answer, numerically safer (QR/SVD)
e = y - X @ theta; X.T @ e                         # ≈ [0, 0]: residual ⟂ columns
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(X); np.allclose(Q @ Q.T @ y, X @ theta)   # projection with an orthonormal basis
