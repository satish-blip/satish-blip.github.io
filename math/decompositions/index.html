Eigenvectors and decompositions

A decomposition rewrites one awkward matrix as a product of simple ones: diagonal, triangular or orthogonal. Each simple piece is easy to understand and cheap to compute with, so a hard problem becomes a few easy ones. This page builds the four you will meet most: eigen, LU, QR and Cholesky, and shows what the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix tell you: positive semi-definiteness, the spectral theorem and covariance ellipses.

What is a decomposition, in plain words?

The idea

We factor numbers to understand them: \(84=2^2\cdot3\cdot7\) tells you at a glance what divides 84. Matrices can be factored too, and the factors play the same role. A diagonal matrix just stretches each axis. A triangular matrix can be solved one row at a time. An orthogonal matrix is a pure rotation or reflection that keeps lengths. (New to these? The field guide to special matrices explains each one with a live picture.) Write \(A\) as a product of such pieces and every question about \(A\) becomes a question about the pieces.

Which factorisation to use depends on the question. "What does \(A\) do when applied many times?" calls for eigenvectors. "Solve \(Ax=b\) for many \(b\)" calls for LU. "Least squares, stably" calls for QR. "Sample from a Gaussian" calls for Cholesky.

How it works

  1. Find the special directions or the elimination steps hidden in \(A\).
  2. Record them as factors: \(A=V\Lambda V^{-1}\), \(A=LU\), \(A=QR\) or \(A=LL^\top\).
  3. Solve, invert, take powers or sample using the easy factors.
  4. Pay the factoring cost once and reuse it many times.

An everyday picture

Kneading dough. Each push stretches the dough more in some directions than others. Most lines you draw on it get both turned and stretched, but a few special lines only get longer or shorter, staying on the same line. Those are the eigen-directions, and the stretch along each is its eigenvalue.

Good at
  • Turning \(O(n^3)\) work into reusable \(O(n^2)\) solves
  • Revealing structure: stretch directions, rank, stability
  • Numerical accuracy when chosen well
Watch out for
  • Not every matrix has real eigenvectors (rotations)
  • Some factorisations need special matrices (Cholesky needs SPD)

1Eigenvectors: directions that only stretch

Drag \(x\) around the unit circle. The orange arrow is \(Ax\). The dashed lines are the eigen-directions: put \(x\) on one and \(Ax\) lines up with it.

Apply a matrix to vectors pointing in every direction (the unit circle) and the circle becomes an ellipse. Almost every vector is both stretched and turned. We are looking for the exceptions: nonzero vectors \(v\) that come out pointing along the same line,

$$Av=\lambda v .$$

\(v\) is an eigenvector and the number \(\lambda\) is its eigenvalue: the stretch factor (negative means it flips). How do we find them?

  1. \(Av-\lambda v=0\;\Rightarrow\;(A-\lambda I)v=0\)
    Move everything to one side; \(\lambda v=\lambda Iv\).
  2. \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\)
    A nonzero \(v\) is sent to 0 only if \(A-\lambda I\) flattens space (see determinants).
  3. \(\lambda^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda+\det A=0\)
    For a \(2\times2\) matrix, expand \((a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc\). The trace is \(a+d\).

Solve the quadratic for \(\lambda\), then solve \((A-\lambda I)v=0\) for the direction. A rotation has no real solutions, because it turns every direction; its eigenvalues are complex.

Going deeper: complex eigenvalues, multiplicity, and why symmetric matrices are special

Over the complex numbers every \(n\times n\) matrix has exactly \(n\) eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, because \(\det(A-\lambda I)\) is a degree-\(n\) polynomial. A rotation by \(\phi\) has \(\lambda=e^{\pm i\phi}\): the modulus 1 says "no stretching" and the angle says "turn by \(\phi\)".

A repeated eigenvalue may not come with enough eigenvectors. The shear \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) has \(\lambda=1\) twice but only one eigen-direction, so it cannot be diagonalised. Such matrices are called defective; the Jordan form handles them.

Spectral theorem. A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, so \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\). Proof sketch for two distinct eigenvalues: \(\lambda_1v_1^\top v_2=(Av_1)^\top v_2=v_1^\top Av_2=\lambda_2v_1^\top v_2\), so \((\lambda_1-\lambda_2)v_1^\top v_2=0\) and \(v_1\perp v_2\). Covariance matrices, Hessians and kernel matrices are all symmetric. That is why PCA always finds perpendicular axes.

The Rayleigh quotient \(R(x)=\frac{x^\top Ax}{x^\top x}\) of a symmetric matrix is largest at the top eigenvector, where it equals \(\lambda_{\max}\). This is exactly the "maximise variance" statement of PCA.

2Eigendecomposition and powers of a matrix

Apply \(A\) again and again to the starting vector (drag it). After normalising each step, the arrow swings onto the eigenvector with the largest \(|\lambda|\).

Suppose a \(2\times2\) matrix has two independent eigenvectors \(v_1,v_2\). Use them as a new set of axes. In those axes, \(A\) does nothing but stretch axis 1 by \(\lambda_1\) and axis 2 by \(\lambda_2\). Put the eigenvectors in the columns of \(V\) and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of \(\Lambda\):

$$A=V\Lambda V^{-1}$$

Read it right to left: change to eigen-axes (\(V^{-1}\)), stretch each axis (\(\Lambda\)), change back (\(V\)).

The payoff is powers: \(A^k=V\Lambda V^{-1}V\Lambda V^{-1}\cdots=V\Lambda^kV^{-1}\), and \(\Lambda^k\) just raises each \(\lambda_i\) to the \(k\)-th power. Write the start vector as \(x_0=c_1v_1+c_2v_2\). Then

$$A^kx_0=c_1\lambda_1^k\,v_1+c_2\lambda_2^k\,v_2 .$$

If \(|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\), the first term wins exponentially fast, at a rate \(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k\). That is power iteration, the idea behind PageRank and behind exploding and vanishing gradients in RNNs, where the same weight matrix is applied at every time step.

Algorithm: power iteration
  1. input square matrix \(A\), start vector \(x_0 \ne 0\), tolerance \(\varepsilon\)
  2. \(x \leftarrow x_0 / \|x_0\|\)
  3. repeat
  4. \(y \leftarrow Ax\) one matrix–vector product: cheap even for huge sparse A
  5. \(\lambda \leftarrow x^\top y\) Rayleigh quotient: current eigenvalue estimate
  6. \(x \leftarrow y / \|y\|\) normalise so the numbers stay finite
  7. until \(x\) changes by less than \(\varepsilon\)
  8. return \(\lambda, x\) dominant eigenpair; error shrinks like |λ₂/λ₁|ᵏ

Uses the matrix from section 1. Try a matrix whose eigenvalues are close in size and watch convergence slow down.

3Positive semi-definite: bowl, valley or saddle?

The colour is the value of \(x^\top Ax\) at every point: blue where it is positive, pink where it is negative, and the thin lines are its contour lines. Drag \(x\) and compare it with \(Ax\) (orange). Dashed lines are the eigen-axes.

Start with plain numbers. A number \(a\) is non-negative exactly when \(a\,x^2\ge0\) for every \(x\): multiplying by a square never flips the sign. For a matrix the same test uses a vector \(x\) and the quadratic form

$$q(x)=x^\top Ax=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}\,x_ix_j .$$

A symmetric matrix \(A\) is positive semi-definite (PSD, written \(A\succeq0\)) if \(x^\top Ax\ge0\) for every \(x\), and positive definite (\(A\succ0\)) if \(x^\top Ax>0\) for every \(x\ne0\).

A small example: \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\) gives \(q(x)=2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2x_2^2=(x_1+x_2)^2+x_1^2+x_2^2\), a sum of squares, so it is never negative. But we cannot always spot such a trick, and we cannot try every \(x\). Is there a test that looks only at \(A\)? The eigenvectors give one.

  1. \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\)
    A symmetric matrix has perpendicular unit eigenvectors \(q_i\) (the spectral theorem, next section). They are the columns of \(Q\), and \(Q^\top Q=I\).
  2. \(x=Qc,\quad c=Q^\top x\)
    Describe \(x\) in the eigen-axes: \(c_i=q_i^\top x\) is how far \(x\) goes along axis \(i\).
  3. \(x^\top Ax=c^\top Q^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top Qc=c^\top\Lambda c\)
    Substitute and cancel \(Q^\top Q=I\) twice.
  4. \(x^\top Ax=\lambda_1c_1^2+\lambda_2c_2^2+\dots+\lambda_nc_n^2\)
    \(\Lambda\) is diagonal, so the cross terms vanish. Every \(c_i^2\ge0\), so the sign is decided by the \(\lambda_i\).
$$A\succeq0\iff\text{every }\lambda_i\ge0,\qquad A\succ0\iff\text{every }\lambda_i>0 .$$

So the surface \(z=x^\top Ax\) has only four shapes, set by the signs of the eigenvalues. All positive: a bowl with one lowest point (positive definite). Some zero, the rest positive: a valley with a flat floor along the zero-eigenvalue direction (semi-definite). Mixed signs: a saddle (indefinite). All negative: a dome. Along each eigen-axis the surface is the parabola \(\lambda_ic_i^2\), so \(\lambda_i\) is literally the curvature in that direction.

There is also an arrow picture. Since \(x^\top Ax=x\cdot(Ax)=\|x\|\,\|Ax\|\cos\angle(x,Ax)\), positive definite means \(Ax\) is always less than \(90^\circ\) away from \(x\). Drag \(x\) into a pink zone of the saddle and the orange arrow swings past the right angle.

Where you meet PSD matrices

  • Covariance matrices. \(u^\top\Sigma u=\operatorname{Var}(u^\top X)\ge0\): a variance cannot be negative (section 5).
  • Gram matrices \(X^\top X\): \(v^\top X^\top Xv=\|Xv\|^2\ge0\). Adding \(\lambda I\) (ridge regression) lifts every eigenvalue by \(\lambda\), so \(X^\top X+\lambda I\) is positive definite and always invertible.
  • Hessians. A function is convex exactly when its Hessian is PSD everywhere, and a stationary point is a minimum when the Hessian there is positive definite.
  • Kernel matrices. A valid kernel must give a PSD matrix \(K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)\) for every data set (Mercer's condition).
Going deeper: four equivalent tests, two traps, and some closure rules

For a symmetric \(A\) these are equivalent ways to say \(A\succ0\): (1) \(x^\top Ax>0\) for all \(x\ne0\); (2) every eigenvalue is positive; (3) every leading principal minor is positive, i.e. \(a_{11}>0\), \(\det A_{2\times2}>0\), …, \(\det A>0\) (Sylvester's criterion); (4) \(A=LL^\top\) with \(L\) lower triangular and a positive diagonal, i.e. the Cholesky factorisation succeeds. Test (4) is what numerical libraries actually use: it is about three times faster than computing eigenvalues.

For semi-definite, use \(A=B^\top B\) for some \(B\), or "every eigenvalue \(\ge0\)". Trap 1: Sylvester's leading-minor test does not carry over to \(\ge\). \(\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\) has leading minors \(0\) and \(0\), yet \(x=(0,1)\) gives \(-1\). For PSD you must check all principal minors, including \(a_{22}\). For a \(2\times2\) matrix: \(a\ge0\), \(d\ge0\) and \(ad-b^2\ge0\).

Trap 2: for a non-symmetric \(A\) the eigenvalues say nothing about the sign of \(x^\top Ax\), because \(x^\top Ax=x^\top\tfrac12(A+A^\top)x\): the form only sees the symmetric part. \(\begin{bmatrix}1&4\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(1,1\) but \(x=(1,-1)\) gives \(1-4+1=-2\). That is why the definition insists on symmetric matrices.

Closure rules. If \(A,B\succeq0\) and \(t\ge0\): \(A+B\succeq0\), \(tA\succeq0\), \(C^\top AC\succeq0\) for any \(C\), and every diagonal entry \(a_{ii}=e_i^\top Ae_i\ge0\). The PSD matrices form a convex cone, the feasible set of semidefinite programming.

Minimum on the unit circle. With \(\|x\|=1\) we have \(\sum c_i^2=1\), so \(x^\top Ax\) is a weighted average of the eigenvalues: it lies between \(\lambda_{\min}\) and \(\lambda_{\max}\), reaching each at its eigenvector. "PSD" is the statement \(\lambda_{\min}\ge0\).

Algorithm: testing whether a symmetric matrix is positive semi-definite
  1. input square matrix \(A\), tolerance \(\varepsilon\) for rounding error
  2. \(A \leftarrow (A + A^\top)/2\) the quadratic form only sees the symmetric part
  3. \(\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n,\ q_1,\dots,q_n \leftarrow\) eigen-decomposition of \(A\)
  4. \(\lambda_{\min} \leftarrow \lambda_n\) the lowest curvature of the surface xᵀAx
  5. if \(\lambda_{\min} > \varepsilon\) return positive definite a bowl
  6. if \(\lambda_{\min} \ge -\varepsilon\) return positive semi-definite a valley with a flat floor
  7. return not PSD, with witness \(x=q_n\) xᵀAx = λmin < 0: a downhill direction

4The spectral theorem: rotate, stretch, rotate back

Press Play to apply \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) one factor at a time to the unit circle and a grid. The dashed orange outline is \(A\) applied in one go: the three steps end exactly on it.

A general matrix both turns and stretches, and section 2 needed a possibly skewed set of eigenvectors \(V\) to undo that. Symmetric matrices are much nicer:

$$A=A^\top\ \Longrightarrow\ A=Q\Lambda Q^\top,\qquad Q^\top Q=I,\ \ \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\ \text{real}.$$

This is the spectral theorem: the eigenvalues are real and the eigenvectors can be chosen perpendicular and of length 1. So \(V\) becomes an orthogonal matrix \(Q\), and \(V^{-1}\) becomes simply \(Q^\top\). Read \(Ax=Q\Lambda Q^\top x\) from right to left:

  1. \(c=Q^\top x\)
    Rotate (or reflect) so the eigen-axes line up with the coordinate axes. The purple and teal arrows \(q_1,q_2\) land on \(e_1,e_2\).
  2. \(\Lambda c=(\lambda_1c_1,\ \lambda_2c_2)\)
    Stretch each axis on its own. Nothing is turned; a negative \(\lambda\) flips that axis.
  3. \(Q\Lambda c=Ax\)
    Rotate back. The circle has become an ellipse whose axes are the eigenvectors, with half-lengths \(|\lambda_1|,|\lambda_2|\).

Multiplying out the columns gives a second, very useful form: a sum of rank-one pieces,

$$A=\lambda_1q_1q_1^\top+\lambda_2q_2q_2^\top+\dots+\lambda_nq_nq_n^\top .$$

Each \(q_iq_i^\top\) projects onto one eigen-axis, and \(\lambda_i\) says how much to keep. Check with \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\): \(\lambda_1=3,\ q_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\) and \(\lambda_2=1,\ q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\), so \(3\cdot\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}+1\cdot\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\). Keeping only the largest terms is exactly how PCA compresses data.

Why it pays off. Any function of \(A\) becomes a function of numbers: \(A^k=Q\Lambda^kQ^\top\), \(A^{-1}=Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^\top\) (when no \(\lambda_i=0\)), and for a PSD matrix the square root \(A^{1/2}=Q\Lambda^{1/2}Q^\top\), which turns white noise into samples with covariance \(A\) (section 5).

Going deeper: proof of the spectral theorem

Real eigenvalues. Let \(Av=\lambda v\) with possibly complex \(v\ne0\), and let \(\bar v\) be its complex conjugate. Then \(\bar v^\top Av=\lambda\,\bar v^\top v\). Conjugating and transposing, and using \(A=A^\top\) real, gives \(\bar v^\top Av=\bar\lambda\,\bar v^\top v\). Since \(\bar v^\top v=\sum|v_i|^2>0\), \(\lambda=\bar\lambda\): \(\lambda\) is real.

Perpendicular eigenvectors. If \(Av=\lambda v\), \(Aw=\mu w\), \(\lambda\ne\mu\), then \(\lambda\,v^\top w=(Av)^\top w=v^\top Aw=\mu\,v^\top w\), so \(v^\top w=0\). When an eigenvalue repeats, its eigenvectors fill a whole plane (or more), and Gram–Schmidt (QR) picks a perpendicular set inside it.

Enough eigenvectors (by induction). Take one unit eigenvector \(q_1\). If \(x\perp q_1\) then \(q_1^\top(Ax)=(Aq_1)^\top x=\lambda_1q_1^\top x=0\), so \(A\) maps the subspace perpendicular to \(q_1\) into itself, and is still symmetric there. Repeat inside that \((n-1)\)-dimensional subspace. After \(n\) steps we have \(n\) perpendicular unit eigenvectors, with no defective case possible.

Compare the non-symmetric shear \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) from section 1, which has only one eigen-direction. The general replacement for every matrix is the SVD, \(A=U\Sigma V^\top\): rotate, stretch, rotate by a different rotation. For a PSD matrix the SVD and the spectral decomposition coincide.

Algorithm: applying A through its spectral decomposition
  1. input symmetric \(A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\), vector \(x\)
  2. \(c \leftarrow Q^\top x\) coordinates of x along the eigen-axes (rotate)
  3. for \(i=1,\dots,n\)
  4. \(c_i \leftarrow \lambda_i\,c_i\) stretch each axis independently
  5. \(y \leftarrow Qc\) rotate back to the original axes
  6. return \(y\) equals Ax; use λᵢᵏ in line 4 to get Aᵏx

5Covariance matrices are PSD: ellipses and PCA

A cloud with covariance \(\Sigma\) and its 1- and 2-standard-deviation ellipses. Drag the direction \(u\): the ticks are the points projected onto it, and the green curve shows the spread \(\sqrt{u^\top\Sigma u}\) in every direction. It peaks on the top eigenvector.

Take centred data points \(x\) with covariance matrix \(\Sigma=\mathbb E[xx^\top]\) (the covariance page builds it from scratch). Squash every point onto a unit direction \(u\): the result is one number per point, \(u^\top x\). Its variance is

  1. \(\operatorname{Var}(u^\top x)=\mathbb E[(u^\top x)^2]\)
    The data are centred, so the projection has mean 0.
  2. \(=\mathbb E[u^\top x\,x^\top u]=u^\top\,\mathbb E[xx^\top]\,u\)
    \(u^\top x\) is a number, so it equals its own transpose \(x^\top u\). \(u\) is fixed, so it comes out of the average.
  3. \(=u^\top\Sigma u\ \ge0\)
    A variance is an average of squares.
$$u^\top\Sigma u=\operatorname{Var}(u^\top x)\ge0\quad\text{for every }u\ \Longrightarrow\ \Sigma\succeq0 .$$

So every covariance matrix is positive semi-definite, with no exceptions. A zero eigenvalue means a direction with no spread at all: the points lie exactly on a line (in 2D) or a flat subspace, so one feature is an exact linear combination of the others. Slide \(\lambda_2\) to 0 to see the cloud collapse. Then \(\Sigma\) has no inverse, and the Gaussian density and the Mahalanobis distance \(x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x\), which both need \(\Sigma^{-1}\), break down.

Which direction has the most spread? Maximise \(u^\top\Sigma u\) over unit vectors. With \(u=Qc\) and \(\sum c_i^2=1\),

$$u^\top\Sigma u=\sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2\ \le\ \lambda_1\sum_ic_i^2=\lambda_1,$$

with equality when \(c=(1,0,\dots)\), i.e. \(u=q_1\). The best direction is the top eigenvector and the variance it captures is \(\lambda_1\). The ratio \(\frac{u^\top\Sigma u}{u^\top u}\) is called the Rayleigh quotient. This one line is the whole of PCA: the principal components are the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\), they are perpendicular because \(\Sigma\) is symmetric, and \(\lambda_i/\sum_j\lambda_j\) is the fraction of variance each one explains.

The ellipse tells the same story: its axes point along the eigenvectors with half-lengths \(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\sqrt{\lambda_2}\). Sampling uses it in reverse: \(x=Q\Lambda^{1/2}z\) with white noise \(z\) has covariance \(Q\Lambda^{1/2}\,I\,\Lambda^{1/2}Q^\top=\Sigma\) (Cholesky, section 8, gives another valid square root).

Going deeper: Lagrange multipliers, the next components, and the width of the ellipse

Via Lagrange. Maximise \(u^\top\Sigma u\) subject to \(u^\top u=1\). Set the gradient of \(u^\top\Sigma u-\lambda(u^\top u-1)\) to zero: \(2\Sigma u-2\lambda u=0\), i.e. \(\Sigma u=\lambda u\). Every stationary point is an eigenvector, and the value there is \(u^\top\Sigma u=\lambda\), so the maximum is the largest eigenvalue (see Lagrange multipliers).

The next component. Add the constraint \(u\perp q_1\). Then \(c_1=0\) and the same bound gives \(\lambda_2\), reached at \(q_2\). In general \(\lambda_k=\max\{u^\top\Sigma u:\|u\|=1,\ u\perp q_1,\dots,q_{k-1}\}\) (the Courant–Fischer min-max theorem).

Width of the ellipse. The 1-sd ellipse \(\{x:x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x=1\}\), squeezed between two parallel lines perpendicular to \(u\), has half-width exactly \(\sqrt{u^\top\Sigma u}\). So the green curve is the ellipse's "shadow length" in each direction. It touches the ellipse on the two axes, where the shadow is longest and shortest.

Sample covariance. For a data matrix \(X\) (rows are centred points), \(S=\tfrac1nX^\top X\) is a Gram matrix, so it is PSD by the identity \(v^\top X^\top Xv=\|Xv\|^2\). With fewer points than features (\(n<d\)), \(S\) has rank at most \(n\), so it is singular. This is why high-dimensional statistics adds shrinkage, \(S+\lambda I\).

6LU: elimination, written down

On the matrices page we solved systems by row reduction. Each step "subtract \(\ell\) times row \(j\) from row \(i\)" is a small matrix. Collect all those multipliers \(\ell_{ij}\) in a lower-triangular matrix \(L\) (ones on the diagonal), and the end result of elimination in an upper-triangular \(U\):

$$PA=LU .$$

\(P\) records any row swaps (pivoting, for numerical safety). Why bother? Because triangular systems are trivial to solve. To solve \(Ax=b\): first solve \(Ly=Pb\) from the top down (forward substitution), then \(Ux=y\) from the bottom up (back substitution). Factoring costs about \(\tfrac23n^3\) operations once; each new \(b\) then costs only \(2n^2\).

Edit \(A\) and \(b\): the factors and both substitutions update.

Algorithm: LU with partial pivoting, then solve
  1. \(U \leftarrow A\); \(L \leftarrow I\); \(P \leftarrow I\)
  2. for \(c = 1\) to \(n-1\) do
  3. swap row \(c\) of \(U\) with the row below having the largest \(|U_{rc}|\); record it in \(P\) (and \(L\)'s finished columns)
  4. for \(r = c+1\) to \(n\) do
  5. \(L_{rc} \leftarrow U_{rc}/U_{cc}\); row\(_r(U) \leftarrow\) row\(_r(U) - L_{rc}\,\)row\(_c(U)\)
  6. solve Ax = b using the factors
  7. for \(i = 1\) to \(n\): \(y_i \leftarrow (Pb)_i - \sum_{k<i} L_{ik}y_k\) forward
  8. for \(i = n\) down to 1: \(x_i \leftarrow (y_i - \sum_{k>i} U_{ik}x_k)/U_{ii}\) backward
  9. return \(x\)

7QR: Gram–Schmidt, written down

Drag \(a_1\) and \(a_2\). Gram–Schmidt turns them into perpendicular unit vectors \(q_1, q_2\) spanning the same plane.

The projections page showed that orthonormal bases make projection easy. Gram–Schmidt makes one from any independent columns:

  1. \(q_1=a_1/\|a_1\|\)
    Keep the first direction, scaled to length 1.
  2. \(u_2=a_2-(q_1\cdot a_2)\,q_1\)
    Remove the part of \(a_2\) that lies along \(q_1\) (its projection). What remains is perpendicular to \(q_1\).
  3. \(q_2=u_2/\|u_2\|\)
    Scale to length 1. With more columns, subtract the projections onto all previous \(q\)'s.

Reading the steps backwards gives \(a_1=r_{11}q_1\) and \(a_2=r_{12}q_1+r_{22}q_2\). In matrix form:

$$A=QR,\qquad Q^\top Q=I,\ R\text{ upper triangular}.$$

For least squares, substitute into the normal equations: \(R^\top Q^\top QR\theta=R^\top Q^\top y\) simplifies to \(R\theta=Q^\top y\), a triangular solve, without ever forming \(X^\top X\). This is what np.linalg.lstsq and most regression libraries do.

Algorithm: Gram–Schmidt QR
  1. input columns \(a_1,\dots,a_n\) of \(A\)
  2. for \(j = 1\) to \(n\) do
  3. \(u \leftarrow a_j\)
  4. for \(i = 1\) to \(j-1\) do
  5. \(R_{ij} \leftarrow q_i \cdot u\); \(u \leftarrow u - R_{ij}\,q_i\) modified G–S: project the running u, more stable
  6. \(R_{jj} \leftarrow \|u\|\); \(q_j \leftarrow u/R_{jj}\) R_jj ≈ 0 means a_j depends on earlier columns
  7. return \(Q = [q_1 \cdots q_n]\), \(R\)

8Cholesky: a square root for covariance matrices

Each dot is \(\mu+Lz\), where \(z\) is a pair of independent standard normal numbers. Change the covariance and the same \(z\)'s are reshaped.

A symmetric matrix with \(x^\top Ax>0\) for every \(x\ne0\) is positive definite. Covariance matrices are (at least semi-) definite, because \(x^\top\Sigma x\) is the variance of the combined feature \(x^\top z\) (a weighted sum of the variables), and a variance cannot be negative. Such matrices have a triangular "square root":

$$\Sigma=LL^\top,\qquad L\ \text{lower triangular with positive diagonal}.$$

It is LU made symmetric, at half the cost. A neat use: to draw samples with covariance \(\Sigma\), take independent standard normals \(z\) and set \(x=\mu+Lz\). Then \(\operatorname{Cov}(x)=L\operatorname{Cov}(z)L^\top=LIL^\top=\Sigma\). Gaussian processes, Kalman filters and the reparameterisation trick in VAEs all use this.

Algorithm: Cholesky factorisation
  1. input symmetric positive definite \(A\) (\(n\times n\))
  2. for \(i = 1\) to \(n\) do
  3. for \(j = 1\) to \(i\) do
  4. \(s \leftarrow A_{ij} - \sum_{k<j} L_{ik}L_{jk}\)
  5. if \(i = j\) then if \(s \le 0\) then fail not positive definite; \(L_{ii} \leftarrow \sqrt s\)
  6. else \(L_{ij} \leftarrow s / L_{jj}\)
  7. return \(L\) sample: x = μ + L z with z ~ N(0, I)
Going deeper: why Cholesky can fail, and what that tells you

The algorithm takes a square root of \(s=A_{ii}-\sum_kL_{ik}^2\) at every diagonal step. The theorem is that \(A\) is positive definite if and only if every such \(s\) is positive. So attempting a Cholesky factorisation is also the cheapest test of positive definiteness. Optimisers use it to check that a Newton step points downhill.

Positive definite has several equivalent faces: \(x^\top Ax>0\) for all \(x\ne0\); all eigenvalues positive; all leading principal minors positive; \(A=B^\top B\) for some \(B\) with independent columns. The last is why \(X^\top X\) (for independent features) and every covariance matrix with no exactly redundant variables qualify. In practice a covariance estimated from fewer samples than dimensions is only semi-definite, so libraries add a small "jitter" \(\epsilon I\) before factoring.

10The same thing in Python (NumPy / SciPy)

import numpy as np, scipy.linalg as sl
A = np.array([[2., 1.], [1., 3.]])
lam, V = np.linalg.eig(A)               # A @ V[:, i] == lam[i] * V[:, i]
lam, Q = np.linalg.eigh(A)              # symmetric: real, orthonormal (preferred)
np.allclose(Q @ np.diag(lam) @ Q.T, A)
P, L, U = sl.lu(A)                      # A = P @ L @ U
lu = sl.lu_factor(A); sl.lu_solve(lu, np.array([1., 2.]))   # reuse for many b
Q, R = np.linalg.qr(A)                  # Q.T @ Q == I, R upper triangular
L = np.linalg.cholesky(A)               # A = L @ L.T (fails if not positive definite)
x = np.array([0., 0.]) + L @ np.random.randn(2, 1000)   # samples with covariance A
