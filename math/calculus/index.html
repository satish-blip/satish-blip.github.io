Calculus for machine learning

Training a model means adjusting thousands or billions of numbers so that a loss goes down. To do that you need to know, for each number, which way to nudge it and how much the loss will respond. That is exactly what a derivative tells you. This page builds the calculus the rest of the site uses: slopes, gradients, the chain rule behind backpropagation, curvature, and optimisation with constraints.

What is calculus for, in plain words?

The idea

Take linear regression with a single weight \(w\). For every value of \(w\) there is a loss \(J(w)\): how badly the line fits. Training asks for the \(w\) with the smallest loss. We could try every value, but with a million weights that is hopeless. Instead we stand at the current \(w\) and ask a local question: if I increase \(w\) a tiny bit, does the loss go up or down, and how fast? The answer is the derivative \(J'(w)\), and stepping against it is gradient descent.

With many weights we ask the same question for each one separately. The answers, collected into a vector, form the gradient. A neural network is a long chain of simple functions, and the chain rule lets us get all those answers in one backward sweep: backpropagation.

How it works

  1. A derivative is a slope: output change divided by input change, for a tiny change.
  2. A gradient lists the slope along every input direction; it points uphill.
  3. The chain rule multiplies slopes along a chain of functions; backpropagation applies it from the loss backwards.
  4. Second derivatives (the Hessian) measure curvature: bowl, ridge or saddle.

An everyday picture

Walking down a hill in thick fog. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your feet. Feeling the tilt in the north–south and east–west directions gives you the gradient; stepping the steepest way down, again and again, gets you to a low point. Whether that low point is the lowest in the whole landscape depends on the shape of the land: on a single smooth bowl (a convex loss) it always is.

Good at
  • Turning "find the best parameters" into small, local, repeatable steps
  • Scaling to billions of parameters with automatic differentiation
  • Explaining why training is fast, slow or unstable
Watch out for
  • Slopes are local: a zero slope may be a dip, a peak or a saddle
  • Functions with kinks (ReLU, \(|x|\)) need care at the kink

1The derivative: the slope at a point

The curve is \(f\). The dashed line joins the points at \(x\) and \(x+h\); its slope is the average rate of change. Shrink \(h\) and it turns into the tangent (solid), whose slope is \(f'(x)\).

The slope of a straight line is "rise over run": how much the output changes per unit of input. A curve has a different slope at every point, so we zoom in. Pick a point \(x\), step a little way \(h\) to the right, and measure the rise over that run:

  1. \(\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\)
    The slope of the dashed line: the average rate of change between \(x\) and \(x+h\).
  2. \(f'(x)=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\)
    Let the step shrink. If the slope settles on one number, that is the derivative, also written \(\frac{df}{dx}\).
  3. \(f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\,h\)
    Read it backwards: near \(x\), the function looks like a straight line with slope \(f'(x)\). That is what makes small steps predictable.

For \(f(x)=x^2\): \(\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}=2x+h\), which tends to \(2x\). Slide \(h\) towards 0 and watch the secant slope approach \(2x\). The sign says which way is uphill; the size says how steep. At the bottom of the bowl the slope is 0: that is how we recognise a minimum.

Going deeper: kinks, and how computers really differentiate

A derivative exists only where the function is smooth enough to look like a line when you zoom in. \(|x|\) and ReLU have a corner at 0: the slope is \(-1\) on one side and \(+1\) (or 0) on the other. Libraries simply pick a value at the corner (a subgradient), which works because landing exactly on the corner almost never happens.

There are three ways to get derivatives on a computer. Symbolic: apply the rules to the formula, as above (SymPy). Numerical: use the difference quotient with a small \(h\); simple but inexact, and too slow for many parameters. Automatic differentiation: apply the chain rule to the actual sequence of operations the program runs, exactly and cheaply. PyTorch and JAX use the last; the second is still used to check it (next section).

2Partial derivatives and the gradient

A function of two inputs, drawn as a contour map: each line joins points of equal height. Drag the point. The red arrow is the gradient (steepest uphill); the purple arrow is minus the gradient, the direction gradient descent steps.

Real losses depend on many parameters, \(f(\theta_1,\theta_2,\dots)\). The trick is to change one input at a time and hold the others fixed. The slope you get is a partial derivative, written with a curly \(\partial\):

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_1}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\theta_1+h,\theta_2)-f(\theta_1,\theta_2)}{h}.$$

On the map, \(\partial f/\partial\theta_1\) is the slope you feel walking due east; \(\partial f/\partial\theta_2\) walking due north. Collect all the partial derivatives into one vector and you have the gradient:

$$\nabla f(\theta)=\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_1},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_2},\ \dots\Big).$$

Three facts make it the most useful object in machine learning:

  1. \(f(\theta+d)\approx f(\theta)+\nabla f(\theta)\cdot d\)
    The change in \(f\) for a small step \(d\) in any direction is a dot product with the gradient.
  2. steepest uphill: \(d\propto\nabla f\)
    Among steps of the same length, the dot product is largest when \(d\) points along \(\nabla f\). So \(-\nabla f\) is the steepest way down.
  3. \(\nabla f\perp\) the contour line
    Walking along a contour keeps \(f\) constant, so \(\nabla f\cdot d=0\) there: the gradient crosses contours at right angles. Look at the red arrow.

Gradient descent repeats \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla f(\theta)\). Press Run on the narrow valley: the gradient points across the valley rather than along it, so the path zig-zags. That is the problem momentum and Adam fix on the optimisers page. Where the gradient is zero there is no downhill direction: a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle (try the saddle).

Going deeper: directional derivatives, and why the gradient is perpendicular

The slope in the direction of a unit vector \(u\) is the directional derivative \(D_uf=\nabla f\cdot u=\|\nabla f\|\cos\angle(\nabla f,u)\). It is largest, \(\|\nabla f\|\), when \(u\) points along the gradient, zero when \(u\) is perpendicular to it, and most negative pointing the opposite way. That single formula gives all three facts above.

For a loss averaged over \(n\) examples, \(\nabla J=\frac1n\sum_i\nabla J_i\): the gradient of a sum is the sum of the gradients. Stochastic gradient descent estimates it from a mini-batch, which is noisy but far cheaper.

Algorithm: gradient check by finite differences
  1. input function \(f\), a gradient \(g=\nabla f(\theta)\) from your code, point \(\theta\), small \(h\) (e.g. \(10^{-5}\))
  2. for each coordinate \(j\) do
  3. \(\tilde g_j\leftarrow\dfrac{f(\theta+he_j)-f(\theta-he_j)}{2h}\) nudge one input both ways: central difference
  4. \(\text{err}\leftarrow\|g-\tilde g\|\,/\,(\|g\|+\|\tilde g\|)\) relative error
  5. if err \(<10^{-6}\) then the gradient code is right else look for a bug

3The chain rule and backpropagation

A one-neuron model as a graph: \(z=wx+b\), \(a=\sigma(z)\), loss \(L=(a-y)^2\). Black numbers are the forward values; red numbers are the backward derivatives \(\partial L/\partial(\cdot)\); each edge shows its local slope.

Most functions in machine learning are built by feeding one function into another. If \(y=g(x)\) and \(L=f(y)\), how fast does \(L\) change with \(x\)? Rates multiply. If turning a crank by 1 turns a gear by 3, and turning that gear by 1 lifts a weight by 2, the crank lifts the weight by \(3\times2=6\):

$$\frac{dL}{dx}=\frac{dL}{dy}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}.$$

That is the chain rule. For a longer chain, multiply all the local slopes along it. When a quantity feeds into several places, add the contributions from each path.

Look at the graph. To get \(\partial L/\partial w\) we need three local slopes: how \(L\) responds to \(a\) (\(2(a-y)\)), how \(a\) responds to \(z\) (\(\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\)), and how \(z\) responds to \(w\) (it is \(x\)). So

  1. \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a}=2(a-y)\)
    Start at the loss.
  2. \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a}\,\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))\)
    Multiply by the local slope of the sigmoid.
  3. \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z}\,x,\qquad \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b}=\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z}\cdot1\)
    Both \(w\) and \(b\) reuse \(\partial L/\partial z\): it was computed once.

Working from the loss backwards, each node needs only the derivative from the node after it and its own local slope. Every intermediate result is reused. That is backpropagation (reverse-mode automatic differentiation): one forward pass to compute values, one backward pass to compute every derivative, at roughly the cost of two forward passes, however many parameters there are. See it on a full network on the neural networks page.

The chain rule also explains a classic failure. The backward signal is a product of local slopes, one per layer. If those slopes are usually below 1 (the sigmoid's is at most 0.25) the product shrinks exponentially with depth: vanishing gradients. Above 1, it explodes. Push \(w\) to an extreme and watch \(\sigma'(z)\) and every red number upstream collapse.

Algorithm: reverse-mode differentiation (backpropagation) on a graph
  1. input a computation graph ending in a scalar \(L\), input values
  2. for each node \(v\) in order from inputs to \(L\) do compute and store its value forward pass
  3. \(\bar L\leftarrow1\); every other \(\bar v\leftarrow0\) v̄ is short for ∂L/∂v
  4. for each node \(v\) in reverse order do
  5. for each input \(u\) of \(v\) do \(\bar u\leftarrow\bar u+\bar v\cdot\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial u}\) chain rule; add over paths
  6. return \(\bar w\) for every parameter \(w\)

4The Jacobian and the Hessian: slopes of vectors, and curvature

Solid lines: the true contours. Dashed: the second-order Taylor approximation built at the point from the gradient and the Hessian. Drag the point and compare. The arrows are the Hessian's eigenvectors (directions of extreme curvature).

The Jacobian: the derivative of a vector-valued function

A layer of a network maps a vector to a vector: \(h=F(x)\) with \(m\) outputs and \(n\) inputs. Each output has its own gradient. Stack those gradients as rows and you get the Jacobian matrix:

$$J_F=\begin{bmatrix}\partial F_1/\partial x_1&\cdots&\partial F_1/\partial x_n\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ \partial F_m/\partial x_1&\cdots&\partial F_m/\partial x_n\end{bmatrix},\qquad F(x+d)\approx F(x)+J_F\,d .$$

It is the best linear map approximating \(F\) near \(x\). For a linear layer \(F(x)=Wx\) the Jacobian is simply \(W\). The chain rule for vectors is a matrix product of Jacobians, \(J_{F\circ G}=J_F\,J_G\), and backpropagation computes it efficiently from the right-hand end, one vector at a time.

The Hessian: curvature

The gradient says which way is downhill; it does not say whether the ground is curving up (a bowl) or down (a hilltop). For that we take derivatives of the derivatives. The Hessian collects all second partial derivatives:

$$H_{jk}=\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial\theta_j\,\partial\theta_k},\qquad f(\theta+d)\approx f(\theta)+\nabla f\cdot d+\tfrac12\,d^\top Hd .$$

The formula on the right is the second-order Taylor approximation: near any point, a smooth function looks like a quadratic bowl, ridge or saddle (the dashed contours). The Hessian is symmetric, so it has perpendicular eigenvectors, and its eigenvalues are the curvatures along them:

  • all eigenvalues positive (positive definite): curving up in every direction, a local minimum if the gradient is also zero;
  • all negative: a local maximum;
  • mixed signs: a saddle, up one way and down another. In high dimensions, most points with zero gradient are saddles.

A large ratio between the biggest and smallest curvature (the condition number of \(H\)) means a long narrow valley, and slow, zig-zagging gradient descent. Newton's method uses the Hessian to jump straight to the minimum of the quadratic approximation, \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-H^{-1}\nabla f\). It converges in a few steps (see maximum likelihood and logistic regression), but storing \(H\) for a million parameters is impossible, which is why deep learning uses first-order methods.

5Convexity: when every valley is the valley

The dashed chord joins two points on the curve. A function is convex when every such chord stays on or above it. The dots show gradient descent from the start point.

A function is convex if the straight line between any two points on its graph never dips below the graph:

$$f\big(t\,a+(1-t)\,b\big)\le t\,f(a)+(1-t)\,f(b)\quad\text{for all }a,b\text{ and }0\le t\le1 .$$

Picture a bowl, possibly with flat or sharp parts, but never with a second dip. For smooth functions there is an easy test: \(f''(x)\ge0\) everywhere (in many dimensions, the Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere).

Why does this matter so much? A local minimum is a point lower than everything nearby; a global minimum is lower than everything, anywhere. For a convex function they are the same thing: if any point is a local minimum, it is the global one. So gradient descent cannot get stuck in the wrong valley. Try the double well: depending on where you start, descent ends in a different valley, and only one is the best.

Linear regression, ridge, lasso, logistic regression and the SVM all have convex losses, which is why they have reliable solvers and a single answer. Neural network losses are not convex. They have many minima and saddle points, yet in practice the minima that stochastic gradient descent finds tend to be about equally good.

Going deeper: building convex functions, and strict convexity

Sums of convex functions are convex; a convex function of a linear map, \(f(Ax+b)\), is convex; the maximum of convex functions is convex. That is how we know at a glance that squared error plus an L1 or L2 penalty is convex, and so is the hinge loss \(\max(0,1-y\,w^\top x)\).

Strictly convex functions (\(f''>0\)) have at most one minimiser. Least squares is convex but not strictly so when features are collinear: a whole line of weights fits equally well. Adding \(\lambda\|w\|^2\) (ridge) makes it strictly convex and the answer unique.

6Optimising with a constraint: Lagrange multipliers

Minimise \(f\) (its contours are the circles around the orange target) while staying on the purple constraint circle \(g=0\). Drag the point around the circle, or drag the target. Red: \(\nabla f\). Purple: \(\nabla g\).

Sometimes the parameters must obey a rule: weights of length 1, probabilities that add up to 1, a budget. We want the lowest \(f(\theta)\) among the points with \(g(\theta)=0\).

Walk around the constraint and watch \(f\). At most points, moving one way along the circle lowers \(f\): the red arrow has a component along the circle. At the best point that component must be zero, so \(\nabla f\) points straight across the circle, exactly like \(\nabla g\) (which is always perpendicular to its own contour \(g=0\)). Two vectors pointing along the same line are multiples of each other:

  1. \(\nabla f(\theta)=\lambda\,\nabla g(\theta),\quad g(\theta)=0\)
    The tangency condition. The number \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier.
  2. \(\mathcal L(\theta,\lambda)=f(\theta)-\lambda\,g(\theta)\)
    Both conditions are \(\nabla\mathcal L=0\) for this one function, the Lagrangian: setting \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\lambda=0\) gives back \(g=0\).
  3. \(\lambda=\dfrac{\partial f^\star}{\partial c}\) for the constraint \(g=c\)
    \(\lambda\) says how much the best value would improve if the constraint were loosened a little: its "price".

A beautiful example is PCA. We want the unit direction \(w\) along which the data varies most: maximise \(w^\top\Sigma w\) subject to \(w^\top w=1\). The Lagrange condition is \(2\Sigma w=2\lambda w\), that is \(\Sigma w=\lambda w\): the best direction is an eigenvector of the covariance matrix, and the variance it captures is \(w^\top\Sigma w=\lambda\), its eigenvalue. The SVM's dual problem and the maximum-entropy derivation of softmax come from the same idea.

Going deeper: inequality constraints and the KKT conditions

With a constraint \(g(\theta)\le0\) there are two cases. Either the best point is strictly inside (the constraint does not matter, \(\lambda=0\)) or it is on the boundary (\(\nabla f=-\lambda\nabla g\) with \(\lambda\ge0\)). Both are captured by \(\lambda\,g(\theta)=0\), complementary slackness. Together with stationarity and feasibility these are the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions. In the SVM, the points with \(\lambda_i>0\) are exactly the support vectors.

Algorithm: Lagrange multipliers for an equality constraint
  1. input objective \(f(\theta)\), constraint \(g(\theta)=0\)
  2. form the Lagrangian \(\mathcal L=f-\lambda g\)
  3. solve \(\nabla f(\theta)=\lambda\nabla g(\theta)\) together with \(g(\theta)=0\) gradients parallel, on the constraint
  4. for each solution \((\theta,\lambda)\) do evaluate \(f(\theta)\) there may be several: minima and maxima
  5. return the candidate with the smallest \(f\)

7Where calculus shows up in ML

Every training loop

Compute the loss, backpropagate to get \(\nabla_\theta J\), step against it. Linear regression's gradient descent, logistic regression and every neural network train this way.

Closed-form answers

Setting the gradient to zero sometimes gives the answer directly: \(\nabla_\theta\|X\theta-y\|^2=0\) gives the normal equations, and setting the derivative of a log-likelihood to zero gives the maximum-likelihood estimate.

Optimisers

Momentum and Adam smooth and rescale gradients to cope with the narrow valleys a badly conditioned Hessian creates. See the optimisers page.

Activations and depth

The slope of each activation function enters the chain-rule product once per layer: the reason for ReLU, residual connections and LSTMs.

Constraints

The SVM dual, PCA as an eigenvalue problem, and the softmax as a maximum-entropy distribution all come from Lagrange multipliers.

Uncertainty

The curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak (the Fisher information, a Hessian) sets the width of confidence intervals: see how sure is the estimate.

8The same thing in Python

import numpy as np
import torch

f = lambda t: 0.4*t[0]**2 + 0.9*t[1]**2 + 0.5*t[0]*t[1]

# numerical gradient: central differences
def num_grad(f, t, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(t)
    for j in range(len(t)):
        e = np.zeros_like(t); e[j] = h
        g[j] = (f(t + e) - f(t - e)) / (2*h)
    return g
num_grad(f, np.array([1., 2.]))          # [1.8, 4.1]

# automatic differentiation (reverse mode = backpropagation)
t = torch.tensor([1., 2.], requires_grad=True)
f(t).backward(); t.grad                    # tensor([1.8, 4.1])
torch.autograd.functional.hessian(f, torch.tensor([1., 2.]))   # [[0.8, 0.5], [0.5, 1.8]]

# one neuron, the graph from section 3
w = torch.tensor(0.8, requires_grad=True); b = torch.tensor(-0.5, requires_grad=True)
L = (torch.sigmoid(w*1.5 + b) - 1.)**2
L.backward(); w.grad, b.grad
