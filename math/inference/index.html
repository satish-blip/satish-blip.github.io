Inference and hypothesis testing

We never see the whole population, only a sample. Inference is the art of saying something reliable about the whole from the part: an estimate, how far off it might be, and whether an apparent effect is real or just luck. These are the tools you need to say honestly whether model B is better than model A.

What is statistical inference, in plain words?

The idea

You train a new model and it scores 84.0% on a test set of 500 examples; the old one scored 82.6%. Is the new model better, or did it get a lucky test set? Another 500 examples would give slightly different numbers. Inference treats the score itself as random and asks how much it would wobble from sample to sample.

Three questions come up again and again. Estimation: what is our best guess of the unknown number (true accuracy, a mean, a weight)? Uncertainty: how far off might that guess be? Testing: is the data surprising enough to reject a "nothing is going on" explanation?

How it works

  1. Model the data as random draws from a distribution with unknown parameters.
  2. Estimate the parameters, usually by maximum likelihood.
  3. Work out how the estimate varies across samples: its standard error, often via the central limit theorem.
  4. Turn that into a confidence interval or a p-value.

An everyday picture

Tasting soup. You do not drink the whole pot to know whether it needs salt; one well-stirred spoonful tells you. A bigger spoon gives a more reliable taste, but with diminishing returns: four times the spoon only halves the uncertainty. And if the pot was not stirred (a biased sample), no spoon size will save you.

Good at
  • Putting error bars on any number you report
  • Separating real improvements from noise
  • Planning how much data you need
Watch out for
  • A p-value is not the probability the hypothesis is true
  • Testing many things at once guarantees some false alarms
  • Significant does not mean large or important

1Samples, estimates and the central limit theorem

Top: the population. Bottom: the means of many samples of size \(n\), with the normal curve the CLT predicts.

Say the population (all possible values) has mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), neither of which we know. We draw \(n\) independent values \(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(n)}\) and compute the sample mean \(\bar x=\frac1n\sum_ix^{(i)}\). A rule like this, which turns data into a guess, is an estimator. Because the data are random, \(\bar x\) is random too. Two facts describe how it behaves:

  1. \(\mathbb E[\bar x]=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbb E[x^{(i)}]=\mu\)
    On average the estimate is right: it is unbiased.
  2. \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(x^{(i)})=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\)
    Variances add for independent draws, and the \(\frac1n\) comes out squared.
  3. \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar x)=\sigma/\sqrt n\)
    The standard error: the typical size of the estimate's error. Four times the data halves it.

The central limit theorem adds the shape: whatever the population looks like, for large enough \(n\) the distribution of \(\bar x\) is close to the normal \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\). Pick the skewed or two-humped population with \(n=1\), then raise \(n\) and watch the bell appear. This is why the normal distribution is everywhere in statistics.

2Maximum likelihood: choose the parameters that make the data least surprising

Twelve data points (dots) and a Gaussian with your \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). The lower plot is the log-likelihood as \(\mu\) varies, with \(\sigma\) held fixed.

Suppose we believe the data come from a Gaussian \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) but do not know \(\mu,\sigma\). The likelihood of a guess is how probable the observed data would be under it. For independent points it is a product, so we work with its log, a sum:

$$\ell(\mu,\sigma)=\log\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(x^{(i)};\mu,\sigma)=-\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i}\big(x^{(i)}-\mu\big)^2 .$$

Maximising it is calculus:

  1. \(\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu}=\dfrac1{\sigma^2}\sum_i(x^{(i)}-\mu)=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\mu=\bar x\)
    The best mean is the sample mean. Note that \(\mu\) only appears in a sum of squares: this is least squares in disguise.
  2. \(\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\sigma}=-\dfrac n\sigma+\dfrac1{\sigma^3}\sum_i(x^{(i)}-\hat\mu)^2=0\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\sigma^2=\dfrac1n\sum_i(x^{(i)}-\bar x)^2\)
    The best variance is the average squared deviation.

Drag the sliders and watch the log-likelihood: it peaks exactly at \(\bar x\) and at \(\hat\sigma\). This is the same principle that justifies least-squares regression (Gaussian noise) and cross-entropy loss (Bernoulli or categorical labels).

The full story, with four models, Fisher information and Newton's method, is on the maximum likelihood page.

Going deeper: why \(n-1\), and how good MLE is

\(\hat\sigma^2\) is slightly too small on average: \(\mathbb E[\hat\sigma^2]=\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\). The reason is that \(\bar x\) is fitted to the same data, so deviations from \(\bar x\) are smaller than deviations from the true \(\mu\). Precisely, \(\sum_i(x^{(i)}-\mu)^2=\sum_i(x^{(i)}-\bar x)^2+n(\bar x-\mu)^2\), and taking expectations gives \(n\sigma^2=\mathbb E\sum_i(x^{(i)}-\bar x)^2+\sigma^2\). Dividing by \(n-1\) instead (Bessel's correction) makes it unbiased. One parameter estimated costs one "degree of freedom".

Under mild conditions MLE is consistent (it converges to the truth as \(n\to\infty\)) and asymptotically normal: \(\hat\theta\approx\mathcal N\big(\theta,\,I(\theta)^{-1}/n\big)\), where the Fisher information \(I(\theta)=-\mathbb E[\partial^2\log p/\partial\theta^2]\) measures how sharply peaked the log-likelihood is. A sharp peak means the data pin the parameter down well. No unbiased estimator can beat this variance (the Cramér–Rao bound). The standard errors reported for logistic regression coefficients come from the inverse Hessian of the log-likelihood, which is exactly this.

Algorithm: maximum likelihood when there is no closed form
  1. input data \(x^{(1..n)}\), model density \(p(x;\theta)\), step size \(\alpha\)
  2. \(\theta\leftarrow\) initial guess
  3. repeat
  4. \(g\leftarrow\sum_{i}\nabla_\theta\log p(x^{(i)};\theta)\) gradient of the log-likelihood
  5. \(\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\,g\) gradient ascent; equivalently descent on −ℓ, the "loss"
  6. until \(\ell(\theta)\) stops increasing
  7. return \(\hat\theta\); standard errors \(\approx\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(-H^{-1})}\), with \(H\) the Hessian of \(\ell\) at \(\hat\theta\)

3Confidence intervals: what "95%" really means

Each bar is the interval from one fresh sample. The vertical line is the true mean. Red bars miss it.

By the CLT, \(\bar x\) is within \(1.96\) standard errors of \(\mu\) in 95% of samples. Flip that sentence around: the interval

$$\bar x\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n}\qquad\text{or, for small }n,\qquad \bar x\pm t_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n}$$

contains \(\mu\) for 95% of samples. Here \(s\) is the sample standard deviation (with \(n-1\)). Because we estimate \(\sigma\) by \(s\), small samples use the slightly wider Student-\(t\) multiplier instead of 1.96.

The careful reading matters. The 95% is a property of the procedure, not of one interval. \(\mu\) is fixed. Each interval either contains it or does not, and over many repetitions 95% do. Draw batches of 50 and count the red ones.

Algorithm: bootstrap confidence interval (for any statistic)
  1. input data \(x^{(1..n)}\), statistic \(T\) (mean, median, accuracy, F1, …), \(B\approx2000\)
  2. for \(b=1\) to \(B\) do
  3. draw \(n\) points from the data with replacement a pretend new sample
  4. \(T_b\leftarrow T(\text{resample})\)
  5. return the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of \(T_1,\dots,T_B\) no formula or normality needed

4Hypothesis tests and p-values

The curve is the distribution of the test statistic if \(H_0\) were true. Shaded tails: the rejection region (area \(\alpha\)). The solid line is what we observed; the purple area beyond it is the p-value.

Back to the opening example. On \(n\) test examples, record for each one the difference \(d^{(i)}\) between the new and old model's score (paired, because both saw the same example). Is the mean difference \(\bar d\) real? A test follows a fixed recipe:

Algorithm: a hypothesis test
  1. State the null hypothesis \(H_0\): "no real difference", \(\mu_d=0\). The alternative \(H_1\): \(\mu_d\ne0\) (two-sided) or \(\mu_d>0\) (one-sided).
  2. Choose the significance level \(\alpha\) (often 0.05) before looking.
  3. Compute a test statistic that measures surprise: \(t=\dfrac{\bar d-0}{s_d/\sqrt n}\) signal ÷ noise
  4. Find its distribution assuming \(H_0\) is true: Student-\(t\) with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom.
  5. p-value \(\leftarrow P_{H_0}(\text{a statistic at least as extreme as } t)\)
  6. if p-value \(\le\alpha\) then reject \(H_0\) else "not enough evidence" never "H₀ is true"

The p-value answers one specific question: if there were truly no effect, how often would chance alone produce data this extreme? A small p-value says "chance alone would rarely do this", which is evidence against \(H_0\). It is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true. That would need Bayes' theorem and a prior (see the medical test).

5Two ways to be wrong, and power

Grey: the statistic's distribution if \(H_0\) is true. Purple: its distribution if the true effect is \(\delta\). The dashed line is the cut-off.

A test can go wrong in two ways:

\(H_0\) true (no effect)\(H_1\) true (real effect)
reject \(H_0\)Type I error (false alarm), probability \(\alpha\)correct: this probability is the power, \(1-\beta\)
do not rejectcorrect, probability \(1-\alpha\)Type II error (missed effect), probability \(\beta\)

Lowering \(\alpha\) moves the cut-off right: fewer false alarms but more misses. The way to get both down is more data. The standard error shrinks like \(1/\sqrt n\), so the two curves separate. For a one-sided z-test with standardised effect size \(\delta/\sigma\), the power is \(1-\Phi\big(z_{1-\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{\sigma}\sqrt n\big)\). Setting it to 80% and solving for \(n\) tells you how much data a study needs before you run it.

6Using this honestly in ML

Comparing two models

Use the same test examples for both and test the paired differences, as in section 4. Pairing removes the variation between easy and hard examples. For accuracy on one test set, McNemar's test looks only at the examples where the models disagree. Across cross-validation folds, remember that folds share training data, so the scores are not independent and naive t-tests are over-confident.

Multiple comparisons

Try 20 hyperparameter settings that are all truly useless, and on average one will look "significant" at \(\alpha=0.05\). The simplest fix (Bonferroni) tests each at \(\alpha/m\). The ML-specific version of this problem is tuning on the test set: every peek uses up its value as an unbiased check. Keep a final test set you touch once.

Significant is not the same as important

With a million examples, a 0.01% accuracy gain can be "highly significant" and still useless. Always report the effect size with a confidence interval, not just a p-value.

Algorithm: permutation test for "is model B better than A?"
  1. input per-example scores \(a^{(i)}, b^{(i)}\) on the same \(n\) test examples, \(R\approx10{,}000\)
  2. \(T_{\text{obs}}\leftarrow\operatorname{mean}(b-a)\)
  3. for \(r=1\) to \(R\) do
  4. for each example, swap \(a^{(i)}\leftrightarrow b^{(i)}\) with probability ½ under H₀ the labels "A" and "B" are interchangeable
  5. \(T_r\leftarrow\operatorname{mean}(b-a)\) on the shuffled scores
  6. p-value \(\leftarrow\big(1+\#\{r:|T_r|\ge|T_{\text{obs}}|\}\big)/(R+1)\)
  7. return p-value no normality assumption; works for F1, BLEU, anything

Regression coefficients

The "is this coefficient zero?" t-statistics in a regression table are exactly section 4 applied to \(\hat\theta_j/\operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta_j)\). The linear regression page computes these live.

Going deeper: frequentist vs Bayesian answers

A confidence interval and a p-value are statements about procedures under repeated sampling, with the parameter fixed. A Bayesian credible interval (from the posterior) is a statement about the parameter given this data: "\(\theta\) is in this range with 95% probability". With a flat prior and lots of data the two often agree numerically, but they answer different questions.

To get from a p-value to "how likely is my effect real?" you need the prior probability that effects like it are real, and the test's power. If only 10% of the ideas you test work, and your power is 50%, then of the results with \(p<0.05\) about \(\frac{0.1\cdot0.5}{0.1\cdot0.5+0.9\cdot0.05}\approx53\%\) are real. That is a coin flip, and it explains much of the replication crisis. The computation is the medical-test calculation again, with "ill" replaced by "the effect is real".

7The same thing in Python (SciPy)

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.exponential(2.0, size=40)
x.mean(), x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(x))          # estimate and standard error
stats.t.interval(0.95, df=len(x)-1, loc=x.mean(), scale=stats.sem(x))   # 95% CI
mu_hat, sigma_hat = stats.norm.fit(x)                 # maximum likelihood (σ with 1/n)
a = rng.random(500) < 0.826; b = rng.random(500) < 0.840   # per-example correctness
stats.ttest_rel(b, a)                                 # paired t-test
stats.permutation_test((a, b), lambda a, b: (b - a).mean(), permutation_type='samples')
boot = stats.bootstrap((b.astype(float),), np.mean); boot.confidence_interval
from statsmodels.stats.power import TTestPower
TTestPower().solve_power(effect_size=0.3, alpha=0.05, power=0.8)   # n needed ≈ 90
