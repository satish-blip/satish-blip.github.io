The singular value decomposition

Eigenvectors only work for square matrices, and even then not always. The SVD works for every matrix, of any shape. It says that any linear map is a rotation, then a stretch along perpendicular axes, then another rotation. From that one statement come rank, the best low-rank approximation, PCA and the pseudo-inverse.

What is the SVD, in plain words?

The idea

Take a table of movie ratings: 1,000 users by 500 films. It has half a million numbers, yet it is far from random. People who like one action film tend to like others; some films please almost everyone. Intuitively, a handful of hidden "tastes" explain most of the table. The SVD finds those tastes and ranks them by importance.

Formally, every \(m\times n\) matrix can be written

$$A=U\Sigma V^\top=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^\top ,$$

a sum of simple rank-one pieces \(u_iv_i^\top\), each weighted by a singular value \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0\). Keep the first few pieces and you keep most of the matrix.

How it works

  1. \(V^\top\) rotates the input so that special perpendicular directions \(v_i\) line up with the axes.
  2. \(\Sigma\) stretches axis \(i\) by \(\sigma_i\) (and drops or adds dimensions if the shape changes).
  3. \(U\) rotates the result into place, sending axis \(i\) to direction \(u_i\).
  4. Sort by \(\sigma_i\): the first terms carry most of what \(A\) does.

An everyday picture

A recipe card for any linear transformation, always with the same three steps: turn the dough, roll it out along two perpendicular directions by different amounts, turn the tray. Different matrices only differ in the angles and the rolling amounts.

Good at
  • Works for any matrix: rectangular, singular, anything
  • Gives the best possible rank-\(k\) approximation
  • Numerically the most reliable way to find rank and conditioning
Watch out for
  • Costly for huge matrices: use truncated or randomised SVD
  • Signs of \(u_i, v_i\) are arbitrary (flip both together)

1Rotate, stretch, rotate

Slide through the three stages. The circle is every unit vector; the two arrows are \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) being carried along.

Apply any \(2\times2\) matrix to the unit circle and you get an ellipse (possibly a flat one). An ellipse has two perpendicular axes, a long one and a short one. Their lengths are the singular values \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\), and their directions are \(u_1, u_2\). The unit vectors that land on those axes are \(v_1, v_2\), and they are perpendicular too. So

$$Av_1=\sigma_1u_1,\qquad Av_2=\sigma_2u_2$$ $$\Longleftrightarrow\quad AV=U\Sigma\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A=U\Sigma V^\top .$$

The last step uses \(V^{-1}=V^\top\), true for any matrix with orthonormal columns. Watch the stages: \(V^\top\) turns \(v_1, v_2\) onto the axes, \(\Sigma\) stretches the axes, \(U\) turns the result into the final ellipse.

2Where \(U\), \(\Sigma\) and \(V\) come from

The picture says the SVD exists in 2-D. Here is why it exists for every matrix, using only the spectral theorem.

  1. \(A^\top A\) is \(n\times n\), symmetric, and \(x^\top A^\top Ax=\|Ax\|^2\ge0\)
    So it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors \(v_i\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\ge0\).
  2. \(\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i},\qquad \|Av_i\|^2=v_i^\top A^\top Av_i=\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2\)
    The singular values are the square roots; \(\sigma_i\) is how much \(A\) stretches \(v_i\).
  3. \(u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i\) (for \(\sigma_i>0\))
    Define the output directions as the normalised images.
  4. \(u_i\cdot u_j=\dfrac{v_i^\top A^\top Av_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=\dfrac{\lambda_jv_i^\top v_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=\begin{cases}1&i=j\\0&i\ne j\end{cases}\)
    The \(u_i\) are automatically orthonormal: perpendicular inputs land on perpendicular outputs.
  5. \(AV=U\Sigma\;\Rightarrow\;A=U\Sigma V^\top\)
    That is steps 2 and 3 for every \(i\), stacked as columns.

The number of nonzero \(\sigma_i\) is the rank. Numerically, "nonzero" means "above a small tolerance", which is how matrix_rank decides.

Algorithm: SVD via the eigenvectors of \(A^\top A\) (teaching version)
  1. input \(A\) of size \(m\times n\)
  2. \(G\leftarrow A^\top A\) n×n, symmetric, positive semi-definite
  3. \((\lambda_i, v_i)\leftarrow\) eigenpairs of \(G\), sorted so \(\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\dots\)
  4. for each \(i\) do
  5. \(\sigma_i\leftarrow\sqrt{\max(\lambda_i,0)}\)
  6. if \(\sigma_i>\varepsilon\) then \(u_i\leftarrow Av_i/\sigma_i\) else stop: rank \(=i-1\)
  7. return \(U=[u_i]\), \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_i)\), \(V=[v_i]\)

This is how the demos on this page compute it. Production code (LAPACK's gesdd) never forms \(A^\top A\), because that squares the condition number. Instead it reduces \(A\) to a two-diagonal (bidiagonal) form with orthogonal transformations and iterates on that.

Worked example

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}\). Then \(A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{bmatrix}\), with eigenvalues 45 and 5 and eigenvectors \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\) and \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\).

So \(\sigma_1=\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt5\approx6.71\) and \(\sigma_2=\sqrt5\approx2.24\). Also \(u_1=Av_1/\sigma_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(1,3)\).

Check: \(\sigma_1\sigma_2=15=|\det A|\). The area scale is the product of the stretches.

3The best low-rank approximation

A 48×48 greyscale image is a 48×48 matrix. Keep only the first \(k\) terms of its SVD.

original
rank k

Write the SVD as a sum of rank-one layers and stop after \(k\) of them:

$$A_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^\top,\qquad \|A-A_k\|_F=\sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2+\dots+\sigma_r^2}.$$

Each layer \(u_iv_i^\top\) is an outer product: a column pattern times a row pattern, the simplest possible "image". The Eckart–Young theorem says no rank-\(k\) matrix is closer to \(A\) than \(A_k\) (in squared error, or in the largest-stretch norm). So truncating the SVD is not just a good compression, it is the optimal one.

Storage drops from \(mn\) numbers to \(k(m+n+1)\). Smooth images with large shapes have quickly falling singular values and compress well. Noise has flat singular values and does not.

4What else the SVD gives you

PCA

Centre the data matrix \(X\) (subtract each column's mean) and take its SVD, \(X=U\Sigma V^\top\). Then the covariance is \(\frac{1}{n-1}X^\top X=V\frac{\Sigma^2}{n-1}V^\top\). The principal directions are the columns of \(V\), the variances are \(\sigma_i^2/(n-1)\), and the projected coordinates are \(U\Sigma\). This is how PCA is computed in practice.

Pseudo-inverse

Invert what can be inverted: \(A^+=V\Sigma^+U^\top\), where \(\Sigma^+\) takes \(1/\sigma_i\) for each nonzero \(\sigma_i\) and leaves zeros alone. \(x=A^+b\) is the least-squares solution, and when there are many it is the one with the smallest \(\|x\|\). It works for any shape or rank.

Rank and conditioning

Rank = number of \(\sigma_i\) above a tolerance. Condition number \(\kappa=\sigma_1/\sigma_r\): the ratio of the longest to the shortest axis of the ellipse. A nearly flat ellipse means a nearly singular matrix, and errors get amplified by \(\kappa\).

Going deeper: Eckart–Young, norms, and the link to eigenvalues

Matrix norms from the SVD. The spectral norm (largest stretch) is \(\|A\|_2=\sigma_1\). The Frobenius norm (square root of the sum of squared entries) is \(\|A\|_F=\sqrt{\sum_i\sigma_i^2}\), because orthogonal matrices do not change lengths. The nuclear norm \(\|A\|_*=\sum_i\sigma_i\) is to rank what the L1 norm is to the number of nonzero entries. It is the convex surrogate used in matrix completion (the Netflix-prize style recommender problem).

Eckart–Young, spectral version. Let \(B\) have rank \(k\). Its null space has dimension \(n-k\), so it meets the \((k+1)\)-dimensional span of \(v_1,\dots,v_{k+1}\) in some unit vector \(w\). Then \(\|(A-B)w\|=\|Aw\|\ge\sigma_{k+1}\), so \(\|A-B\|_2\ge\sigma_{k+1}=\|A-A_k\|_2\). \(\square\)

Eigenvalues vs singular values. For a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix they coincide. For a symmetric matrix \(\sigma_i=|\lambda_i|\). In general they differ: the shear \(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) has both eigenvalues 1 but singular values \(\approx1.62\) and \(0.62\). Eigenvalues describe repeated application (\(A^k\)); singular values describe a single application (how much one step stretches). That is why exploding gradients are about the largest singular value of the weight matrix, while long-run stability is about its eigenvalues.

5Where the SVD shows up in ML

LoRA

Fine-tuning updates a weight matrix by \(\Delta W=BA\) of rank \(r\ll d\). The bet is that useful updates have quickly decaying singular values. See the LLM adaptation map.

Latent semantic analysis

Take the SVD of a word–document count matrix and keep \(k\approx300\) components. Words used in similar documents land close together: an early form of word embeddings.

Recommenders

A user–item rating matrix is approximated as \(UV^\top\) with a few hidden factors per user and per item, the "tastes" from the opening example.

Whitening and initialisation

Orthogonal initialisation sets weight matrices to have all \(\sigma_i=1\), so signals neither explode nor vanish at the start of training.

Spectral normalisation

Dividing a layer's weights by \(\sigma_1\) caps how much the layer can stretch its input, which stabilises GAN training.

Compression

Large fully connected layers can be replaced by two thin ones from a truncated SVD, trading a little accuracy for big savings in memory.

6The same thing in Python (NumPy)

import numpy as np
A = np.array([[3., 0.], [4., 5.]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)             # s = [6.708, 2.236]
np.allclose(U @ np.diag(s) @ Vt, A)
img = np.random.rand(48, 48)
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(img, full_matrices=False)
k = 5; img_k = U[:, :k] @ np.diag(s[:k]) @ Vt[:k]       # best rank-k approximation
err = np.sqrt((s[k:]**2).sum())                  # == np.linalg.norm(img - img_k)
np.linalg.pinv(A)                       # pseudo-inverse V Σ⁺ Uᵀ
s[0] / s[-1], np.linalg.cond(A)               # condition number
from sklearn.utils.extmath import randomized_svd     # fast top-k SVD for big matrices
