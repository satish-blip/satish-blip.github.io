Embeddings: bag of words to BERT

After tokenisation every word is just an id. An embedding turns that id into a list of numbers, and the whole history of NLP is a search for better lists: ones where similar meanings get similar numbers. This page walks the path from counting words, through word2vec, to vectors that change with context.

What is an embedding, in plain words?

The problem

To a computer, "cat" and "kitten" are just two different ids, exactly as different as "cat" and "carburettor". A model that learns something about cats learns nothing about kittens. We want each word to be a point in space where similar words sit close together, and we want the space learned automatically from raw text, since nobody can hand-label the meaning of a million words.

The idea

An embedding is a list of numbers that stands for a word (or a sentence), chosen so that words with similar meanings get similar lists. Early methods counted words (one-hot, bag of words, TF-IDF); word2vec learned one vector per word by predicting its neighbours; contextual models such as BERT give a word a different vector in every sentence.

How it works

  1. Give every word a vector (random at first).
  2. Train on a simple job, such as predicting nearby words.
  3. Words used in similar contexts drift together; closeness is measured by cosine similarity.
  4. Contextual models then adjust each vector using the rest of the sentence.

An everyday picture

A map of a city: cafés cluster near other cafés, banks near banks. A word’s position tells you what neighbourhood of meaning it lives in.

Good at
  • Turns meaning into geometry you can compute with
  • Learned from raw text, no labels needed
  • Feeds every later NLP model
Watch out for
  • Static vectors blur words with two meanings ("bank")
  • Learns the biases present in its training text
  • Toy corpora give fragile analogies

1One-hot: every word is equally different

The simplest vector for word number \(k\) in a vocabulary of size \(V\) is all zeros with a single 1 in position \(k\). It is exact and needs no training, but:

  • it is as long as the vocabulary (tens of thousands of numbers, almost all zero);
  • every pair of different words has cosine similarity 0 and distance \(\sqrt2\): "cat" is exactly as far from "kitten" as from "democracy".

Every method below tries to fix the second problem: put related words near each other.

2Bag of words and TF-IDF: vectors for documents

Add up the one-hot vectors of a document's words and you get its bag of words: word counts, order thrown away. Edit the documents (one per line) and search them.

Document × word matrix

Document similarity (cosine)

$$\text{tf-idf}(w,d)=\underbrace{\frac{\text{count}(w,d)}{|d|}}_{\text{term frequency}}\times\underbrace{\Big(\log\frac{1+N}{1+\text{df}(w)}+1\Big)}_{\text{inverse document frequency}}$$

TF-IDF down-weights words that appear in every document ("the", "a") and boosts words that are distinctive. It is still the backbone of classic search engines (BM25 is a refinement). Its weakness: "cat" and "kitten" are still unrelated columns, and "bank" means one thing in every document.

Analogy

On a toy corpus analogies are fragile; the word2vec playground does this with 100,000 real Google News vectors.

The idea: "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth, 1957). For each word in the text, the model is asked to tell its real neighbours (within 2 positions) from random words. To do that well, words that appear in similar contexts must get similar vectors.

$$\mathcal L=-\log\sigma(u_{o}^\top v_{c})-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\sigma(-u_{k}^\top v_{c})$$

\(v_c\): centre word's vector, \(u_o\): a real context word, \(u_k\): \(K=5\) random "negative" words drawn by frequency\(^{0.75}\).

Algorithm: word2vec skip-gram with negative sampling
  1. input a long text, window size \(c\), dimension \(d\), \(k\) negatives, learning rate \(\eta\)
  2. give every word two random vectors: \(v_w\) (as centre word) and \(u_w\) (as context word)
  3. for each position \(t\) in the text do
  4. for each context word \(o\) within \(c\) words of the centre \(w_t\) do
  5. draw \(k\) random "negative" words \(n_1..n_k\) usually unrelated to w
  6. \(\mathcal L\leftarrow-\log\sigma(u_o^\top v_{w_t})-\sum_j\log\sigma(-u_{n_j}^\top v_{w_t})\)
  7. nudge \(v_{w_t}\) and \(u_o\) together; push \(u_{n_j}\) and \(v_{w_t}\) apart one SGD step
  8. return the vectors \(v_w\) as the embeddings
Going deeper: what the vectors are secretly learning

The loss is logistic regression: "is this (centre, context) pair real or random?". Its gradients are short and intuitive. For the true pair, \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial v=-(1-\sigma(u_o^\top v))\,u_o\), so \(v\) moves toward \(u_o\), more strongly the less sure the model was. For each negative, \(v\) moves away from \(u_{n}\) by \(\sigma(u_n^\top v)\,u_n\).

Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed that at the optimum, \(u_o^\top v_w=\operatorname{PMI}(w,o)-\log k\), where \(\operatorname{PMI}(w,o)=\log\frac{P(w,o)}{P(w)P(o)}\) measures how much more often the two words co-occur than chance. So skip-gram is implicitly factorising a word–context PMI matrix, much like an SVD. Two words get similar vectors when they have similar PMI rows, that is, when they appear in similar contexts: the distributional hypothesis, "you shall know a word by the company it keeps".

4Context changes meaning: from static to contextual

Grey dots: the trained word vectors from section 3. Black ring: the single static vector for "bank". Coloured stars: "bank" after mixing in its sentence’s other words with one round of attention.

word2vec gives "bank" one vector, a blend of its money sense and its river sense. A contextual model produces a different vector for every occurrence, computed from the whole sentence.

Here "bank" keeps half of its static vector and takes the other half from a weighted average of the other content words in its sentence, weighted by similarity: \(c_i=\tfrac12 v_i+\tfrac12\sum_{j\ne i}\operatorname{softmax}_j(v_i^\top v_j/\tau)\,v_j\). The half it keeps is a residual connection. This is one untrained attention step, but it already pulls "bank" towards money words or river words depending on the sentence.

ELMo (2018) did this with a two-layer bidirectional LSTM; BERT (2018) with 12 to 24 layers of self-attention trained to fill in masked words. Each layer refines every token's vector using all the others.
