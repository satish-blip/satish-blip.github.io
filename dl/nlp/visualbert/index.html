VisualBERT: one transformer for words and pictures

In part 1 an image became a list of visual tokens. VisualBERT's idea is almost cheeky in its simplicity: put those visual tokens right after the words of a caption, and feed the whole thing to an ordinary BERT. Self-attention, which already links words to words, now links words to parts of the picture too. This page builds the input, shows the attention, and walks through how it is trained.

What is VisualBERT, in plain words?

The problem

"What is the dog playing with?" To answer, a model must find the word "dog" in the question, find the dog in the picture, look next to it, and name what it sees. A text model has no picture; an image classifier has no question. Training a separate model for every such task, from scratch, needs huge labelled datasets. We want one pre-trained model that already knows how words and image regions relate, and that can be adapted to many vision-and-language tasks with a small amount of extra training, the way BERT is for text.

The idea

VisualBERT (Li et al., 2019) is a single-stream multimodal transformer. The input is the caption's word tokens followed by the image's region features, each tagged with a segment embedding that says "text" or "image". One BERT processes them together, so every word can attend to every region. It is pre-trained on image captions with two jobs: fill in masked words using the picture, and decide whether a caption matches the picture.

How it works

  1. Detect objects in the image; each box becomes a visual token.
  2. Tokenise the caption as in BERT and add [CLS] and [SEP].
  3. Add segment (and position) embeddings, concatenate, and run BERT.
  4. Pre-train on captions, then fine-tune on a task such as VQA.

An everyday picture

1One sequence for words and regions

BERT's input embedding for each word is the sum of three vectors: the token's own embedding, a segment embedding, and a position embedding. VisualBERT gives each image region the same three-part treatment, so regions can sit in the same sequence as words. Pick a scene to see its input.

Text tokens are exactly as in BERT: \(e_i=E_{\text{tok}}[w_i]+E_{\text{seg}}[A]+E_{\text{pos}}[i]\).

Visual tokens are built from the detector's feature \(f_j\) (2,048 numbers for box \(j\)):

$$v_j=W_vf_j+E_{\text{seg}}[B]+E_{\text{pos}}[\text{aligned}(j)]$$

\(W_v\) is a learned matrix that maps the 2,048 detector numbers to BERT's width (768). The segment vector \(E_{\text{seg}}[B]\) marks "this is image". The position term is used only when a dataset says which words describe which box (Flickr30K Entities does); the region then gets the position embedding of its words, and otherwise none.

The input is then simply \(\big[\,[\text{CLS}],e_1,\dots,e_n,[\text{SEP}],v_1,\dots,v_m\,\big]\).

Purple: segment A (text). Orange: segment B (image). "pos of …" marks a region whose box is aligned with a word in the caption.

2Attention across modalities

Because words and regions share one sequence, the attention matrix has four blocks: word to word (ordinary BERT), word to region, region to word, and region to region. Tap a word or region (a row label) to see where it looks, and its weights painted on the picture.

The weights here are set by hand from simple rules (a word attends to regions it names, function words attend to [CLS] and [SEP], neighbours attend to each other) so you can see the pattern a trained head converges to. The VisualBERT paper found that some heads in the trained model do exactly this: without ever being told which box goes with which word, they put most of their weight on the right box, and the accuracy of this implicit grounding improves in later layers.

3Pre-training: learning from captioned images

VisualBERT is pre-trained on COCO, about 100,000 images with five captions each, using two self-supervised jobs. No answers, boxes-to-words links or any other labels are needed: just images and their captions.

Job 1: masked words, with the image

Tap a word in the caption to mask it. The model predicts it from the other words and the image regions (the regions are never masked). The scene is the one picked on the right.

Job 2: does the caption match the image?

Job 1 is BERT's masked language modelling with one change: the image is visible. In "a dog plays with a red [MASK]", text alone suggests ball, car, apple; the image settles it. This pushes the model to use the regions. Job 2 (sentence–image prediction) takes the output above [CLS] and predicts whether the caption describes this image; half the time it is swapped for a caption of a different image. Prediction scores in this demo come from a toy scoring rule, not from a trained network.

Algorithm: VisualBERT pre-training step
  1. input image regions \(f_1..f_m\) (from the detector), a caption \(w_1..w_n\) that is the true one with probability \(\tfrac12\)
  2. text tokens: \(e_i\leftarrow E_{\text{tok}}[w_i]+E_{\text{seg}}[A]+E_{\text{pos}}[i]\)
  3. visual tokens: \(v_j\leftarrow W_vf_j+E_{\text{seg}}[B]\) + aligned position, if known
  4. replace 15% of the words by [MASK] regions are never masked
  5. \(H\leftarrow\operatorname{BERT}([\text{CLS}],e_{1..n},[\text{SEP}],v_{1..m})\) every token attends to every token
  6. \(\mathcal L_{\text{MLM}}\leftarrow-\sum_{\text{masked }i}\log p(w_i\mid H_i)\) the image can help
  7. \(\mathcal L_{\text{SIP}}\leftarrow\) cross-entropy of \(\sigma(u^\top H_{\text{CLS}})\) against "caption is true"
  8. update all weights to reduce \(\mathcal L_{\text{MLM}}+\mathcal L_{\text{SIP}}\)
Going deeper: the three training stages, and the exact objectives

VisualBERT is trained in three stages. (1) Task-agnostic pre-training on COCO captions with the two jobs above, starting from the text-only BERT weights (which matters: the paper reports a clear drop without them). (2) Task-specific pre-training: the same masked-word job on the target task's own images and text (for example VQA questions), to adapt to that domain. (3) Fine-tuning with a task head and the task's labels.

The masked-word loss is the usual cross-entropy over the vocabulary, but conditioned on the regions: \(-\log p(w_i\mid w_{\setminus i},f_{1..m})\). In the paper's sentence–image prediction the text segment holds two captions, one of which describes the image while the other does so only half the time, and the [CLS] output classifies which case it is; the single-caption version above is the same idea in its simplest form. Later models (UNITER, LXMERT) added a third job, masked region modelling: hide some regions and predict their detector class or feature from the words, so learning flows in both directions.

4Fine-tuning: one model, many tasks

After pre-training, each task only changes what goes in the text segment and adds a small output head. The image side always stays the same: region tokens with segment B.

Fusion versus matching. VisualBERT is a fusion model: words and regions interact in every layer, which is good for reasoning ("what is left of the dog?") but means every image–text pair must go through the whole network together. CLIP is a matching model: each side is encoded once on its own, so you can embed a million images ahead of time and search them with a text query in milliseconds, but the two sides never look at each other's details.

Modern assistants combine both lessons: a CLIP-style ViT turns the image into patch tokens, and a large language model reads them as if they were extra words, which is VisualBERT's single-stream idea at a much larger scale.

Next: part 3, ViLBERT, keeps the two modalities in separate streams and joins them with co-attention layers.

Going deeper: CLIP's contrastive loss in one line

Take a batch of \(B\) image–caption pairs. Encode them to unit vectors \(u_i\) (image) and \(t_i\) (text), and form the \(B\times B\) matrix of similarities \(s_{ij}=u_i^\top t_j/\tau\) with a learned temperature \(\tau\). The diagonal holds the true pairs. The loss is a cross-entropy in both directions:

$$\mathcal L=-\frac1{2B}\sum_i\Big[\log\frac{e^{s_{ii}}}{\sum_je^{s_{ij}}}+\log\frac{e^{s_{ii}}}{\sum_je^{s_{ji}}}\Big]$$

Every other caption in the batch is a negative example, so large batches (32,768 in CLIP) make the task harder and the embedding sharper. The result is a multimodal embedding space where "a photo of a dog" lands next to photos of dogs, which is what makes zero-shot classification and text-to-image search possible.

6The same thing in Python

import torch
from transformers import BertTokenizer, VisualBertForQuestionAnswering
tok   = BertTokenizer.from_pretrained("bert-base-uncased")
model = VisualBertForQuestionAnswering.from_pretrained("uclanlp/visualbert-vqa")

inputs = tok("What is the dog playing with?", return_tensors="pt")   # segment A: the question
regions = torch.randn(1, 36, 2048)    # 36 boxes x 2048 numbers: in practice from a Faster R-CNN
inputs.update({
    "visual_embeds": regions,
    "visual_token_type_ids": torch.ones(1, 36, dtype=torch.long),   # segment B
    "visual_attention_mask": torch.ones(1, 36),
})
out = model(**inputs)
answer_id = out.logits.argmax(-1)     # one of the frequent VQA answers
