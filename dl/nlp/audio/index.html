Audio & speech: sound as numbers

A microphone produces one number tens of thousands of times a second. Speech recognition turns that stream into words; speech synthesis turns words back into it. This page follows the pipeline: sampling and why it has a speed limit, the spectrogram that turns sound into a picture, the mel scale that bends it to match our ears, the CTC trick that lets a network output text without knowing where each letter starts, and the models (Whisper, wav2vec 2.0, modern text-to-speech) built on top. Every signal here is generated in your browser, and you can listen to it.

How do networks hear, in plain words?

The problem

One second of audio at 16 kHz is 16,000 numbers, and the word “hello” can take 0.3 s or 0.8 s depending on who says it. The information that matters (pitch, vowels, consonants) is in how fast the air vibrates, not in individual samples. And the transcript is far shorter than the audio, with no marks saying which samples belong to which letter.

The idea

Describe sound by its frequencies over time: a spectrogram, computed with the short-time Fourier transform, then squeezed onto the mel scale that matches human hearing. Treat it like an image or a sequence of tokens. For the length mismatch, let the network output one symbol per frame, including a “blank”, and let CTC sum over every way those frames could spell the transcript; or use an encoder–decoder that writes the text token by token, as Whisper does.

How it works

  1. Sample the waveform (16 kHz is standard for speech).
  2. Cut it into short overlapping frames (25 ms, every 10 ms) and take each frame's spectrum.
  3. Pool the spectrum into 80 mel bands and take the log: a log-mel spectrogram.
  4. Feed it to a CNN or transformer encoder.
  5. Decode text with CTC or an attention decoder.

An everyday picture

Sheet music. A recording is a wobbly line, but a musician writes it as which notes sound when: frequency against time. That is a spectrogram. The piano keyboard is spaced so each octave gets the same number of keys, although an octave near the top covers far more hertz than one near the bottom; the mel scale does the same for our ears.

Good at
  • Spectrograms make sound look like images, so vision models work
  • Self-supervised pre-training needs only raw audio
  • Large models handle accents, noise and many languages
Watch out for
  • Window length trades time detail against frequency detail
  • Sampling too slowly folds high sounds into false low ones
  • Hallucinated words in silence; voice cloning misuse

1Sampling and aliasing

A pure tone, and the samples a recorder takes of it. Push the tone above half the sample rate and the samples trace a different, lower tone: the one you would hear back.

Sound is a pressure wave. A microphone turns it into a voltage, and an analogue-to-digital converter measures that voltage \(f_s\) times a second, the sample rate, giving a sequence \(x[n]=x(n/f_s)\). Speech models usually use \(f_s=16\) kHz; music uses 44.1 or 48 kHz.

How fast must we sample? The Nyquist–Shannon theorem says a signal containing no frequencies above \(B\) is perfectly recoverable from its samples if \(f_s>2B\). Half the sample rate is the Nyquist frequency. A tone above it is not lost, it is disguised: its samples are identical to those of a lower tone, the alias,

$$f_{\text{alias}}=\big|\,f-f_s\cdot\mathrm{round}(f/f_s)\,\big| .$$

In the lab at 8 kHz, a 5 kHz tone produces exactly the samples of a 3 kHz tone, and that is what you hear back. Recorders therefore apply a low-pass filter before sampling. Speech fits comfortably in 16 kHz: the important energy of vowels and most consonants is below 8 kHz, which is also why old telephone audio at 8 kHz sounds muffled, with “s” and “f” hard to tell apart.

Each sample is stored with a fixed number of bits: 16-bit audio has 65,536 levels. Networks usually take samples as floats in \([-1,1]\).

Adapted from: Shannon, Communication in the presence of noise (1949); Smith, The Scientist and Engineer's Guide to Digital Signal Processing, ch. 3.

2The spectrogram and the time–frequency trade-off

One second of audio. Change the window length and watch the picture trade sharp timing for sharp pitch. Low frequencies are at the bottom; brighter means louder.

0 stime →1 s

The Fourier transform writes a signal as a sum of sine waves and tells us how much of each frequency it contains. Speech changes every few milliseconds, so we take it over short windows: the short-time Fourier transform. Slide a window \(w\) of \(N\) samples along the signal in hops of \(H\), and for each frame \(m\) compute

$$X[m,k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n+mH]\,w[n]\,e^{-2\pi i\,kn/N},\qquad k=0,\dots,\tfrac N2 .$$

Bin \(k\) corresponds to frequency \(k f_s/N\). The spectrogram is \(|X[m,k]|^2\), usually in decibels, drawn as an image. The window (here a Hann window, \(w[n]=\sin^2(\pi n/N)\)) tapers each frame so its edges do not create false frequencies.

The window length cannot be chosen to be good at everything:

$$\Delta f=\frac{f_s}{N},\qquad \Delta t\approx\frac{N}{f_s},\qquad \Delta f\cdot\Delta t\approx 1 .$$

A long window separates the two close tones (440 and 470 Hz) into two lines, but smears the clicks across many frames; a short window pins the clicks but blurs the tones into one band. This is the uncertainty principle of signal processing. Speech systems settle on about 25 ms windows (400 samples at 16 kHz) with a 10 ms hop: long enough to resolve the harmonics of a voice, short enough to follow consonants.

Algorithm: spectrogram by STFT
  1. input samples \(x\), window length \(N\), hop \(H\)
  2. for \(m=0,1,\dots\) while \(mH+N\le\) length of \(x\)
  3. frame \(\leftarrow x[mH : mH+N]\cdot w\) Hann taper
  4. \(X[m,:]\leftarrow\text{FFT}(\text{frame})\), keep bins \(0..N/2\)
  5. column \(m\) of the image \(\leftarrow 10\log_{10}(|X[m,:]|^2+\epsilon)\)
  6. return the image: frequency up, time across

Adapted from: Allen and Rabiner, A unified approach to short-time Fourier analysis (1977); Müller, Fundamentals of Music Processing (2015), ch. 2.

3Mel filterbanks and MFCCs

Triangular filters spaced evenly on the mel scale, so they crowd together at low frequencies like our hearing does. Below: the same one-second scene as a log-mel spectrogram, and its MFCCs.

log-mel spectrogram(bands up, time across)
first 13 MFCCs(coefficient up, time across)

We hear pitch roughly logarithmically: 100 → 200 Hz sounds like the same step as 1,000 → 2,000 Hz. The mel scale captures this:

$$m(f)=2595\,\log_{10}\!\Big(1+\frac{f}{700}\Big).$$

It is nearly linear below about 1 kHz and logarithmic above. A mel filterbank places \(M\) triangular filters with centres evenly spaced in mel, so each filter is narrow at low frequencies and wide at high ones, and pools the power spectrum with them: \(S[m,j]=\sum_k H_j[k]\,|X[m,k]|^2\). Taking \(\log S\) compresses the huge dynamic range of sound, as our ears do. The result, the log-mel spectrogram with \(M=80\) bands, is the input to Whisper and most modern speech models: it keeps what matters for speech and shrinks 201 frequency bins to 80.

MFCCs go one step further. A discrete cosine transform along the band axis,

$$c[m,q]=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\log S[m,j]\,\cos\!\Big(\frac{\pi q}{M}\big(j-\tfrac12\big)\Big),$$

keeps the first 13 coefficients, which describe the smooth shape of the spectrum (the vocal tract's resonances, which distinguish vowels) and discard the fine ripple (the pitch harmonics). The DCT also decorrelates the features, which suited the Gaussian mixture models of classical speech recognition. Deep networks learn their own decorrelation, so today log-mel features are preferred and MFCCs survive mostly in small and classical systems.

Adapted from: Stevens, Volkmann and Newman, the mel scale (1937); Davis and Mermelstein, Comparison of parametric representations for monosyllabic word recognition (1980); O'Shaughnessy's mel formula as used in librosa (htk=True).

4CTC: text from frames without alignments

A CTC model outputs one symbol per frame, with “_” meaning blank. Type a frame sequence and see it decode. Below, a toy network's probabilities for 8 frames: compare the single best path with the total probability of the word.

An acoustic encoder produces a vector every 20 ms or so: a 2-second clip gives about 100 frames for a transcript of maybe 15 characters. Nobody has marked which frames belong to which letter. Connectionist temporal classification (Graves et al., 2006) solves this without any alignment. Add a blank symbol “_” to the vocabulary and let the network output a distribution over symbols at every frame. A frame-level path \(\pi\) maps to text by a collapse function \(\mathcal B\):

  1. merge consecutive repeats;
  2. remove blanks.

So hh_ee_l_ll_ooo → h_e_l_l_o → hello. The blank does two jobs: it fills frames between letters, and it separates genuine double letters (“ll” needs a blank between the two l's, or the repeats merge). The probability of a transcript \(y\) is the sum over every path that collapses to it, assuming frames are conditionally independent given the audio:

$$P(y\mid x)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal B^{-1}(y)}\ \prod_{t=1}^{T}p_t(\pi_t\mid x),$$ $$\mathcal L_{\text{CTC}}=-\log P(y\mid x).$$

There are exponentially many paths, but the forward algorithm, a dynamic programme over the target with blanks inserted (_c_a_t_), sums them in \(O(T\cdot|y|)\). The lab computes it: the best single path usually carries only a small share of the total probability, which is why training on the sum is better than on one guessed alignment. At test time, greedy decoding takes the most likely symbol per frame and collapses; beam search, often with a language model, does better.

Algorithm: CTC forward pass
  1. input per-frame probabilities \(p_t(\cdot)\), target \(y\)
  2. \(y'\leftarrow\) \(y\) with blanks around every symbol (length \(2|y|+1\))
  3. \(\alpha_1(1)\leftarrow p_1(\_)\), \(\alpha_1(2)\leftarrow p_1(y'_2)\)
  4. for \(t=2..T\), each position \(s\): \(\alpha_t(s)\leftarrow p_t(y'_s)\,[\alpha_{t-1}(s)+\alpha_{t-1}(s-1)+\alpha_{t-1}(s-2)]\)
  5. the \(s-2\) jump only if \(y'_s\) is not blank and \(y'_s\ne y'_{s-2}\)
  6. return \(P(y\mid x)=\alpha_T(\text{last})+\alpha_T(\text{last}-1)\)

Adapted from: Graves et al., Connectionist Temporal Classification (ICML 2006); Hannun, Sequence Modeling with CTC (Distill, 2017).

5Whisper, wav2vec 2.0 and text-to-speech

wav2vec 2.0: self-supervised speech

A CNN turns raw 16 kHz audio into a latent vector every 20 ms. Spans of these are masked, a transformer reads the rest, and for each masked frame it must pick the true (quantised) latent out of distractors: a contrastive task, the speech version of the ideas in self-supervised learning. Pre-trained on 53,000 hours of unlabelled audio and fine-tuned with a CTC head, wav2vec 2.0 reached usable recognition with just 10 minutes of labelled speech. HuBERT and WavLM refine the target (predict cluster labels instead).

Whisper: weak supervision at scale

Whisper takes the opposite route: 680,000 hours of audio paired with transcripts found on the web, noisy but enormous. The model is a plain encoder–decoder transformer. The encoder reads 30 s of 80-band log-mel spectrogram (through two convolution layers that halve the frame rate); the decoder writes text tokens, steered by special tokens that choose the task: language, transcribe or translate to English, with or without timestamps. One model handles about 100 languages and is robust to noise and accents. Its weakness is the decoder's: like any language model, it can hallucinate fluent text, especially over silence or music.

Text-to-speech: the pipeline in reverse

Classic neural TTS runs in two stages. An acoustic model (Tacotron 2, FastSpeech 2) turns characters or phonemes into a mel spectrogram, predicting how long each sound lasts and its pitch. A vocoder then turns the mel spectrogram into a waveform; since the log-mel throws away phase, this is a generation problem. WaveNet did it sample by sample (slow); HiFi-GAN does it with a GAN in one fast pass. End-to-end models (VITS) train both together.

Newer systems treat speech like text. A neural audio codec (SoundStream, EnCodec) compresses audio into discrete tokens at a few dozen per second; a language model then predicts those tokens from text and a few seconds of a speaker's voice (VALL-E), and the codec decodes them. The same tokens power music generation and speech-to-speech assistants, and the same risk follows: convincing voice cloning from seconds of audio.

6The same thing in Python

Features with librosa, speech recognition with Whisper and wav2vec 2.0 through Hugging Face Transformers, and a CTC loss in PyTorch.

# pip install librosa soundfile torch transformers
import numpy as np, librosa, torch

# ---- 1. Waveform -> spectrogram -> log-mel -> MFCC ----
y, sr = librosa.load(librosa.ex("libri1"), sr=16000)          # resample to 16 kHz
S = np.abs(librosa.stft(y, n_fft=400, hop_length=160, window="hann")) ** 2   # 25 ms windows, 10 ms hop
mel = librosa.feature.melspectrogram(S=S, sr=sr, n_mels=80)  # 80 mel bands
log_mel = librosa.power_to_db(mel)                           # (80, frames)
mfcc = librosa.feature.mfcc(S=log_mel, n_mfcc=13)            # (13, frames)
print(S.shape, log_mel.shape, mfcc.shape)

# ---- 2. Speech recognition with Whisper (encoder-decoder) ----
from transformers import pipeline
asr = pipeline("automatic-speech-recognition", model="openai/whisper-small")
print(asr({"raw": y, "sampling_rate": sr}, return_timestamps=True)["text"])

# ---- 3. wav2vec 2.0 with a CTC head: greedy decoding ----
from transformers import Wav2Vec2Processor, Wav2Vec2ForCTC
proc = Wav2Vec2Processor.from_pretrained("facebook/wav2vec2-base-960h")
w2v = Wav2Vec2ForCTC.from_pretrained("facebook/wav2vec2-base-960h")
with torch.no_grad():
    logits = w2v(proc(y, sampling_rate=sr, return_tensors="pt").input_values).logits   # (1, frames, vocab)
ids = logits.argmax(-1)                                       # best symbol per frame
print(proc.batch_decode(ids)[0])                              # merges repeats and removes blanks

# ---- 4. The CTC loss itself ----
T, C = 50, 28                                                 # frames, vocabulary (blank = 0)
log_probs = torch.randn(T, 1, C).log_softmax(-1)              # (time, batch, classes)
target = torch.tensor([[3, 1, 20]])                           # "c a t"
loss = torch.nn.CTCLoss(blank=0)(log_probs, target, torch.tensor([T]), torch.tensor([3]))
print("CTC loss:", loss.item())
