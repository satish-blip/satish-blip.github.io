      Why the score splits into bars

      For unit vectors, cos(t, v) = [cos(v, king) − cos(v, man) + cos(v, woman)] / ‖t‖. So a candidate wins by being like the words you added and unlike the words you subtracted. The coloured bars in "Nearby options" are exactly those three terms, which is why queen beats monarch: both are very like king, but queen is much more like woman.

      3CosAdd and 3CosMul

      3CosAdd is the plain sum above (Mikolov et al., 2013). 3CosMul (Levy & Goldberg, 2014) multiplies the similarities instead, ∏ sim(v, added) / (∏ sim(v, subtracted) + ε) with sim = (cos+1)/2, so one huge similarity cannot drown out the others. It often fixes near misses.

      Things to notice

      • Close calls are common. Paris − France + Italy gives Milan and Rome almost tied. The method finds "a big Italian city", and Rome only sometimes wins.
      • Vectors learn our biases. Try doctor − man + woman or programmer − man + woman. The model reflects patterns in 2013 news text, not facts about people.
      • One vector per word. apple mixes fruit and company, so its neighbours are a blend.

      About the data

      The vectors are the 100,000 most frequent single words (letters only) from Google's pretrained word2vec model (GoogleNews-vectors-negative300, skip-gram with negative sampling). To fit a static web page each number is stored in 4 bits with one scale per word, which shrinks 120 MB to 15 MB. On the standard Google analogy test this scores 76.6% versus 76.8% for the full-precision vectors. Everything runs in your browser; nothing you type is sent anywhere.

      Find the closest word

      Score every one of the 100,000 words by cosine similarity, cos(t, v) = t·v / (‖t‖‖v‖), and rank them.
