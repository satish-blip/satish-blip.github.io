Anomaly detection: finding the points that do not fit

A fraudulent card payment, a failing bearing, a mistyped claim amount, an intrusion on a network. Anomalies are rare, varied and usually unlabelled, so we cannot simply train a classifier. Instead we learn what normal looks like and flag what is far from it. This page builds four detectors that each define “far” differently and runs them side by side.

What is anomaly detection, in plain words?

The problem

An anomaly (or outlier) is an observation that is unlikely under the process that made most of the data. They are rare (often well under 1%), each kind is different, and new kinds appear all the time. So there are almost never enough labelled examples to learn them directly, and a classifier trained on last year’s frauds misses this year’s.

The idea

Model normal instead, then score every point by how badly it fits. Each detector on this page has its own notion of fitting badly:

  1. Far from the centre, measured in standard deviations (Gaussian, Mahalanobis).
  2. Easy to isolate with a few random cuts (isolation forest).
  3. Outside a boundary drawn around most of the data (one-class SVM).
  4. Less crowded than its neighbours (local outlier factor).

Each gives a score; a threshold, often set by the expected share of anomalies (the contamination), turns scores into flags for a person to check.

An everyday picture

A bank teller who has seen thousands of customers does not need a list of every possible fraud. They notice the customer who withdraws ten times their usual amount, in a city they have never visited, at 3 a.m. They have learned normal, and that is enough to spot strange.

Good at
  • Fraud and intrusion screening
  • Machine and sensor monitoring
  • Data-quality checks before modelling
  • Finding new, unseen kinds of problem
Watch out for
  • “Unusual” is not the same as “bad”
  • Many outliers together can hide each other
  • Scaling and feature choice decide what counts as far
  • Thresholds need business costs, not defaults

1Distance from normal: z-scores, Mahalanobis and masking

Grey dots are normal, red-ringed dots are the true anomalies (the detector never sees which is which). The ellipse is the flagging threshold: everything outside is flagged. Pink shading is the flagged region.

For one feature the classic rule flags a value whose z-score \(|x-\mu|/\sigma\) exceeds 3. For several correlated features, distance must follow the shape of the data cloud, which is what the Mahalanobis distance does:

$$D_M(x)=\sqrt{(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)}.$$

It is the ordinary distance after rotating and rescaling the cloud into a round one, so a point can be unusual because of an odd combination of values (tall and light) even when each value alone is normal. If normal data are Gaussian in \(d\) dimensions, \(D_M^2\) follows a \(\chi^2_d\) distribution, which turns the threshold into a probability.

The catch is that \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) are estimated from data that contain the anomalies. Choose Cluster of anomalies: the 40 clustered outliers drag the mean towards them and stretch the covariance in their direction, so most of them no longer look far (set the share flagged to about 15%, their true share). This is masking. A robust fit estimates the centre and shape from the most central points only (the idea behind the minimum covariance determinant, EllipticEnvelope in scikit-learn); switch to it and the cluster stands out again.

Gaussian methods are fast and explainable, but they assume one roughly elliptical cloud. On the moons they flag the wrong places, which motivates the detectors below.

Algorithm: Mahalanobis outlier detection (robust)
  1. input points, share to flag \(c\)
  2. estimate \(\mu,\Sigma\) from all points
  3. repeat a few times robust version only
  4. keep the 75% of points with the smallest \(D_M\); re-estimate \(\mu,\Sigma\) from them
  5. score every point by \(D_M(x)\)
  6. threshold \(\tau\leftarrow\) the \((1-c)\) quantile of the scores
  7. return points with \(D_M(x)>\tau\)

2Isolation forest: anomalies are easy to cut off

Shading: the forest’s anomaly score (pink = above the flagging threshold). Tap any point to watch one random tree isolate it: each line is a random cut, and the count is how many cuts it took.

Liu, Ting and Zhou (2008) turned the problem around: instead of describing normal points, measure how easy each point is to isolate. Pick a random feature and a random cut between its minimum and maximum; keep cutting the part that contains the point until it is alone. A point in a dense crowd needs many cuts; a lone point far away is usually separated in two or three. Tap a far point and then a central one to see the difference.

One tree is noisy, so build many, each on a small random subsample of \(\psi\) points (256 by default), and average the path length \(h(x)\). The isolation forest score compares it with the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a random binary tree of \(\psi\) points:

$$s(x)=2^{-\,E[h(x)]/c(\psi)},\qquad c(\psi)=2H_{\psi-1}-\frac{2(\psi-1)}{\psi},\quad H_i\approx\ln i+0.5772.$$

\(s\) near 1 means much shorter paths than usual (anomalous); around 0.5 or below means ordinary. Small subsamples are a feature, not a compromise: fewer normal points crowd around the anomalies, which reduces masking, and training is very fast, linear in \(n\). It needs no distance at all, so it works with mixed feature scales and in high dimensions, which is why it is often the first detector people try.

Algorithm: isolation forest
  1. input points, number of trees \(t\), subsample size \(\psi\)
  2. for each tree: draw \(\psi\) points at random
  3. grow: pick a random feature \(q\) and a random cut \(p\) between its min and max
  4. split the points into \(x_q<p\) and \(x_q\ge p\); grow each side until one point is left or depth \(\lceil\log_2\psi\rceil\)
  5. for a point \(x\): \(h(x)\) = number of cuts to reach its leaf (+ \(c(\text{leaf size})\))
  6. return \(s(x)=2^{-\bar h(x)/c(\psi)}\) near 1 = anomaly

3One-class SVM: a boundary around most of the data

The dark curve is where the decision function is zero; pink is outside. Ringed points are support vectors, the only points that shape the boundary.

Schölkopf et al. (2001) adapted the support vector machine to data with one class only. Map the points into the feature space of an RBF kernel \(K(x,x')=e^{-\gamma\lVert x-x'\rVert^2}\) and find a hyperplane that separates them from the origin with the largest margin, allowing a few points on the wrong side:

$$\min_{w,\xi,\rho}\ \tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2+\frac{1}{\nu n}\sum_i\xi_i-\rho\quad\text{s.t.}\quad w^\top\phi(x_i)\ge\rho-\xi_i,\ \ \xi_i\ge0 .$$

The dual is a small quadratic programme, \(\min_\alpha\tfrac12\sum_{ij}\alpha_i\alpha_jK(x_i,x_j)\) with \(0\le\alpha_i\le\frac{1}{\nu n}\) and \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\), solved here by SMO as on the SVM page. The decision function is \(f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_iK(x_i,x)-\rho\): positive inside, negative outside.

The parameter \(\nu\in(0,1]\) has a clean meaning: it is an upper bound on the fraction of training points left outside and a lower bound on the fraction of support vectors. \(\gamma\) sets the reach of each point, as in the SVM: small \(\gamma\) gives one smooth blob, large \(\gamma\) wraps every little group, eventually every point. Watch both stats as you move the sliders.

Algorithm: one-class SVM by SMO
  1. input points, \(\nu\), kernel \(K\)
  2. start with a feasible \(\alpha\): \(\lfloor\nu n\rfloor\) entries at \(1/\nu n\), the rest 0, \(\sum\alpha_i=1\)
  3. repeat
  4. gradient \(G_i=\sum_j\alpha_jK_{ij}\)
  5. pick \(i\) with \(\alpha_i>0\) and largest \(G_i\), \(j\) with \(\alpha_j<1/\nu n\) and smallest \(G_j\)
  6. move weight from \(i\) to \(j\) (clipped to the box) keeps \(\sum\alpha=1\)
  7. until \(G_i-G_j\) is tiny
  8. \(\rho\leftarrow G\) at the margin support vectors; return \(f(x)=\sum\alpha_iK(x_i,x)-\rho\)

4Local outlier factor: less crowded than its neighbours

Dot size grows with the LOF score; the most anomalous points are labelled. The mixed-density data has a tight cluster, a loose cluster, and a few points just outside the tight one: far by its standards, normal by the loose one’s.

A global rule (“flag anything more than distance 1 from its neighbours”) cannot be right for both clusters at once. Breunig et al. (2000) compared each point’s density with the densities of its own neighbours. With \(N_k(x)\) the \(k\) nearest neighbours of \(x\):

  • Reachability distance: \(\text{reach}_k(x,o)=\max\{k\text{-dist}(o),\ d(x,o)\}\), which smooths out tiny distances inside a crowd.
  • Local reachability density: \(\text{lrd}_k(x)=1\Big/\tfrac{1}{k}\sum_{o\in N_k(x)}\text{reach}_k(x,o)\).
$$\text{LOF}_k(x)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{o\in N_k(x)}\frac{\text{lrd}_k(o)}{\text{lrd}_k(x)}.$$

LOF ≈ 1: as dense as its neighbours. LOF well above 1: in a much emptier spot than its neighbours, i.e. a local outlier. On the mixed-density data, compare the labelled top scores with what the Mahalanobis and isolation-forest views flag: LOF catches the points hugging the tight cluster that global methods ignore.

\(k\) (typically 10 to 50) sets “local”: too small and scores are noisy, too large and small anomalous groups are treated as a cluster. LOF needs all-pairs neighbours, so it is slower than an isolation forest on big data, and plain LOF scores only the training points (scikit-learn’s novelty=True scores new ones).

Algorithm: local outlier factor
  1. input points, neighbours \(k\)
  2. for each point: find \(N_k(x)\) and \(k\)-dist\((x)\) distance to its k-th neighbour
  3. for each point: \(\text{lrd}(x)=k\big/\sum_{o\in N_k(x)}\max\{k\text{-dist}(o),d(x,o)\}\)
  4. for each point: \(\text{LOF}(x)=\) mean of \(\text{lrd}(o)/\text{lrd}(x)\) over \(o\in N_k(x)\)
  5. return LOF scores ≈1 normal, ≫1 outlier

5Four detectors, side by side

The same data and the same share flagged for every method. Under each map: ROC AUC (how well the scores rank true anomalies above normal points; 1 is perfect, 0.5 is chance) and precision among the flagged points.

No detector wins everywhere; the benchmark study by Campos et al. (2016) found method rankings change from dataset to dataset. Practical advice: scale the features, try an isolation forest first, add LOF if densities vary, and compare on whatever labelled cases you have. For images, text or long sequences, use the same detectors on learned embeddings, or an autoencoder’s reconstruction error.

6Anomaly detection in practice

Outlier or novelty detection?

Outlier detection: the training data already contain some anomalies, and we want to find them (all methods above). Novelty detection: we train on data we trust to be clean and score new data later. The difference matters: a one-class SVM or LOF with novelty=True trained on clean data draws a tighter boundary.

Setting the threshold

Every detector outputs a score; the flag is a threshold. The contamination setting just picks the quantile. Better: estimate the cost of a missed anomaly versus a false alarm and the capacity of the people reviewing alerts (say 50 cases a day), then flag the top 50. That is why precision at k, the share of real anomalies among the top \(k\) scores, is often the metric that matters.

Evaluating with few labels

Collect labels from reviewed alerts and from known incidents. Use ranking metrics (ROC AUC, average precision, precision at k) rather than accuracy, which is meaningless at 1% prevalence (see metrics). Be aware that labels from past alerts are biased towards what the old system flagged.

When you do have labels

If you have enough labelled anomalies of the kinds you care about, a supervised classifier with class weights usually beats unsupervised scores, and the anomaly score can become one of its features. Keep an unsupervised detector running alongside to catch new kinds. In time series, flag points far outside the forecast interval of a forecasting model.

7The same thing in Python

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.covariance import EllipticEnvelope            # robust Gaussian (minimum covariance determinant)
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest
from sklearn.svm import OneClassSVM
from sklearn.neighbors import LocalOutlierFactor
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, average_precision_score

X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X_raw)
det = {
  "robust gaussian": EllipticEnvelope(contamination=0.05, random_state=0),
  "isolation forest": IsolationForest(n_estimators=200, max_samples=256, contamination=0.05, random_state=0),
  "one-class svm":   OneClassSVM(kernel="rbf", nu=0.05, gamma="scale"),
}
for name, m in det.items():
    m.fit(X)
    score = -m.decision_function(X)                               # higher = more anomalous
    print(name, roc_auc_score(y_anom, score), average_precision_score(y_anom, score))

lof = LocalOutlierFactor(n_neighbors=20, contamination=0.05)
flags = lof.fit_predict(X)                                        # -1 = outlier
score = -lof.negative_outlier_factor_
