Recommender systems: people like you also liked

Every feed, shop and streaming service answers the same question millions of times a second: of everything we could show this person, what should go first? This page builds the answer up from the simplest idea that works, finding similar users, to learnt embeddings of users and items, the two-stage retrieve-then-rank systems used at scale, the metrics that judge a ranked list, and the re-ranking that keeps it from being ten copies of the same thing.

What is a recommender, in plain words?

The problem

A catalogue has thousands or millions of items; each person has seen or rated a tiny fraction of them. The user–item matrix is more than 99% empty. From the few cells we know, predict which of the empty ones this person would fill with a high score, and put those at the top of their screen.

The idea

Taste has structure. People who agreed in the past tend to agree again (collaborative filtering), and that structure can be squeezed into a few numbers per user and per item, latent factors such as “how much action” or “how highbrow”, learnt rather than labelled. A rating is then roughly a dot product between a user's vector and an item's vector.

How it works

  1. Log interactions: ratings, clicks, purchases, watch time.
  2. Learn user and item vectors that reproduce the known interactions.
  3. Retrieve a few hundred candidates quickly with a nearest-neighbour search.
  4. Rank them with a richer model; re-rank for diversity and business rules.
  5. Measure with ranking metrics offline and A/B tests online.

An everyday picture

A good bookshop assistant. They do not know you, but they know that people who bought the two books you mention also loved a third one, and they know their shop well enough to group books by feel rather than by shelf label. When a new book arrives with no sales history, they judge it by its cover, author and genre until people start buying it.

Good at
  • Learning taste from behaviour alone, with no item descriptions needed
  • Embeddings that scale to millions of users and items
  • Clear offline metrics for ranked lists
Watch out for
  • Cold start: new users and items have no history
  • Popularity bias and feedback loops: you only learn about what you showed
  • Offline metrics do not always predict online gains

1Collaborative filtering by hand

Six people, six films, ratings from 1 to 5; blanks are films they have not seen. Pick whose blanks to fill. The highlighted cells are predictions from the most similar people.

The oldest recommender idea fits in two steps. First, measure how alike two people are from the films both have rated. Ratings have personal scales (some people give everyone 4 or 5), so we centre each person's ratings on their own mean \(\bar r_u\) before comparing. The cosine of the centred vectors is the Pearson correlation over shared items:

$$\text{sim}(u,v)=\frac{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}(r_{ui}-\bar r_u)(r_{vi}-\bar r_v)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}(r_{ui}-\bar r_u)^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{uv}}(r_{vi}-\bar r_v)^2}}$$

Second, predict a missing rating from the \(k\) most similar people who did rate the film, each pulling the prediction up or down by how far they were from their own mean:

$$\hat r_{ui}=\bar r_u+\frac{\sum_{v\in N_k(u,i)}\text{sim}(u,v)\,(r_{vi}-\bar r_v)}{\sum_{v\in N_k(u,i)}|\text{sim}(u,v)|}$$

That is all of user-based collaborative filtering. Notice what it never needed: no genres, no plot summaries, only who liked what. Its weaknesses show up in the lab, too: with few shared ratings the similarities are noisy, and a film nobody similar has seen cannot be predicted at all.

Going deeper: item-based filtering and implicit feedback

Item-based filtering flips the roles: two films are similar if the same people rated them alike, and your predicted rating for a film is a weighted average of your own ratings of similar films. Amazon popularised it (Linden et al., 2003) because item similarities are more stable than user ones and can be computed offline.

Most real data is implicit: clicks, plays and purchases, not star ratings. A missing cell then means “not seen” more often than “disliked”, so models treat observed interactions as positives with a confidence weight and sample unobserved ones as weak negatives (Hu, Koren and Volinsky, 2008).

Adapted from: Resnick et al., GroupLens (1994); Sarwar et al., Item-based collaborative filtering (2001).

2Matrix factorisation, trained live

80 users and 48 films from three hidden genres; only some ratings are observed. Train \(\hat r_{ui}=\mu+b_u+b_i+\mathbf p_u^\top\mathbf q_i\) and watch the error on held-out ratings, then look at the film vectors it learnt.

Collaborative filtering compares people directly. Matrix factorisation instead gives every user a vector \(\mathbf p_u\in\mathbb R^k\) and every item a vector \(\mathbf q_i\in\mathbb R^k\) and says a rating is their dot product, plus a global mean \(\mu\) and biases for generous users and popular items. In matrix form the full rating matrix is approximated by a product of two thin matrices, \(R\approx PQ^\top\): rank \(k\), like a truncated SVD, but fitted only to the cells we know:

$$\min_{P,Q,b}\ \sum_{(u,i)\in\Omega}\Big[\big(r_{ui}-\mu-b_u-b_i-\mathbf p_u^\top\mathbf q_i\big)^2$$$$\qquad+\lambda\big(\lVert\mathbf p_u\rVert^2+\lVert\mathbf q_i\rVert^2+b_u^2+b_i^2\big)\Big]$$

The sum runs over the observed set \(\Omega\) only; ignoring the blanks is what makes this different from PCA. Each observed rating gives a cheap stochastic-gradient step, shown below. Regularisation \(\lambda\) matters a lot: users with three ratings would otherwise get huge vectors that fit those three exactly and predict nonsense.

Algorithm: matrix factorisation by SGD
  1. input observed ratings \(\Omega\), factors \(k\), step \(\eta\), penalty \(\lambda\)
  2. initialise \(\mathbf p_u,\mathbf q_i\) small and random; \(b_u=b_i=0\); \(\mu\leftarrow\) mean rating
  3. repeat for each epoch, in random order over \((u,i,r)\in\Omega\)
  4. \(e\leftarrow r-(\mu+b_u+b_i+\mathbf p_u^\top\mathbf q_i)\) prediction error
  5. \(b_u\mathrel{+}=\eta(e-\lambda b_u)\); \(b_i\mathrel{+}=\eta(e-\lambda b_i)\)
  6. \(\mathbf p_u\mathrel{+}=\eta(e\,\mathbf q_i-\lambda\mathbf p_u)\); \(\mathbf q_i\mathrel{+}=\eta(e\,\mathbf p_u-\lambda\mathbf q_i)\) use the old p_u
  7. return \(P, Q, b\)

The second plot shows what the model found. Nobody told it the genres, yet with \(k=2\) the film vectors fall into three clusters: the factors are the hidden genres, rediscovered from ratings alone. Raise \(k\) and the held-out error drops a little and then rises (overfitting) unless \(\lambda\) grows with it; lower the share of observed ratings and everything gets harder.

Going deeper: ALS, the Netflix Prize and implicit data

Alternating least squares fixes \(Q\) and solves for every \(\mathbf p_u\) exactly (each is a small ridge regression), then fixes \(P\) and solves for \(Q\), and repeats. Each half-step is embarrassingly parallel, which is why Spark's recommender uses it. For implicit data the weighted ALS of Hu et al. puts every cell in the loss, with confidence \(1+\alpha\cdot\text{count}\) on observed ones.

In the Netflix Prize (2006–2009) a single well-tuned factorisation with biases (Koren, Bell and Volinsky) beat Netflix's own system by around 6%; the winning blend of hundreds of models reached the 10% target. The biases alone explain a surprising share of the variance, which is why the lab shows a bias-only baseline.

Adapted from: Koren, Bell and Volinsky, Matrix Factorization Techniques for Recommender Systems (IEEE Computer, 2009); Funk, “Netflix Update: Try This at Home” (2006).

3Two towers, retrieval and cold start

60 items as 2-D embeddings, coloured by genre; popular items have longer vectors. Tap the plot to place a user's vector. The top 5 by dot product (ringed) is what retrieval returns; switch to cosine to see popularity drop out.

Matrix factorisation has one vector per user ID and one per item ID. A two-tower model replaces the lookup tables with two neural networks: a user tower that turns everything known about the user (recent items, time of day, device, country) into a vector \(\mathbf u\), and an item tower that turns item features (ID, text, image, category, price) into a vector \(\mathbf v\). The score is still a dot product,

$$s(u,i)=f_\theta(\text{user features})^\top g_\phi(\text{item features}),$$

trained so that items the user really engaged with score higher than other items, usually with a softmax over the true item and the other items in the batch (in-batch negatives):

$$\mathcal L=-\log\frac{e^{s(u,i^+)/\tau}}{\sum_{j\in\text{batch}}e^{s(u,j)/\tau}}.$$

The towers never look at each other, and that is the point. All item vectors can be computed ahead of time and put in an approximate nearest-neighbour index (such as FAISS or ScaNN); at request time you run the user tower once and fetch the top few hundred items in milliseconds out of millions. This is candidate retrieval; a heavier ranking model, which can look at user and item together, then orders those few hundred.

Cold start is where features pay off. A new item with no interactions has no learnt ID vector, but its features still go through the item tower and land next to similar items (the star in the lab, placed by its genre), so it can be retrieved from day one. Dot product versus cosine matters too: the dot product rewards long vectors, and popular items get long vectors in training, so it tilts results towards the popular; cosine ignores length.

Going deeper: sampling bias and the logQ correction

In-batch negatives are drawn in proportion to popularity, because popular items appear in more batches. The model therefore learns to push popular items down too hard. Yi et al. (2019) correct for this by subtracting \(\log Q(j)\), the log of each item's sampling probability, from its logit. Mixed negative sampling adds uniformly drawn items to the batch for the same reason.

Adapted from: Covington, Adams and Sargin, Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations (2016); Yi et al., Sampling-bias-corrected neural modeling (2019).

4Ranking metrics on a list you reorder

A recommended list of 8 films. Squares show how relevant each one really was (0 to 3). Move items up and watch every metric respond. The user has 6 relevant films in the whole catalogue; one never made the list.

Recommenders are judged on lists, and position matters: nobody scrolls to item 50. Accuracy metrics like RMSE ignore this, so we use ranking metrics, computed per user on the top \(k\) and averaged over users. Call an item relevant if its grade is above zero, and let \(\text{rel}_j\) be the grade of the item at position \(j\).

  • Precision@k = relevant items in the top \(k\) ÷ \(k\). Recall@k = relevant items in the top \(k\) ÷ all relevant items the user has.
  • Average precision averages precision@j at each position \(j\) that holds a relevant item, so early hits count more; MAP is its mean over users.
  • Reciprocal rank is \(1/\text{position of the first relevant item}\): good when one right answer is enough (search, “play next”).
  • nDCG uses the grades and discounts by position:
$$\text{DCG@}k=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{2^{\text{rel}_j}-1}{\log_2(j+1)},\qquad \text{nDCG@}k=\frac{\text{DCG@}k}{\text{IDCG@}k},$$

where IDCG is the DCG of the best possible ordering of all the user's relevant items, so nDCG lies between 0 and 1. Try swapping a grade-3 item from position 1 with a grade-1 item lower down: precision does not move, nDCG drops. That sensitivity to order is exactly why nDCG is the default for graded relevance.

Going deeper: offline versus online evaluation

Offline metrics are computed on held-out interactions, usually the last few per user (a time-based split avoids training on the future). They are biased towards what the old system showed: an item nobody was shown cannot appear as relevant. So a new model's offline gain must be confirmed by an online A/B test on clicks, watch time or revenue, which is the only measure of what the change actually caused.

Adapted from: Järvelin and Kekäläinen, Cumulated gain-based evaluation of IR techniques (2002); Manning, Raghavan and Schütze, Introduction to Information Retrieval, ch. 8.

5Diversity: re-ranking with MMR

20 candidates scored for relevance; most of the best are from one genre. MMR picks 8 one at a time, trading relevance against similarity to what is already picked. Slide λ from pure diversity to pure relevance.

The 8 recommendations, in order

A list ranked purely by predicted relevance often repeats itself: eight episodes of the same series, ten near-identical phone cases. Each item is individually a good bet, but the list as a whole is a poor one, because if the person is not in the mood for that series, the whole screen is wasted. Maximal marginal relevance builds the list greedily, and each new item must earn its place by being relevant and different from what is already there:

$$i^*=\arg\max_{i\notin S}\Big[\lambda\,\text{rel}(i)-(1-\lambda)\max_{j\in S}\text{sim}(i,j)\Big]$$
Algorithm: MMR re-ranking
  1. input candidates \(C\) with relevance, similarity function, list length \(n\), \(\lambda\)
  2. \(S\leftarrow[\,]\)
  3. while \(|S|<n\)
  4. for each \(i\in C\setminus S\): \(m_i\leftarrow\lambda\,\text{rel}(i)-(1-\lambda)\max_{j\in S}\text{sim}(i,j)\)
  5. append \(\arg\max_i m_i\) to \(S\)
  6. return \(S\)

We measure the result with intra-list diversity, the average dissimilarity \(1-\text{sim}\) over pairs in the list, and genre coverage. In the lab, λ near 1 gives the relevance ranking; around 0.5–0.7 the list keeps most of its relevance and covers far more ground. Real systems add other list-level goals the same way: freshness, fairness to sellers, or not showing what the user already bought.

Adapted from: Carbonell and Goldstein, The use of MMR, diversity-based reranking (SIGIR 1998); Ziegler et al., Improving recommendation lists through topic diversification (2005).

6The same thing in Python

Matrix factorisation on MovieLens with the Surprise library, nDCG with scikit-learn, and a minimal two-tower model in PyTorch.

# pip install scikit-surprise scikit-learn torch numpy
import numpy as np
from surprise import Dataset, SVD, KNNWithMeans, accuracy
from surprise.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import ndcg_score

# ---- 1. Collaborative filtering and matrix factorisation on MovieLens 100k ----
data = Dataset.load_builtin("ml-100k")                      # 100,000 ratings, 943 users, 1,682 films
train, test = train_test_split(data, test_size=0.2, random_state=0)

knn = KNNWithMeans(k=40, sim_options={"name": "pearson", "user_based": True})
knn.fit(train); accuracy.rmse(knn.test(test))               # user-based CF, mean-centred

mf = SVD(n_factors=50, reg_all=0.05, lr_all=0.005, n_epochs=30, random_state=0)
mf.fit(train); accuracy.rmse(mf.test(test))                 # mu + b_u + b_i + p_u . q_i, trained by SGD
print(mf.qi.shape)                                          # learnt item vectors: (n_items, 50)

# ---- 2. Ranking metrics: nDCG@5 for one user's list ----
true_relevance = np.array([[3, 2, 3, 0, 1, 2, 0, 0]])       # graded relevance of 8 items
model_scores   = np.array([[.9, .8, .3, .7, .6, .2, .1, .4]])
print("nDCG@5 =", ndcg_score(true_relevance, model_scores, k=5))

# ---- 3. A minimal two-tower model with in-batch negatives ----
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class Tower(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_ids, n_feats, d=32):
        super().__init__()
        self.id_emb = nn.Embedding(n_ids, d)
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d + n_feats, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, d))
    def forward(self, ids, feats):
        return F.normalize(self.mlp(torch.cat([self.id_emb(ids), feats], -1)), dim=-1)

users, items = Tower(1000, 8), Tower(5000, 16)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(list(users.parameters()) + list(items.parameters()), lr=1e-3)

def train_step(u_ids, u_feats, i_ids, i_feats, tau=0.05):
    u, v = users(u_ids, u_feats), items(i_ids, i_feats)    # (B, d) each; row b is a true pair
    logits = u @ v.T / tau                                  # every other item in the batch is a negative
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, torch.arange(len(u)))
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return loss.item()

# At serving time: precompute items(all_ids, all_feats) into an ANN index (e.g. FAISS),
# run the user tower once per request, and fetch the nearest few hundred items.
