Clustering: finding groups nobody labelled

You have customers, genes, documents or sensor readings, and no labels. Clustering asks the data a simple question: which points belong together? This page builds the four main answers, centres (k-means), merges (hierarchical), density (DBSCAN and HDBSCAN) and probabilities (Gaussian mixtures), and runs each one live so you can see where it shines and where it breaks.

What is clustering, in plain words?

The problem

In supervised learning every example comes with an answer. Most data in the world do not. A shop has millions of purchase histories but no column called “customer type”; a biologist has thousands of cells but no label saying which kind each one is. We still want to know what kinds of thing are in there.

The idea

Clustering splits the points into groups so that points in the same group are similar and points in different groups are not. Everything depends on what “similar” means, and each family of methods means something different:

  1. Centres. A cluster is the points closest to one central point (k-means).
  2. Merges. Start with every point alone and keep joining the two closest groups (hierarchical clustering).
  3. Density. A cluster is a crowded region separated from others by emptier space (DBSCAN, HDBSCAN).
  4. Probability. The data were drawn from a few bell-shaped clouds; find them (Gaussian mixtures).

An everyday picture

Tip a bag of mixed buttons on a table and sort them without instructions. You might sort by colour, by size, or by the number of holes, and each choice gives different, equally “correct” piles. Clustering is the same: the algorithm and the distance you choose decide which piles you get, so the result is a hypothesis to check, not a fact.

Good at
  • Customer and market segments
  • Grouping documents, images or genes
  • Compressing data (colour palettes, vector quantisation)
  • Spotting points that fit no group
Watch out for
  • Every method finds clusters, even in pure noise
  • Feature scaling changes the answer
  • The number of clusters is a choice, not a given
  • Distances lose meaning in very high dimensions

1k-means, one step at a time

Large rings are the centroids. Press Step to alternate the two moves: colour every point by its nearest centroid, then move each centroid to the mean of its points. The thin lines are the borders between centroids’ territories.

Take \(n\) points \(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(n)}\) and a number \(k\). We want \(k\) centres \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k\) and an assignment \(c^{(i)}\in\{1,\dots,k\}\) of each point to a centre. How do we score a choice? Add up the squared distance from every point to its own centre:

$$J(c,\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\lVert x^{(i)}-\mu_{c^{(i)}}\big\rVert^2 .$$

This is called the inertia (or within-cluster sum of squares). Minimising it over everything at once is hard (NP-hard, in fact), but minimising over one half while the other is fixed is easy:

  • Fix the centres, choose the assignments. Each term depends on one \(c^{(i)}\) only, so give each point its nearest centre: \(c^{(i)}=\arg\min_j\lVert x^{(i)}-\mu_j\rVert^2\).
  • Fix the assignments, choose the centres. For cluster \(j\), \(\sum_{i:c^{(i)}=j}\lVert x^{(i)}-\mu_j\rVert^2\) is a bowl in \(\mu_j\); setting its gradient to zero gives the mean of the cluster’s points, the centroid.

Neither move can raise \(J\), and there are finitely many assignments, so the loop always stops. It stops at a local minimum, though: try New start on the stretched blobs with random starts and you will see different answers with different \(J\). That is why libraries run it several times and keep the best, and why the seeding matters.

Algorithm: k-means (Lloyd’s algorithm)
  1. input points \(x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(n)}\), number of clusters \(k\)
  2. choose starting centres \(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_k\) k-means++ below
  3. repeat
  4. for each point: \(c^{(i)}\leftarrow\arg\min_j\lVert x^{(i)}-\mu_j\rVert^2\) assignment step
  5. for each cluster: \(\mu_j\leftarrow\) mean of the points with \(c^{(i)}=j\) update step
  6. until no assignment changes
  7. return \(c,\ \mu\) and the inertia \(J\)
Algorithm: k-means++ seeding
  1. pick the first centre uniformly at random from the points
  2. while fewer than \(k\) centres do
  3. \(D(x)\leftarrow\) distance from each point to its nearest chosen centre
  4. pick the next centre at random with probability \(\propto D(x)^2\) far points are likely
  5. return the \(k\) centres, then run Lloyd’s algorithm
Going deeper: what k-means assumes, and why k-means++ works

Each cluster is the set of points nearer to its centre than to any other centre, so the borders are straight lines (a Voronoi diagram). k-means therefore suits round, similar-sized groups and fails on long, curved or nested ones, as the moons and rings show. It is also the special case of a Gaussian mixture with equal, spherical covariances and hard assignments (section 6).

Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007) proved that k-means++ seeding alone gives an expected inertia within a factor \(O(\log k)\) of the best possible, and in practice it needs fewer Lloyd iterations. Each iteration costs \(O(nkd)\), which is why k-means scales to millions of points (mini-batch k-means scales further still).

2How many clusters? Elbow and silhouette

k-means run for \(k=1\) to 8 on the data from section 1 (best of 4 starts each). Left axis, purple: inertia. Orange: mean silhouette (scaled to the same axis; its value is in the table). The best silhouette is ringed.

Inertia always falls as \(k\) grows (with \(k=n\) it is zero), so we cannot just minimise it. Two common heuristics:

The elbow. Plot inertia against \(k\) and look for the bend where adding a cluster stops paying off. On the round blobs the elbow is sharp at 4. On real data it is often a gentle curve with no clear bend.

The silhouette. For each point, let \(a(i)\) be its mean distance to the other points of its own cluster and \(b(i)\) its mean distance to the points of the nearest other cluster. Then

$$s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\{a(i),\,b(i)\}}\in[-1,1].$$

\(s(i)\) near 1 means the point sits deep inside its cluster, near 0 means it is on a border, and negative means it is probably in the wrong cluster. The mean silhouette over all points is highest for compact, well-separated clusters, so we pick the \(k\) that maximises it.

Both measures reward round, separated groups, so they share k-means’ blind spots: on the rings they happily prefer a wrong answer. Switch the data to No structure: the silhouette never reaches 0.5, which is one hint that the clusters are not real. The honest test is usually outside the data: do the groups mean something, and are they stable when you resample?

Going deeper: other ways to choose k

The gap statistic (Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, 2001) compares \(\log J(k)\) with its value on uniform random data of the same extent and picks the \(k\) with the largest gap. For Gaussian mixtures, the BIC \(-2\log L+p\log n\) penalises extra parameters and is a principled choice (section 6). Stability: cluster many bootstrap samples and keep the \(k\) whose clusters agree most across samples, measured by the adjusted Rand index.

3Hierarchical clustering and the dendrogram

120 points merged bottom-up. The dendrogram (below) records every merge at the height where it happened. Drag the cut line on the dendrogram, or use the slider, to choose how many clusters to keep.

Agglomerative clustering needs no \(k\) up front. Start with \(n\) clusters of one point each. At every step merge the two closest clusters, until one is left. The result is a tree, the dendrogram; cutting it at any height gives a flat clustering.

The one design choice is how to measure the distance between two clusters \(A\) and \(B\), called the linkage:

$$\begin{aligned}\text{single:}&\ \min_{a\in A,b\in B}\lVert a-b\rVert &\quad \text{complete:}&\ \max_{a\in A,b\in B}\lVert a-b\rVert\\ \text{average:}&\ \tfrac{1}{|A||B|}\textstyle\sum_{a,b}\lVert a-b\rVert &\quad \text{Ward:}&\ \Delta J=\tfrac{|A||B|}{|A|+|B|}\lVert\mu_A-\mu_B\rVert^2\end{aligned}$$
  • Single follows chains of near neighbours, so it finds moons and rings, but one bridge of noise merges two groups (“chaining”).
  • Complete and average prefer compact groups of similar width.
  • Ward merges the pair that raises the k-means inertia \(J\) the least, so it behaves like a hierarchical k-means. It is the usual default.
Algorithm: agglomerative clustering
  1. input points, a linkage (single, complete, average or Ward)
  2. make each point its own cluster; compute all pairwise distances
  3. while more than one cluster do
  4. find the two clusters \(A,B\) with the smallest linkage distance
  5. merge them; record the merge and its height in the dendrogram
  6. update the distances from \(A\cup B\) to every other cluster Lance–Williams formula
  7. return the dendrogram; cut it at a height (or at \(k\) clusters)
Going deeper: cost, and the Lance–Williams update

All four linkages can be updated from the old distances alone: \(d(A\cup B,C)=\alpha_A d(A,C)+\alpha_B d(B,C)+\beta d(A,B)+\gamma|d(A,C)-d(B,C)|\), with constants that depend on the linkage (for single linkage \(\alpha=\tfrac12,\beta=0,\gamma=-\tfrac12\), giving the minimum). The simple version on this page costs \(O(n^3)\) time and \(O(n^2)\) memory, fine for thousands of points but not millions. Single linkage equals cutting the minimum spanning tree, which is how HDBSCAN builds on it.

4DBSCAN: clusters are crowded regions

Filled dots are core points (at least minPts points within ε, counting themselves), small dots are border points (within ε of a core point), grey crosses are noise. Tap a point to draw its ε-circle.

Centres cannot describe a moon or a ring. DBSCAN (Ester et al., 1996) uses density instead. It has two settings: a radius \(\varepsilon\) and a count minPts. The \(\varepsilon\)-neighbourhood of a point is \(N_\varepsilon(x)=\{y:\lVert x-y\rVert\le\varepsilon\}\).

  • A core point has \(|N_\varepsilon(x)|\ge\) minPts: it sits inside a crowd.
  • Two core points within \(\varepsilon\) of each other are in the same cluster; follow such links as far as they go.
  • A non-core point within \(\varepsilon\) of a core point joins that cluster as a border point; anything else is noise.

DBSCAN finds the number of clusters itself, follows any shape, and labels outliers instead of forcing them into a group. Its weakness is the single \(\varepsilon\): on the Mixed density data, an \(\varepsilon\) small enough to split the tight group from its neighbours leaves the loose group as noise, and one large enough for the loose group merges everything else. The next section fixes that.

Picking ε: sort every point’s distance to its minPts-th neighbour and look for the knee of that curve (shown below); a common rule is minPts ≈ 2 × the number of features.

Algorithm: DBSCAN
  1. input points, radius \(\varepsilon\), minPts
  2. for each point \(p\): find \(N_\varepsilon(p)\); \(p\) is core if \(|N_\varepsilon(p)|\ge\) minPts
  3. for each core point \(p\) not yet labelled do
  4. start a new cluster; put \(p\) on a queue
  5. while the queue is not empty: pop \(q\); label every point of \(N_\varepsilon(q)\); queue the core points among them grow through core points
  6. points never labelled are noise
  7. return the cluster labels (−1 for noise)

5HDBSCAN: every density at once

Mixed-density data clustered two ways. Top: DBSCAN with the ε and minPts from section 4. Bottom: HDBSCAN, which has no ε, only a smallest cluster size. Grey crosses are noise. Numbers give the agreement with the true groups (adjusted Rand index, 1 = perfect).

HDBSCAN (Campello, Moulavi and Sander, 2013) runs DBSCAN for every ε at once and keeps the clusters that survive longest. In four steps:

  1. Core distance. \(\text{core}_m(x)\) = distance to the \(m\)-th nearest neighbour: small in crowds, large in sparse areas.
  2. Mutual reachability. \(d_{\text{mr}}(a,b)=\max\{\text{core}_m(a),\ \text{core}_m(b),\ \lVert a-b\rVert\}\). Sparse points are pushed away from everything, so noise cannot bridge clusters.
  3. Tree. Build the minimum spanning tree under \(d_{\text{mr}}\) and remove edges from longest to shortest. That is single-linkage clustering, i.e. DBSCAN at every ε.
  4. Condense and select. Lower ε (raise \(\lambda=1/\varepsilon\)). A split counts only if both sides have at least min cluster size points; otherwise the small side just “falls out” as noise. Each cluster’s stability is \(\sum_{x}(\lambda_x-\lambda_{\text{birth}})\), how long its points stay in it. Keep the set of non-overlapping clusters with the largest total stability.

The result handles mixed densities, needs one intuitive setting, and gives noise labels. It is now usually the first density method to try (it is in scikit-learn as HDBSCAN).

Algorithm: HDBSCAN
  1. input points, smallest cluster size \(m\)
  2. core distance of each point = distance to its \(m\)-th nearest neighbour
  3. build the minimum spanning tree under mutual-reachability distance
  4. sort its edges; removing them from longest to shortest gives the cluster tree
  5. condense: a split counts only if both parts have \(\ge m\) points else the small part is noise
  6. stability of a cluster \(=\sum_x(\lambda_x-\lambda_{\text{birth}})\)
  7. bottom-up: keep a cluster if it is more stable than its children together
  8. return the kept clusters; other points are noise

6Gaussian mixtures and expectation–maximisation

Each ellipse is one Gaussian (drawn at 1 and 2 standard deviations). Point colours are blended by responsibility: a point halfway between two clouds is half one colour, half the other. Step through EM and watch the log-likelihood (below) climb.

A Gaussian mixture model says each point was made in two steps: first pick a component \(z\in\{1,\dots,k\}\) with probability \(\phi_j\), then draw \(x\) from a Gaussian \(\mathcal N(\mu_j,\Sigma_j)\). The density of \(x\) is

$$p(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\phi_j\,\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j).$$

If we knew every \(z^{(i)}\) this would be GDA: fit one Gaussian per group. We do not, so the EM algorithm guesses them softly and improves the guess:

  • E-step. For each point, the responsibility of component \(j\) is the posterior probability that it made the point: \(w^{(i)}_j=\dfrac{\phi_j\,\mathcal N(x^{(i)}\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}{\sum_l\phi_l\,\mathcal N(x^{(i)}\mid\mu_l,\Sigma_l)}\).
  • M-step. Refit each Gaussian with the points weighted by responsibility: \(N_j=\sum_i w^{(i)}_j\), \(\phi_j=N_j/n\), \(\mu_j=\tfrac{1}{N_j}\sum_i w^{(i)}_jx^{(i)}\), \(\Sigma_j=\tfrac{1}{N_j}\sum_i w^{(i)}_j(x^{(i)}-\mu_j)(x^{(i)}-\mu_j)^\top\).

Each EM step can never lower the log-likelihood \(\ell=\sum_i\log p(x^{(i)})\) (the chart below the plot), so it converges, again to a local maximum. Because each component has its own covariance, a mixture fits the tilted, stretched groups that k-means cuts wrongly, and it says how sure it is about every point.

Algorithm: EM for a Gaussian mixture
  1. input points, number of components \(k\)
  2. initialise \(\phi_j,\mu_j,\Sigma_j\) e.g. from k-means++
  3. repeat
  4. E-step: \(w^{(i)}_j\leftarrow P(z^{(i)}=j\mid x^{(i)})\) for every point and component
  5. M-step: \(\phi_j,\mu_j,\Sigma_j\leftarrow\) weighted proportion, mean and covariance
  6. compute the log-likelihood \(\ell\)
  7. until \(\ell\) stops improving
  8. return the mixture and the responsibilities
Going deeper: why EM climbs, and k-means as a special case

For any distribution \(Q_i\) over \(z^{(i)}\), Jensen’s inequality gives a lower bound \(\ell(\theta)\ge\sum_i\sum_jQ_i(j)\log\frac{p(x^{(i)},z^{(i)}=j;\theta)}{Q_i(j)}\), the evidence lower bound. The E-step sets \(Q_i\) to the posterior, which makes the bound touch \(\ell\) at the current \(\theta\); the M-step maximises the bound over \(\theta\). So \(\ell(\theta_{t+1})\ge\text{bound}(\theta_{t+1})\ge\text{bound}(\theta_t)=\ell(\theta_t)\). This argument is the same one used by variational autoencoders.

Fix every \(\Sigma_j=\sigma^2I\) and \(\phi_j=1/k\) and let \(\sigma\to0\): responsibilities become 0 or 1 (nearest centre) and the M-step becomes the centroid update, which is exactly k-means. To choose \(k\), compare the BIC \(-2\ell+p\log n\), where \(p\) counts the parameters (in 2-D, each component adds 2 means, 3 covariance entries and 1 weight).

7Which method for which shape?

The same data clustered five ways with sensible settings (k set to the true number for the methods that need it). The score under each is the adjusted Rand index against the true groups: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than chance.

Practical order: scale the features (see datasets), try k-means or a mixture for a quick baseline, then HDBSCAN if the groups are irregular or you expect noise. In high dimensions cluster on a reduced representation (PCA, or embeddings from a neural network). Use t-SNE or UMAP to look at the result, but be careful clustering directly on their output, because they distort densities.

Always look at the pure-noise case: every method above reports clusters on No structure. A clustering is only worth acting on if it is stable across resamples and settings, and means something to someone who knows the data.

8The same thing in Python

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, AgglomerativeClustering, DBSCAN, HDBSCAN
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score, adjusted_rand_score

X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X_raw)                       # distances need comparable units

for k in range(2, 9):                                         # elbow and silhouette
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(k, km.inertia_, silhouette_score(X, km.labels_))

ward   = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=4, linkage="ward").fit_predict(X)
db     = DBSCAN(eps=0.3, min_samples=5).fit_predict(X)          # -1 = noise
hdb    = HDBSCAN(min_cluster_size=12).fit_predict(X)              # scikit-learn >= 1.3
gmm    = GaussianMixture(n_components=3, covariance_type="full", n_init=5).fit(X)
soft   = gmm.predict_proba(X)                                     # responsibilities
print(gmm.bic(X), adjusted_rand_score(y_true, gmm.predict(X)))  # if you happen to have labels

from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram, fcluster  # the full tree
Z = linkage(X, method="average"); dendrogram(Z); labels = fcluster(Z, t=4, criterion="maxclust")
