Support Vector Machines

When two classes can be separated by a line, there are usually infinitely many lines that do it. Which one should we trust on new data? The support vector machine's answer is: the one that stays as far as possible from both classes. That single idea leads to a boundary that depends on only a handful of points, the support vectors, and to the kernel trick, which lets the same straight-line machinery draw circles and spirals. Move points around and watch which ones matter, then swap kernels to bend the boundary.

What is a support vector machine, in plain words?

The problem

Picture red and blue points on a page that a ruler can separate. You could place the ruler so it nearly grazes a red point, or nearly grazes a blue one, or somewhere in the middle. All three get the training data 100% right. But a new red point that lands a little off from its friends would be misclassified by the first line. The line in the middle leaves the most room for error on both sides, so it is the safest bet.

The idea

A support vector machine picks the line with the widest empty corridor, the margin, between the classes. Only the points touching or inside that corridor matter; they are the support vectors, and the rest of the data could be deleted without changing the answer.

Real data overlaps, so a soft margin lets some points cross at a price set by \(C\). For curved boundaries a kernel measures similarity in a richer hidden space (the kernel trick), so the SVM can draw circles and spirals while still solving a straight-line problem behind the scenes.

How it works

  1. Find \(w,b\) that maximise the margin width \(2/\lVert w\rVert\) while keeping points on the right side.
  2. Allow violations with slack, each costing \(C\).
  3. Solve the dual problem, which only needs similarities between pairs of points (SMO solver).
  4. Swap in a kernel (linear, polynomial, RBF) to bend the boundary.

An everyday picture

Building a road between two villages: you want the widest possible road that does not knock down any houses. Only the houses right at the edge of the road decide where it goes. Houses further back could be moved or demolished and the road would stay exactly where it is.

Good at
  • Strong on small and medium datasets
  • Kernels give flexible boundaries with few hyperparameters
  • Convex: one best answer
  • Prediction needs only the support vectors
Watch out for
  • Needs feature scaling
  • C and γ must be tuned
  • Slow for very large datasets; no probabilities by default

2Support vectors: only a few points decide the boundary

After training, every point gets a weight \(\alpha_i\in[0,C]\) that says how much it pushes on the boundary (section 3 explains where these come from). The prediction for a new point \(x\) is a weighted vote of the training points, each one voting with its label \(y_i\) and its similarity \(K(x_i,x)\) to \(x\):

$$f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\,y_i\,K(x_i,x)+b=\sum_{i\,\in\,\mathrm{SV}}\alpha_i\,y_i\,K(x_i,x)+b$$

Points with \(\alpha_i=0\) drop out of the sum, so they have no influence at all. The KKT conditions sort every point into one of three groups:

3The maths

We turn "widest corridor" into an optimisation problem in three steps: measure the corridor, allow some mistakes, then rewrite the problem in a form where only similarities between points appear.

Step 1: how wide is the corridor? (hard margin)

A line (in general a hyperplane) is the set of points with \(w^\top x+b=0\); the vector \(w\) is perpendicular to it. The signed distance from a point \(x_i\) to the line is \((w^\top x_i+b)/\lVert w\rVert\), the length of its projection onto the unit normal. There is a free choice of scale: \(w,b\) and \(2w,2b\) describe the same line. Use it to fix the closest points on each side at \(y_i(w^\top x_i+b)=1\). Then the margin edges are the lines \(w^\top x+b=\pm1\), and the width between them is \(2/\lVert w\rVert\). Making the corridor wide means making \(\lVert w\rVert\) small:

$$\min_{w,b}\ \tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1\ \ \forall i$$

(Squaring and halving does not change the minimiser, and makes the problem a smooth quadratic.) This is a convex problem with a unique answer.

Step 2: allow some mistakes (soft margin)

If the classes overlap, no line satisfies every constraint, and the problem has no solution. So give each point some slack \(\xi_i\ge0\): how far it is allowed to fall short of the margin. Each unit of slack costs \(C\):

$$\min_{w,b,\xi}\ \tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_i\xi_i\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ y_i(w^\top x_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i,\ \xi_i\ge0$$

At the optimum each \(\xi_i\) is as small as allowed, \(\xi_i=\max(0,1-y_if(x_i))\), which is the hinge loss. So the soft-margin SVM is just hinge loss plus an L2 penalty: \(\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_i\max(0,1-y_if(x_i))\). Compare logistic regression, which is log-loss plus an L2 penalty. The hinge is exactly zero for points safely outside the margin, and that is where the sparsity comes from. Small \(C\) = wide, forgiving margin (more bias). Large \(C\) = narrow margin that tries to classify every training point (more variance).

Step 3: the KKT conditions sort the points

Attach a Lagrange multiplier \(\alpha_i\ge0\) to each margin constraint. At the optimum the KKT conditions hold:

$$w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i,\qquad \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0,\qquad \alpha_i\big[y_if(x_i)-1+\xi_i\big]=0,\qquad (C-\alpha_i)\,\xi_i=0$$

Read them one at a time. The first says the weight vector is a combination of the training points. The third, complementary slackness, says: either a point's constraint is tight (it sits on the margin) or its \(\alpha_i\) is zero. So a point strictly outside the margin contributes nothing to \(w\). The fourth says a point with slack (inside the margin or misclassified) has \(\alpha_i=C\), the maximum. That is exactly the three groups in the table of section 2.

Going deeper: deriving the dual from the Lagrangian

The Lagrangian of the soft-margin problem, with multipliers \(\alpha_i\ge0\) for the margin constraints and \(\mu_i\ge0\) for \(\xi_i\ge0\), is

$$\mathcal L=\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_i\xi_i-\sum_i\alpha_i\big[y_i(w^\top x_i+b)-1+\xi_i\big]-\sum_i\mu_i\xi_i .$$

Minimise over the primal variables by setting derivatives to zero: \(\partial_w\mathcal L=0\Rightarrow w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\); \(\partial_b\mathcal L=0\Rightarrow\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0\); \(\partial_{\xi_i}\mathcal L=0\Rightarrow\alpha_i=C-\mu_i\), so \(0\le\alpha_i\le C\). Substitute back: the \(\xi\) terms cancel, \(\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2-\sum_i\alpha_iy_iw^\top x_i=-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jx_i^\top x_j\), and the \(b\) term vanishes. What remains is the dual on the right. The problem is convex and strictly feasible (Slater's condition), so strong duality holds: the dual optimum equals the primal optimum, and the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient.

The dual problem

Plugging \(w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\) back in removes \(w\) entirely and leaves a problem in the \(\alpha\)'s alone:

$$\max_{\alpha}\ \sum_i\alpha_i-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,\langle x_i,x_j\rangle\quad\text{s.t.}\quad 0\le\alpha_i\le C,\ \ \sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0$$

Look at what the data appear as: only inner products \(\langle x_i,x_j\rangle\), the dot products between pairs of points. That is the key observation. Replace them with a kernel \(K(x_i,x_j)=\langle\phi(x_i),\phi(x_j)\rangle\) and the SVM works in the feature space of \(\phi\) without ever computing \(\phi\). That is the kernel trick.

Kernels on this page

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Linear: }&K(x,z)=x^\top z\\ \text{Polynomial: }&K(x,z)=(\gamma\,x^\top z+r)^d\\ \text{RBF: }&K(x,z)=\exp(-\gamma\lVert x-z\rVert^2)\\ \text{Sigmoid: }&K(x,z)=\tanh(\gamma\,x^\top z+r)\end{aligned}$$

Think of a kernel as a similarity score. The RBF kernel is 1 for identical points and decays to 0 with distance; \(1/\sqrt{\gamma}\) is roughly its radius of influence. Each support vector puts a Gaussian bump of height \(\alpha_iy_i\) on the plane, and \(f\) is their sum: positive bumps from one class, negative from the other, and the boundary is where they cancel.

Going deeper: what makes a valid kernel (Mercer)

Not every similarity function is an inner product in some feature space. \(K\) is a valid kernel exactly when, for every finite set of points, the Gram matrix \(G_{ij}=K(x_i,x_j)\) is symmetric positive semi-definite (Mercer's condition). That is also what keeps the dual concave, so the optimum is unique. Valid kernels can be combined: sums, positive multiples, products, and \(\exp\) of a valid kernel are valid. The RBF kernel's feature map is infinite-dimensional: expanding \(e^{2\gamma x^\top z}\) as a power series gives every polynomial degree at once. The sigmoid kernel is not PSD for all \(\gamma,r\), which is why it can behave badly in the gallery below.

How this page solves it

The dual is a quadratic problem with box constraints and one equality. The equality \(\sum\alpha_iy_i=0\) means you cannot change a single \(\alpha\) on its own, but you can change two at once, keeping their weighted sum fixed. That is the idea behind SMO (sequential minimal optimisation): repeatedly pick the two \(\alpha\)'s that most violate the KKT conditions (using LIBSVM's second-order working-set selection) and solve that two-variable problem in closed form.

Algorithm: SMO for the SVM dual
  1. input kernel matrix \(K_{ij}=K(x_i,x_j)\), labels \(y_i\in\{-1,+1\}\), \(C\), tolerance \(\varepsilon\)
  2. \(\alpha\leftarrow0\); gradient \(G_i\leftarrow-1\) for all \(i\) G = ∂(−dual)/∂α
  3. repeat
  4. pick \(i\) with the largest \(-y_iG_i\) among those that can move up
  5. pick \(j\) giving the largest decrease among those that can move down second-order choice
  6. if the KKT gap \(<\varepsilon\) then stop
  7. \(\eta\leftarrow K_{ii}+K_{jj}-2K_{ij}\) curvature along the pair
  8. move \(\alpha_i,\alpha_j\) along the line \(y_i\alpha_i+y_j\alpha_j=\)const by the Newton step, clipped to \([0,C]\)
  9. update \(G\) using columns \(i\) and \(j\) of \(K\)
  10. \(b\leftarrow\) average over free SVs (\(0<\alpha_i<C\)) of \(y_i-\sum_j\alpha_jy_jK_{ji}\)
  11. return \(\alpha\), \(b\) predict with f(x) = Σ αᵢyᵢK(xᵢ,x) + b

4The kernel trick: from 2D to 3D

Why would working in a bigger space help? First look at the problem flat, in 2D, where no straight line can work. Then look at the same points after adding a third coordinate, first side-on and then in full 3D.

Step 1 · The data in 2D: try a straight line

Dashed: the straight line you control. Solid purple: the circle that the 3D plane below cuts out (set by the plane height slider). The middle number is the best any angle and offset can reach (found by trying them all): a line can only cut the ring off on one side, so many points stay wrong.

Step 2 · Add \(z=x_1^2+x_2^2\) and look from the side

Horizontal axis: distance from the centre \(r=\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}\). Vertical axis: the new coordinate \(z=r^2\), so every point sits on the curve \(z=r^2\). The inner class is low, the ring is high, and a flat cut at one height (purple) now separates them. In 2D that cut is the circle on the left.

Step 3 · The same lift in full 3D

Drag to rotate. Lifting with \(\phi(x_1,x_2)=(x_1,\,x_2,\,x_1^2+x_2^2)\) pushes the outer ring up. A flat plane (a linear separator in 3D) now splits the classes, and that plane cuts back down to a circle in 2D.

The circles dataset cannot be split by a line in the plane. But add a third coordinate \(z=x_1^2+x_2^2\) (squared distance from the origin) and the inner class sits low while the outer ring sits high. A curve in the original space has become a flat plane in the lifted one.

The trick is that the SVM never needs the lifted points themselves, only their dot products, and those can be computed straight from the original coordinates:

$$\phi(x)^\top\phi(z)=x_1z_1+x_2z_2+(x_1^2+x_2^2)(z_1^2+z_2^2)$$

A kernel lets the SVM use a feature map like this one, or even an infinite-dimensional one (RBF), at the cost of evaluating \(K\) on pairs of points. The polynomial kernel of degree 2 contains exactly these squared terms, which is why it handles circles well in the gallery below.

Going deeper: why the kernel trick saves work

The polynomial kernel \((x^\top z+1)^d\) in \(p\) dimensions corresponds to a feature map with every monomial up to degree \(d\): \(\binom{p+d}{d}\) features. For \(p=100\), \(d=3\) that is 176,851 features, but the kernel costs one dot product and a cube. The representer theorem guarantees this is enough: for any loss plus an L2 penalty on \(w\), the optimal \(w\) lies in the span of the \(\phi(x_i)\), so \(f(x)=\sum_i\beta_iK(x_i,x)\). The flip side is that the work now scales with \(n\): the kernel matrix has \(n^2\) entries, which is why kernel SVMs struggle beyond about \(10^5\) examples.

5Which kernel for which data?

Every cell is a separate SVM trained live on 200 points (C = ) and scored on 100 held-out points. The number is test accuracy; the small number is the count of support vectors. Click a cell to load it into the lab above.

Linear: best when the classes really are separated by a line (linear blobs, noisy labels). Fewest knobs, hardest to overfit, and gives an interpretable \(w\).
Polynomial: captures interactions such as \(x_1x_2\) (XOR) and \(x_1^2+x_2^2\) (circles). High degrees get numerically wild far from the data.
RBF: the default for unknown shapes (moons, spirals). Tune \(\gamma\) and \(C\) together with cross-validation; large \(\gamma\) memorises noise.

A useful habit: count the support vectors. If nearly every point is one, the model is either very forgiving (small \(C\)) or memorising (large \(\gamma\)), and test accuracy will usually tell you which.

6C and γ together (RBF kernel)

The two knobs work together. \(C\) says how much a training mistake costs; \(\gamma\) says how far each point's influence reaches. A 3 × 3 grid of values, fitted on the dataset currently in the lab. Rows raise \(C\), columns raise \(\gamma\).

Small \(\gamma\) + small \(C\): nearly linear and smooth (underfits). Large \(\gamma\) + large \(C\): little islands around individual points (overfits). The sweet spot is usually in between, chosen by cross-validation.

7The same thing in Python

from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

svm = SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1.0, gamma=1.0).fit(X, y)        # kernel: "linear" | "poly" (degree, coef0) | "rbf" | "sigmoid"
svm.support_            # indices of the support vectors
svm.dual_coef_          # alpha_i * y_i for each support vector
svm.intercept_          # b
svm.decision_function(X_new)   # f(x) = sum alpha_i y_i K(x_i, x) + b

# choose C and gamma by cross-validation
grid = GridSearchCV(SVC(kernel="rbf"), {"C": [0.1, 1, 10, 100], "gamma": [0.1, 1, 10]}, cv=5).fit(X, y)

You can also solve the same dual with scipy.optimize.minimize; SMO (used here and inside LIBSVM, which scikit-learn wraps) is the specialised algorithm that scales to large \(n\).
