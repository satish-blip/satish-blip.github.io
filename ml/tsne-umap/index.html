t-SNE and UMAP: maps of data with too many dimensions

A dataset of handwritten digits has 64 numbers per image; a word embedding has 300; a single cell, 20,000 genes. We cannot look at those spaces, but we can draw a 2-D map that keeps each point next to its true neighbours. This page builds the two standard map-makers, t-SNE and UMAP, runs them live, and shows what their pictures do and do not tell you.

What are t-SNE and UMAP, in plain words?

The problem

Plots have two axes. Data often have hundreds. PCA squashes many dimensions into two by keeping the directions of largest spread, but it can only rotate and drop axes. If the data lie on a curved surface, or in many separate groups, a flat projection piles different things on top of each other.

The idea

Forget global geometry and keep only who is next to whom. For each point, list its near neighbours in the original space. Then place the points on a page and move them until each point’s neighbours on the page match its neighbours in the data. This is manifold learning, or non-linear dimensionality reduction.

  1. Measure similarities between points in the original space.
  2. Start with points scattered on a 2-D page.
  3. Pull together points that are similar in the data; push apart the rest.
  4. Repeat until the layout settles.

An everyday picture

Seating a wedding from a list of who knows whom. You cannot keep everyone’s exact “social distance”, but you can make sure friends share tables and strangers do not. The room plan shows the friendship groups clearly, yet the distance between two tables, or the size of a table, means very little.

Good at
  • Seeing clusters in embeddings, images, cells or documents
  • Spotting mislabelled or odd points
  • Checking what a neural network has learned
Watch out for
  • Cluster sizes and gaps between clusters are not meaningful
  • Settings (perplexity, neighbours) change the picture
  • Random noise can look like clusters
  • Not a feature extractor for new data (t-SNE)

1Why not just PCA?

The same high-dimensional data drawn three ways. Colours are the true groups (or the position along the roll); none of the methods sees them. The number under each map is the share of each point’s 10 nearest neighbours in the original space that are still among its 10 nearest on the map.

Take the Swiss roll: a sheet of paper rolled up in 3-D. Two points on neighbouring layers of the roll are close in straight-line distance but far apart along the sheet. PCA looks for the flattest view of the whole roll and lays the layers on top of each other, so neighbours on the map are often not neighbours on the sheet. A neighbour-based method unrolls it.

With the Digits data (8×8 pixel images of the digits 0 to 9, so 64 numbers each, generated on this page with random shifts, thickness and noise), PCA separates a few digits but stacks the others. t-SNE and UMAP pull the ten digits apart, because each image’s nearest images really are the same digit.

PCA is still the right first step: it is linear, exact, fast, keeps global distances and can project new points. Practitioners often run PCA to 50 dimensions first and give that to t-SNE or UMAP, which removes noise and speeds them up.

$$\text{neighbours kept}=\frac{1}{nk}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|\,\text{kNN}_{\text{data}}(i)\cap\text{kNN}_{\text{map}}(i)\,\big|$$

This score is a simple cousin of the “trustworthiness” measure (Venna and Kaski, 2001). 1 means every neighbourhood survived.

2t-SNE, iteration by iteration

Press Run and watch the map form. During the first 100 iterations (“early exaggeration”) the attractions are multiplied by 12, which pulls clusters into tight clumps that can then move past each other.

t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) turns distances into probabilities twice, once in the data and once on the map, and makes the two sets of probabilities match.

In the data. Put a Gaussian of width \(\sigma_i\) on point \(x_i\). The chance that \(x_i\) would “pick” \(x_j\) as its neighbour is

$$p_{j\mid i}=\frac{\exp(-\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k\ne i}\exp(-\lVert x_i-x_k\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2)},\qquad p_{ij}=\frac{p_{j\mid i}+p_{i\mid j}}{2n}.$$

Each \(\sigma_i\) is chosen so the point has a fixed effective number of neighbours, the perplexity (next section). Symmetrising gives one probability \(p_{ij}\) per pair, summing to 1 over all pairs.

On the map. With map positions \(y_i\in\mathbb R^2\), use a Student-t curve with one degree of freedom instead of a Gaussian:

$$q_{ij}=\frac{(1+\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k\ne l}(1+\lVert y_k-y_l\rVert^2)^{-1}}.$$

Match them. Minimise the Kullback–Leibler divergence \(C=\mathrm{KL}(P\,\|\,Q)=\sum_{i\ne j}p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}\) by gradient descent. The gradient has a neat physical reading, a spring between every pair:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial y_i}=4\sum_{j}(p_{ij}-q_{ij})\,\frac{y_i-y_j}{1+\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^2}.$$

If the pair is more similar in the data than on the map (\(p_{ij}>q_{ij}\)) the spring pulls them together; otherwise it pushes them apart. KL is asymmetric: putting true neighbours far apart (large \(p\), small \(q\)) costs a lot, putting non-neighbours close costs little. So t-SNE protects local structure and is careless about global layout.

Algorithm: t-SNE
  1. input points \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), perplexity, iterations \(T\)
  2. for each point: binary-search \(\sigma_i\) so that \(\text{Perp}(P_i)\) = perplexity
  3. \(p_{ij}\leftarrow(p_{j\mid i}+p_{i\mid j})/2n\)
  4. place \(y_i\) at small random positions
  5. for \(t=1,\dots,T\) do
  6. compute all \(q_{ij}\) from the Student-t kernel
  7. gradient \(g_i=4\sum_j(\alpha p_{ij}-q_{ij})(y_i-y_j)/(1+\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert^2)\) \(\alpha=12\) early, then 1
  8. \(y_i\leftarrow y_i+\) momentum update with step \(-\eta g_i\)
  9. return the map \(y_1,\dots,y_n\)
Going deeper: making it fast

The exact version above costs \(O(n^2)\) per iteration, fine for a few thousand points. Barnes–Hut t-SNE (van der Maaten, 2014) groups far-away points into the centre of mass of a quadtree cell, giving \(O(n\log n)\); FIt-SNE (Linderman et al., 2019) interpolates on a grid with FFTs and handles millions of points. Modern defaults (openTSNE, scikit-learn) also start from a PCA layout rather than random positions, which keeps more global structure and makes runs repeatable (Kobak and Berens, 2019).

3Perplexity: how many neighbours each point listens to

The point ringed on the t-SNE map above (tap the map to choose another). Bars: its neighbour probabilities \(p_{j\mid i}\) against distance in the original space, for the current perplexity. Purple bars are points of the same true group.

A single global width would not work: in a dense region a small \(\sigma\) already finds many neighbours, while in a sparse region the same \(\sigma\) finds none. t-SNE therefore gives each point its own \(\sigma_i\), chosen so that its neighbour distribution has the same perplexity:

$$\text{Perp}(P_i)=2^{H(P_i)},\qquad H(P_i)=-\sum_j p_{j\mid i}\log_2 p_{j\mid i}.$$

Perplexity is “the effective number of neighbours”: a distribution spread evenly over 30 points has perplexity exactly 30. Because \(H\) rises steadily with \(\sigma_i\), a binary search finds the right \(\sigma_i\) in a few dozen steps.

Low perplexity (2 to 5) makes each point listen only to its closest few, giving many small, fragmented clumps; high perplexity (50 and above) averages over wider areas and shows bigger structure but can merge small groups. The usual range is 5 to 50, and it must stay well below \(n\). Try it on the Pure noise data with perplexity 3 and you will see “clusters” that do not exist.

Algorithm: finding σᵢ for a target perplexity
  1. input squared distances from \(x_i\), target perplexity \(u\)
  2. \(\beta\leftarrow1\) \(\beta=1/2\sigma_i^2\); lo \(\leftarrow0\), hi \(\leftarrow\infty\)
  3. repeat
  4. \(p_{j\mid i}\propto e^{-\beta d_{ij}^2}\); \(H\leftarrow\) entropy
  5. if \(H>\log u\): lo \(\leftarrow\beta\), raise \(\beta\) too many neighbours: narrow
  6. else: hi \(\leftarrow\beta\), lower \(\beta\) too few: widen
  7. until \(|H-\log u|\) is tiny
  8. return \(\sigma_i=1/\sqrt{2\beta}\)

4The crowding problem, and why the map uses a heavy tail

Top: distances from a point to 2,000 random neighbours spread evenly in a ball of dimension \(d\) (scaled so the farthest is 1). Bottom: the Gaussian kernel used in the data and the Student-t kernel used on the map, at equal height at distance 0.

In high dimensions there is far more room at medium distance than near the centre. In a \(d\)-dimensional ball the share of volume within radius \(r\) is \(r^d\), so in 50 dimensions half of the volume lies beyond radius \(0.5^{1/50}\approx0.986\): almost every neighbour sits at nearly the same distance. Slide \(d\) up and watch the distances pile up near 1.

A 2-D page has no such room. If the map tried to keep those many moderately-distant neighbours at moderate distance, they would crowd into the middle and squash the clusters together. This is the crowding problem, and it ruined the earlier method SNE, which used Gaussians on both sides.

t-SNE’s fix is the Student-t on the map. Its tail falls like \(1/r^2\) instead of \(e^{-r^2}\), so a moderate similarity in the data is matched by a much larger distance on the map. Clusters get room to separate, which is exactly the clean gaps you see in t-SNE plots, and also why gap sizes are exaggerated.

5UMAP: a fuzzy neighbour graph, laid out

UMAP on the data chosen in section 2, starting from the PCA layout. Turn on the graph to see the weighted neighbour links it is trying to keep (darker = stronger).

UMAP (McInnes, Healy and Melville, 2018) has the same shape of idea, with three changes that make it faster and a little more faithful to global structure.

1. A k-nearest-neighbour graph with local scaling. For each point take its \(k\) nearest neighbours. Let \(\rho_i\) be the distance to the closest one and choose \(\sigma_i\) so that \(\sum_{j}\exp\!\big(-(d_{ij}-\rho_i)/\sigma_i\big)=\log_2 k\). The weight of edge \(i\to j\) is

$$w_{j\mid i}=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{\max(0,\,d_{ij}-\rho_i)}{\sigma_i}\Big),\qquad w_{ij}=w_{j\mid i}+w_{i\mid j}-w_{j\mid i}\,w_{i\mid j}.$$

Subtracting \(\rho_i\) guarantees every point is fully connected to at least one neighbour, so no point is left isolated; the last formula is a fuzzy “or” of the two directions.

2. A curve on the map with a chosen minimum gap. On the page, the similarity of two points at distance \(d\) is \(v(d)=(1+a\,d^{2b})^{-1}\), where \(a,b\) are fitted so the curve is about 1 out to min_dist and then decays (bottom chart). With \(a=b=1\) it is exactly t-SNE’s Student-t.

3. Cross-entropy, optimised by sampling. UMAP minimises \(\sum_{ij}\big[w_{ij}\log\frac{w_{ij}}{v_{ij}}+(1-w_{ij})\log\frac{1-w_{ij}}{1-v_{ij}}\big]\). The first term attracts linked points, the second repels unlinked ones. Instead of summing over all \(n^2\) pairs it samples edges in proportion to their weight and a few random “negative” points per edge, which makes each epoch cost \(O(nk)\).

Algorithm: UMAP
  1. input points, n_neighbors \(k\), min_dist, epochs \(E\)
  2. find each point’s \(k\) nearest neighbours approximate search for big data
  3. \(\rho_i\leftarrow\) nearest distance; binary-search \(\sigma_i\); edge weights \(w_{j\mid i}\)
  4. symmetrise: \(w_{ij}=w_{j\mid i}+w_{i\mid j}-w_{j\mid i}w_{i\mid j}\)
  5. fit \(a,b\) to min_dist; initialise \(y\) (spectral or PCA layout)
  6. for epoch \(=1,\dots,E\) do
  7. for each edge \((i,j)\), with probability \(\propto w_{ij}\): pull \(y_i,y_j\) together
  8. pick a few random points \(m\): push \(y_i\) away from \(y_m\) negative sampling
  9. shrink the step size linearly
  10. return \(y\)
Going deeper: t-SNE versus UMAP, honestly

UMAP is much faster on large data, can embed new points with transform, and can embed into more than two dimensions as a pre-processing step (for example before HDBSCAN). The claim that UMAP preserves global structure better turned out to come mostly from its initialisation: with the same spectral or PCA start, t-SNE keeps global layout about as well (Kobak and Linderman, 2021). Both methods are tools for looking, and both can mislead in the ways shown in the next section.

6Reading the map without being fooled

Three tight clusters in 20-D with very different spreads (one 6× wider than another) and different gaps between them. t-SNE at four perplexities, computed one after another.

The lessons from Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson’s well-known article How to Use t-SNE Effectively (Distill, 2016), reproduced here on our own data:

  1. Cluster sizes mean nothing. Each point gets the same effective number of neighbours, so a wide cluster and a tight one come out about the same size. Here the true spreads differ six-fold; on the maps they barely differ.
  2. Distances between clusters may mean nothing. The gaps depend on perplexity and on the heavy tail, not on the true separation.
  3. Random noise can look like clusters at low perplexity. Try Pure noise in section 2.
  4. Shapes can be invented. Elongated or curved clusters at low perplexity are often artefacts.
  5. Look at more than one setting (and several random seeds) before believing a structure.

A practical habit: colour the map by a variable you trust (a label, a measurement), check that patterns survive changes of perplexity or n_neighbors, and confirm any “cluster” with an analysis in the original space, such as the clustering methods on the previous page.

8The same thing in Python

from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.manifold import TSNE, trustworthiness
X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)                          # 1,797 images, 64 pixels each

X50 = PCA(n_components=50).fit_transform(X)                  # common first step
Y = TSNE(n_components=2, perplexity=30, init="pca", learning_rate="auto",
         random_state=0).fit_transform(X50)
print(trustworthiness(X, Y, n_neighbors=10))                 # 1.0 = neighbourhoods kept

import umap                                                   # pip install umap-learn
