Interpretability: what did the model learn?

A model that predicts well can still be right for the wrong reasons: it may lean on a column that leaks the answer, treat a postcode as a proxy for something it should not use, or break the moment the data drift. Interpretability methods let you look inside, or at least around, a trained model and ask what it relies on. This page builds the classic methods one at a time on a house-price model whose true formula we know, so you can check every explanation against the truth. The next page explains single predictions with SHAP values.

What is interpretability, in plain words?

The idea

Interpretability is how well a person can understand why a model gives the predictions it does. Some models are readable as they stand: a short decision tree or a linear model with a handful of coefficients. Others, such as a forest of 500 trees or a neural network, are effectively black boxes, and we explain them after training by poking them: change an input, watch the output.

Why bother?

  1. Debugging. Find leakage, spurious shortcuts and bugs before users do.
  2. Trust. A doctor, underwriter or loan officer will not act on a number they cannot question.
  3. Rules. Credit and insurance decisions must come with reasons; the EU AI Act asks for transparency in high-risk systems.
  4. Science. Sometimes what the model learned is the finding.

1The running example: a price model we can check

Predicted against actual price for 200 test houses the models never saw. Points on the dashed line are perfect predictions.

Every explanation on this page needs something to explain, and a way to check it. So we make up a world where we know the answer. Six hundred houses each have six features, and the price (in thousands) comes from this formula plus random noise with standard deviation 14:

$$\text{price}=40+1.4\,\text{area}+8\,\text{bedrooms}-60\big(1-e^{-\text{age}/15}\big)-32\log\!\big(1+\tfrac{\text{dist}}{2}\big)\tfrac{\text{area}}{100}+15\,\text{garden}+\varepsilon$$

We train two models on 400 houses: linear regression, readable but unable to bend, and gradient-boosted trees (120 depth-3 trees, see bagging & boosting), flexible but opaque. Switch between them: the boosted model fits better because the true formula curves and interacts. That is the usual trade: accuracy often costs readability, and the rest of the page wins readability back.

2Reading a linear model: coefficients, units and scale

The fitted coefficients with 95% intervals (±1.96 standard errors). Raw: thousands per unit of each feature. Standardised: thousands per standard deviation.

A linear model predicts \(\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\dots+\beta_dx_d\). Each coefficient has a crisp meaning: \(\beta_j\) is the change in the prediction when \(x_j\) rises by one unit and every other feature stays fixed. The garden coefficient near 15 says “a garden adds about 15 thousand”, matching the truth.

But look at the raw bars: floor area gets a tiny coefficient (about 1) and the garden a big one. That does not mean area matters less. Area is measured in square metres, and houses typically differ by about 36 m², so area moves prices by far more than the garden ever can. Coefficients carry the units of their feature, so you cannot rank them as they are.

The fix is to standardise: rescale each feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (a z-score) before fitting, or equivalently multiply each coefficient by its feature's standard deviation:

$$\beta_j^{\text{std}}=\beta_j\,\mathrm{sd}(x_j)\quad=\quad\text{change in }\hat y\text{ for a one-standard-deviation change in }x_j.$$

Now area dominates, as it should. Two warnings remain. The linear model cannot see that the age effect levels off or that distance matters more for big houses, so its coefficients average those patterns away. And the listing code gets a small coefficient whose interval straddles zero: a reminder to read the uncertainty, not only the bar.

Going deeper: multiplicative models, odds ratios and GLM relativities

In a generalised linear model with a log link, used for insurance claim frequency (Poisson) and severity (gamma), \(\log\mathbb E[y]=\beta_0+\sum_j\beta_jx_j\), so \(\mathbb E[y]=e^{\beta_0}\prod_j e^{\beta_jx_j}\). Each \(e^{\beta_j}\) is a relativity: a multiplier on the base rate. A pricing table might read: base claim frequency 0.08 per year; young driver ×1.9; urban postcode ×1.35; telematics discount ×0.85. A young urban telematics driver is priced at \(0.08\times1.9\times1.35\times0.85\approx0.174\).

In logistic regression the link is the logit, so \(e^{\beta_j}\) is an odds ratio: the odds \(p/(1-p)\) are multiplied by \(e^{\beta_j}\) for each unit of \(x_j\). An odds ratio of 2 doubles the odds, which is not the same as doubling the probability unless the probability is small.

Categorical features are coded against a reference level (the base), so every relativity is relative to that level; change the base and every number changes while the predictions do not.

The GLMs for pricing page builds such a rating table from claims data, with exposure offsets, deviance tests and credibility.

3When features move together, coefficients wobble

Each dot is one fit of the linear model on a fresh bootstrap resample of 150 houses. Horizontal: the area coefficient. Vertical: the bedrooms coefficient. Here prices are made purely linear in the features, so the cross marks the true values exactly (1.4 and 8).

“Every other feature stays fixed” is the catch in the definition of a coefficient. Bedrooms and floor area rise together: in the data you almost never see a big house with one bedroom. So the data say very little about the effect of a bedroom holding area fixed, and the fit can trade one coefficient against the other with almost no change in the predictions.

Slide the noise down so bedrooms follow area more tightly. The cloud stretches into a long diagonal: when the area coefficient comes out high, the bedrooms coefficient comes out low, and vice versa. Individual coefficients become unstable, can even flip sign, yet the predictions barely change. This is collinearity, measured by the variance inflation factor

$$\mathrm{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2},$$

where \(R_j^2\) is how well the other features predict feature \(j\). A VIF of 10 means the variance of \(\hat\beta_j\) is ten times what it would be with uncorrelated features. Remedies: drop or combine one of the pair (price per m², rooms per 100 m²), use ridge regression, which spreads the weight evenly and steadies the cloud, or explain the pair as a group.

Going deeper: why the variance blows up

Least squares gives \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\). With two standardised features of correlation \(\rho\) and \(n\) rows, \(X^\top X=n\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\), whose inverse is \(\frac{1}{n(1-\rho^2)}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\rho\\-\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\). So each coefficient's variance is \(\sigma^2/\big(n(1-\rho^2)\big)\), the VIF \(1/(1-\rho^2)\) times the uncorrelated case, and the two estimates have correlation \(-\rho\): that is the downward tilt of the cloud. In eigenvector terms, \(X^\top X\) has a tiny eigenvalue \(n(1-\rho)\) along the direction “more area, fewer bedrooms”, the one direction the data barely explore.

This problem is not special to linear models. Every method on this and the next page struggles when features are strongly correlated; section 6 shows the tree-model version.

4Which features matter? Impurity versus permutation importance

Violet: impurity importance (MDI), read off the trees on the training data. Orange: permutation importance, the drop in test R² when one column is shuffled (whiskers: ±1 sd over 5 shuffles). Both scaled so the largest bar is 1.

Feature importance gives each feature one number for how much the model relies on it. Tree ensembles offer one for free. Every split reduces the squared error (or Gini impurity) of its node; add up those reductions for each feature over all splits and all trees, and you get the mean decrease in impurity (MDI), the feature_importances_ of scikit-learn.

MDI has a known bias. It is measured on the training data, and a deep tree can always find a split on a feature with many distinct values that reduces training error a little, even if the feature is noise. Look at the random forest: the listing code, a random number, gets more MDI than the garden, which really adds 15 thousand. The bias favours continuous and high-cardinality features (IDs, postcodes, timestamps).

Permutation importance asks a cleaner question on test data: if this column were scrambled, so its link to the price was broken but its distribution kept, how much worse would the model do?

Algorithm: permutation importance
  1. input fitted model \(f\), test data \((X,y)\), score \(s\) (here \(R^2\)), repeats \(K\)
  2. \(s_0\leftarrow s(f,X,y)\) baseline score
  3. for each feature \(j\) do
  4. for \(k=1\) to \(K\) do
  5. \(\quad X^{(k)}\leftarrow X\) with column \(j\) randomly shuffled
  6. \(\quad d_{jk}\leftarrow s_0-s(f,X^{(k)},y)\) how much worse?
  7. \(I_j\leftarrow\frac1K\sum_kd_{jk}\)
  8. return \(I_1,\dots,I_d\) near zero (or negative) means unused

The noise feature now scores about zero, and the garden is ranked fairly. Permutation importance works for any model, costs only predictions, and is on the same scale as the metric you care about. Its weak spots: shuffling one of two correlated features creates impossible houses (a 200 m² one-bedroom), and the importance gets shared between the pair, so each looks less important than the pair is. Shuffle correlated features together, or use drop-column importance (retrain without the feature), which is honest but costs one refit per feature.

5Partial dependence and ICE: how the prediction moves with one feature

Thin lines: one ICE curve per house (40 houses), coloured by floor area, small to large: ● to ●. Thick: the partial dependence (their average). Dashed: the same average for the true formula.

Importance says how much. To see how, take one house, slide one of its features across its range while holding everything else fixed, and record the prediction. That curve is an individual conditional expectation (ICE) curve. Do it for many houses and average the curves, and you get the partial dependence (PD) of the model on that feature:

$$\mathrm{PD}_j(v)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\big(v,\;x_{i,-j}\big),$$

where \(x_{i,-j}\) means house \(i\)'s values for every feature except \(j\).

Algorithm: partial dependence and ICE curves
  1. input model \(f\), data rows \(x_1..x_n\), feature \(j\), grid \(v_1<\dots<v_G\)
  2. for each row \(i\) do
  3. for each grid value \(v_g\) do
  4. \(\quad\)copy \(x_i\), set its feature \(j\) to \(v_g\), predict: \(\mathrm{ICE}_i(v_g)\)
  5. for each \(v_g\): \(\mathrm{PD}(v_g)\leftarrow\) average of \(\mathrm{ICE}_i(v_g)\) over \(i\)
  6. return the ICE curves and the PD curve

Try age: the boosted model has learned the fast early drop that levels off, close to the dashed truth, while the linear model can only draw a straight line. Now try distance. The ICE lines fan out: big houses (red) lose much more with distance than small ones (blue). When ICE curves are not parallel, the feature interacts with others, and the PD average hides it. Tick c-ICE to line the curves up at the left edge, which makes the fan easy to see. The listing code gives flat lines: the boosted model has learned to ignore it.

Going deeper: what PD estimates, and when it is causal

PD estimates \(\mathbb E_{X_{-j}}[f(v,X_{-j})]\): the model's average prediction when feature \(j\) is set to \(v\) and the others keep their own distribution. Zhao and Hastie (2021) show this equals the causal effect of \(x_j\) on the model output (always true, by construction) and on the real-world outcome only if the other features block every back-door path (no unmeasured confounders) and the model is accurate. Being an average of the model at points that may never occur, it also inherits the problem of the next section.

6Correlated features: where PD makes up data, and ALE

Left: the training data (\(x_1\), \(x_2\)) and, in red, the points PD evaluates to get its value at \(x_1=0.8\). Right: the effect of \(x_1\) estimated by PD and by ALE for boosted trees trained on \(y=x_1+2x_2^2+\text{noise}\); the true effect is the dashed line of slope 1.

To get the PD value at \(x_1=0.8\), we pair 0.8 with every house's \(x_2\), including houses whose \(x_2\) is −0.9. If the two features are correlated, a point with \(x_1=0.8\) and \(x_2=-0.9\) never occurs in the data. A tree model has never seen anything there, so its prediction in that corner is whatever the nearest leaf says: an extrapolation. PD averages those made-up values in as if they were real.

Raise the correlation: the red points leave the cloud, and the PD slope drifts away from the true slope of 1. Accumulated local effects (ALE, Apley and Zhu) avoid the empty regions. Cut \(x_1\) into narrow bins. Within a bin, take only the houses that really fall there, move each one from the bin's left edge to its right edge, and average the change in prediction. Then add up (accumulate) these local changes from left to right:

$$\mathrm{ALE}_j(v)=\sum_{k=1}^{k(v)}\frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i:\,x_{ij}\in\text{bin }k}\Big[f(z_k,x_{i,-j})-f(z_{k-1},x_{i,-j})\Big]\;-\;\text{constant}.$$
Algorithm: accumulated local effects (ALE)
  1. input model \(f\), data, feature \(j\), number of bins \(K\)
  2. bin edges \(z_0<\dots<z_K\) at quantiles of \(x_j\) equal counts per bin
  3. for each bin \(k\) do
  4. for each row \(i\) with \(x_{ij}\) in bin \(k\): \(\Delta_i\leftarrow f(z_k,x_{i,-j})-f(z_{k-1},x_{i,-j})\)
  5. \(\delta_k\leftarrow\) average of \(\Delta_i\) only real neighbours used
  6. \(\mathrm{ALE}(z_k)\leftarrow\delta_1+\dots+\delta_k\) accumulate
  7. return ALE minus its average, so it is centred on 0

ALE stays near the truth because every evaluation is close to real data. It is also cheaper than PD. The price: it is harder to explain to a non-specialist, and it has no per-house version like ICE. At very high correlation, no method can tell the two features' effects apart, because the data contain no house where they disagree; the model itself may have divided the credit arbitrarily.

7Global surrogates: a small tree that imitates the big model

A single regression tree trained to predict the boosted model's predictions (not the real prices). Fidelity is how well it imitates the model, as R². The chart shows fidelity at every depth; the tree below is the one you picked.

A global surrogate is an interpretable model trained to mimic a black box. Feed the training inputs to the black box, keep its predictions as labels, and fit a short tree or a sparse linear model to them. Then read the surrogate.

At depth 2 the surrogate already tells the boosted model's main story: floor area first, and then, for big houses only, distance to the centre, which is the interaction from section 5 showing up on its own. Fidelity climbs from about 0.5 at depth 1 to about 0.7 at depth 2 and 0.9 by depth 4, but each extra level doubles the number of leaves: fidelity is bought with readability. Always report fidelity; a surrogate that imitates the model with R² of 0.6 explains a different model, not yours.

Or skip the black box

For tabular data, a model that is interpretable by design often costs little accuracy. A generalised additive model (GAM) predicts \(\beta_0+\sum_jf_j(x_j)\), a sum of one curve per feature, so its “explanation” is just those curves, with no approximation. Explainable boosting machines (EBMs, in Microsoft's InterpretML) fit each \(f_j\) with boosted shallow trees and add a few chosen pairwise terms \(f_{jk}(x_j,x_k)\); on many tabular benchmarks they come close to unrestricted boosting. Rudin (2019) argues that for high-stakes decisions this should be the first thing to try. In insurance pricing, the GLM with relativities from section 2 is exactly such a model.

Habits that keep explanations honest

  • Explain on held-out data, and check the model is accurate first: explaining a bad model explains nothing.
  • Use two methods that work differently; trust what they agree on.
  • Check correlations before reading any single feature's effect.
  • Report uncertainty: repeat shuffles, bootstrap the data.

The Rashomon effect

Breiman (2001) noticed that many different models fit the same data almost equally well, and they can tell different stories about which features matter. An explanation describes this model, not the world. If a conclusion matters, check that it holds across several good models.
