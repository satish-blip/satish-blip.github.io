L1 and L2 Regularisation

A flexible model can fit the training data perfectly and still predict new data badly. Regularisation adds a price for complexity, usually the size of the weights, so the model only uses a large weight when the data really demands it. The two classic prices, L1 and L2, behave very differently: one shrinks everything smoothly, the other switches features off.

What is regularisation, in plain words?

The problem

Fit a degree-8 polynomial through 25 noisy points that really come from a gentle sine wave. With 9 coefficients to play with, the curve can bend through almost every point, and it does: it wiggles wildly between them and predicts nonsense for any new \(x\). Looking at the fitted coefficients you see huge numbers with alternating signs (+3000, −8000, +7000…) that nearly cancel. Large, finely balanced weights are the fingerprint of overfitting.

We could use a simpler model, but often we don't know in advance how simple. Regularisation keeps the flexible model and instead discourages it from using large weights unless the data really insists.

The idea

Training normally minimises the loss alone. Regularisation minimises the loss plus a penalty on the weights:

$$\underbrace{L(w)}_{\text{fit the data}}\;+\;\lambda\,\underbrace{R(w)}_{\text{keep it simple}}$$

The knob \(\lambda\) sets the exchange rate. At \(\lambda=0\) you get the unregularised fit, which may overfit. As \(\lambda\) grows the weights are pulled towards zero and the model becomes smoother, until it underfits. The best \(\lambda\) is found with a validation set or cross-validation.

How it works

  1. Standardise the features, so one penalty is fair to all of them.
  2. Pick a penalty: L2 \(\tfrac12\sum w_j^2\) (ridge), L1 \(\sum|w_j|\) (lasso), or a mix (elastic net).
  3. Minimise loss + \(\lambda\times\)penalty. Leave the intercept unpenalised.
  4. Try a range of \(\lambda\) values and keep the one with the lowest validation error.

An everyday picture

Packing for a flight with a baggage fee. L2 is a fee that grows with the square of each bag’s weight: you bring everything, but lighter. L1 is a flat fee per kilo: it is cheapest to leave whole items at home, so some items go to exactly zero.

Good at
  • Cutting variance and overfitting
  • L1: automatic feature selection, sparse models
  • L2: stable with correlated features, keeps problems well-conditioned
Watch out for
  • Unscaled features get penalised unfairly
  • Too much \(\lambda\): underfitting
  • L1 picks one of a group of correlated features somewhat arbitrarily

1Diamond versus circle

Two weights \(w_1,w_2\). The ellipses are contours of the data loss, centred on the unregularised solution \(\hat w\) (drag it). The shaded shape is every \(w\) with the same penalty as the regularised solution \(w^\star\) (dot). The curve is the path of \(w^\star\) as \(\lambda\) goes from 0 to large.

Penalty

Why L1 gives zeros. The solution is where the smallest loss ellipse touches the penalty shape. A circle is smooth, so the touching point is almost never on an axis: both weights shrink but stay non-zero. The L1 diamond has corners on the axes, and an ellipse coming from most directions hits a corner first, which means one weight is exactly 0.

Drag \(\hat w\) around with L1 selected and watch \(w^\star\) snap onto an axis. Switch to L2 and it slides smoothly instead. Elastic net (half L1, half L2) has rounded corners: it still produces zeros, but behaves more stably when features are correlated.

Why "same penalty" shapes? Minimising loss \(+\lambda R(w)\) is equivalent to minimising the loss alone inside a budget \(R(w)\le t\), for some \(t\) that depends on \(\lambda\). The shaded shape is that budget. So the picture asks: what is the lowest point of the loss bowl that still fits inside the budget?

Going deeper: the zero condition for lasso (subgradients)

\(|w|\) has no derivative at 0, but it has a subgradient: any number in \([-1,1]\). The optimality condition for \(\tfrac12\lVert y-Xw\rVert^2+\lambda\lVert w\rVert_1\) is that zero lies in the subdifferential, coordinate by coordinate: \(x_j^\top(y-Xw)=\lambda\,\mathrm{sign}(w_j)\) if \(w_j\ne0\), and \(|x_j^\top(y-Xw)|\le\lambda\) if \(w_j=0\). In words: a weight may stay at exactly zero as long as its feature's correlation with the residual is no bigger than \(\lambda\). A whole interval of situations gives \(w_j=0\), which is why zeros are common. For ridge the condition is \(x_j^\top(y-Xw)=\lambda w_j\), which gives \(w_j=0\) only when the correlation is exactly zero: a measure-zero event.

Choosing \(\lambda\) honestly

The test curve above is a luxury: in real life we do not have the true function. We must estimate the test error from the training data itself, and the standard way is \(K\)-fold cross-validation. Note that the training error is useless for choosing \(\lambda\): it always prefers \(\lambda=0\).

Algorithm: choosing λ by K-fold cross-validation
  1. input data, a grid \(\lambda_1>\lambda_2>\dots>\lambda_m\) on a log scale, folds \(K\) (often 5 or 10)
  2. split the data at random into \(K\) equal folds
  3. for each \(\lambda\) in the grid do
  4. for \(k=1\) to \(K\) do
  5. fit the scaler and the model on all folds except \(k\) never let fold k leak into scaling
  6. \(e_k\leftarrow\) error on fold \(k\)
  7. \(\mathrm{CV}(\lambda)\leftarrow\) mean of \(e_1..e_K\); \(\mathrm{SE}(\lambda)\leftarrow\) their standard error
  8. \(\lambda^\star\leftarrow\arg\min\mathrm{CV}(\lambda)\) or the largest λ within one SE: the "1-SE rule", simpler model
  9. refit on all the data with \(\lambda^\star\); return the model
Going deeper: ridge as shrinkage along principal directions

Ridge has a closed form, \(\hat w_\lambda=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\), and the SVD \(X=UDV^\top\) shows what it does. The fitted values are

$$X\hat w_\lambda=\sum_j u_j\,\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\,u_j^\top y .$$

Without the penalty every factor is 1 (plain least squares projects \(y\) onto the column space). With it, the component of \(y\) along each principal direction is multiplied by \(d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)\): directions with large variance in the data (large \(d_j\)) are barely touched; directions with little variance, where the data say almost nothing and the estimate is noisy, are shrunk hard. That is the precise sense in which ridge reduces variance. Adding \(\lambda I\) also makes \(X^\top X+\lambda I\) invertible even when features are perfectly correlated, which is why ridge is "stable with correlated features". The effective number of parameters is \(\mathrm{df}(\lambda)=\sum_jd_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)\), sliding from \(d\) down to 0.

3Shrink or switch off: what each penalty does to one weight

ridge \(z/(1+\lambda)\)lasso \(S_\lambda(z)\)best subset (L0) hard threshold- - no penalty

With uncorrelated, standardised features each weight can be solved on its own. If \(z\) is the unregularised weight, the regularised one is:

$$\text{L2: } w=\frac{z}{1+\lambda}\qquad\text{L1: } w=S_\lambda(z)=\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(0,|z|-\lambda)$$

Ridge multiplies every weight by the same factor: big and small weights all shrink, none become zero. Lasso subtracts a fixed amount, and anything smaller than \(\lambda\) becomes exactly zero: this is the soft-thresholding operator. The L0 penalty (count of non-zero weights) keeps or kills each weight whole, but makes the problem combinatorial.

When features are correlated there is no per-weight formula, but the same operator is applied repeatedly: coordinate descent (scikit-learn’s Lasso) or proximal gradient descent (used on the logistic regression page for L1):

$$w\leftarrow S_{\eta\lambda}\big(w-\eta\nabla L(w)\big)$$

Read it as two moves: take an ordinary gradient step on the data loss, then apply the penalty's own "pull towards zero" exactly. For L2 the pull would be a multiplication by \(1/(1+\eta\lambda)\), which is precisely weight decay.

Algorithm: proximal gradient descent (ISTA) for any loss + L1
  1. input differentiable loss \(L\), penalty \(\lambda\), step \(\eta\le1/\)(Lipschitz constant of \(\nabla L\))
  2. \(w\leftarrow0\)
  3. repeat
  4. \(u\leftarrow w-\eta\nabla L(w)\) gradient step ignoring the penalty
  5. for each \(j\): \(w_j\leftarrow\operatorname{sign}(u_j)\max(|u_j|-\eta\lambda,0)\) soft-threshold
  6. until \(w\) stops changing
  7. return \(w\)
Going deeper: deriving the soft-threshold

For one standardised feature the problem is \(\min_w\tfrac12(w-z)^2+\lambda|w|\). If \(w>0\), the derivative is \(w-z+\lambda=0\), so \(w=z-\lambda\), which is only consistent when \(z>\lambda\). If \(w<0\), \(w=z+\lambda\), consistent when \(z<-\lambda\). Otherwise (\(|z|\le\lambda\)) neither side works and the minimum sits at the kink, \(w=0\): the subgradient \(\lambda\cdot[-1,1]\) is wide enough to absorb \(z\). Together these three cases are \(S_\lambda(z)\). For L2, \(\min\tfrac12(w-z)^2+\tfrac\lambda2w^2\) is smooth: \(w-z+\lambda w=0\Rightarrow w=z/(1+\lambda)\).

4The Bayesian view: a penalty is a prior

Suppose before seeing any data you believe weights are probably small. That belief is a prior \(p(w)\). Bayes’ rule combines it with the likelihood, and the most probable weights (the MAP estimate) are

$$\hat w_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\max_w\ p(\text{data}\mid w)\,p(w)=\arg\min_w\ \underbrace{-\log p(\text{data}\mid w)}_{\text{loss}}\ \underbrace{-\log p(w)}_{\text{penalty}}$$
  • Gaussian prior \(p(w_j)\propto e^{-w_j^2/2\tau^2}\) gives \(-\log p(w)=\frac1{2\tau^2}\sum w_j^2\): L2, with \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\).
  • Laplace prior \(p(w_j)\propto e^{-|w_j|/b}\) gives \(\frac1b\sum|w_j|\): L1.

The Laplace prior has a sharp peak at exactly zero, a strong belief that many weights are exactly zero, which is where sparsity comes from. A wider prior (larger \(\tau\) or \(b\)) means weaker regularisation.

This view also explains why \(\lambda\) should shrink as data grows: the prior is fixed, but the likelihood term is a sum over \(n\) examples, so with more data the evidence outweighs the prior belief. See Bayesian updating for the same effect with a coin.

Going deeper: deriving ridge as MAP for linear regression

Model \(y_i=x_i^\top w+\varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\), and prior \(w_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) independently. Then \(-\log p(y\mid w)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert y-Xw\rVert^2+\)const and \(-\log p(w)=\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert w\rVert^2+\)const. Multiply the sum by \(2\sigma^2\) (which does not move the minimiser): \(\lVert y-Xw\rVert^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\lVert w\rVert^2\). That is ridge with \(\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2\): noisy data (large \(\sigma\)) or a confident prior (small \(\tau\)) mean stronger shrinkage. In this Gaussian case the whole posterior is Gaussian, \(w\mid y\sim\mathcal N\big((X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y,\ \sigma^2(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}\big)\), so the MAP is also the posterior mean.

Gaussian prior → L2Laplace prior → L1

Both priors have variance 1. The Laplace puts more mass right at zero and in the tails: it prefers "mostly zero, a few large" weights.

6Regularising a neural network

Three identical networks (2 → 32 → 32 → 1, tanh, Adam) trained on only 60 noisy points, differing only in the penalty strength. Test accuracy is on 400 fresh points from the same distribution.

What to look for: with no penalty the boundary wraps tightly around individual noisy points (train accuracy near 100%, lower test accuracy). A moderate penalty gives a smoother boundary that generalises better. Too much and the network can barely bend. In Adam, adding \(\lambda w\) to the gradient (as here) is not quite the same as weight decay, because Adam rescales it; AdamW applies the decay \(w\leftarrow w-\eta\lambda w\) separately, which is what most transformer training uses.
