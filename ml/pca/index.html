Principal Component Analysis

Real data often has many features that move together: a person's height and arm span, a house's floor area and number of rooms. That means the data really lives near a lower-dimensional shape, and we can describe each point with fewer numbers. PCA finds the directions along which the data varies most, and lets you keep only the top few. Rotate a direction by hand until the projected variance peaks (that is PC1), see why that same direction minimises reconstruction error, spin a 3D cloud, and rebuild images from a handful of eigen-images.

What is PCA, in plain words?

The problem

Suppose each student in a class is described by marks in 10 subjects. Plotting 10 dimensions is impossible, and many of those marks are strongly related (good at physics usually means good at maths). Is there a way to summarise each student with, say, two numbers that keep most of the differences between students?

The idea

Principal component analysis finds the directions in which your data varies the most. The first principal component is the single line that captures the biggest spread of the data (for the students, something like "overall ability"); the second is the next biggest, at right angles to the first (perhaps "science versus humanities"), and so on.

Keeping only the first few components gives a smaller description of each point that loses as little as possible: dimensionality reduction. It is used to compress images, remove noise and plot high-dimensional data in 2-D. PCA does not use labels: it is unsupervised.

How it works

  1. Centre the data (and usually scale each feature).
  2. Compute the covariance matrix.
  3. Find its eigenvectors (directions) and eigenvalues (variance along each).
  4. Keep the top \(k\) directions and project the data onto them; the explained variance tells you how much you kept.

An everyday picture

Photographing a teapot: some camera angles show its shape clearly (spout, handle, lid), others hide it. A photo is a projection from 3-D to 2-D, and PCA picks the angle whose photo shows the most spread, so that points that were far apart stay far apart.

Good at
  • Fast, exact and simple
  • Removes correlated, redundant features
  • Great for visualisation and compression
Watch out for
  • Only finds straight-line structure
  • Directions of high variance are not always the ones that separate classes (compare LDA)
  • Sensitive to feature scaling

2The maths

You just found PC1 by eye. Now we find it exactly, in three steps: write the spread along a direction as a formula, maximise it, and check that the same direction loses the least information.

Step 1: the spread along a direction

First centre the data so the cloud sits at the origin: \(\tilde x_i=x_i-\bar x\). For a unit vector \(u\), the coordinate of point \(i\) along \(u\) is the dot product \(u^\top\tilde x_i\). These coordinates have mean zero, so their variance is the average of their squares. Collecting terms gives a compact formula with the sample covariance \(S=\tfrac1{n-1}\sum_i\tilde x_i\tilde x_i^\top\):

$$\frac1{n-1}\sum_i(u^\top\tilde x_i)^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_iu^\top\tilde x_i\tilde x_i^\top u=u^\top Su$$

So the covariance matrix \(S\) contains everything we need: give it any direction, and \(u^\top Su\) tells you the spread along it.

Step 2: make that spread as large as possible

We need the constraint \(\lVert u\rVert=1\), otherwise making \(u\) longer would make the "variance" as large as we like. Maximise with a Lagrange multiplier:

$$\max_{u}\ u^\top Su\quad\text{s.t.}\quad u^\top u=1\qquad\mathcal L=u^\top Su-\lambda(u^\top u-1)$$
$$\nabla_u\mathcal L=2Su-2\lambda u=0\ \Longrightarrow\ Su=\lambda u,\qquad u^\top Su=\lambda$$

So \(u\) must be an eigenvector of \(S\): a direction that \(S\) only stretches, without turning. And the variance along it is the eigenvalue. The best is the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue: PC1. PC2 is the best direction orthogonal to PC1, which is the next eigenvector, and so on. Because \(S\) is symmetric, its eigenvectors are automatically at right angles.

Algorithm: PCA
  1. input data matrix \(X\) (\(n\) rows, \(d\) features), number of components \(k\)
  2. \(\bar x\leftarrow\) column means; \(\tilde X\leftarrow X-\mathbf 1\bar x^\top\) centre
  3. optionally divide each column by its standard deviation when units differ
  4. \(\tilde X=U\Sigma V^\top\) thin SVD; or eigen-decompose S = X̃ᵀX̃/(n−1)
  5. \(W_k\leftarrow\) first \(k\) columns of \(V\) principal directions
  6. \(\lambda_j\leftarrow\sigma_j^2/(n-1)\) variance along each
  7. \(Z\leftarrow\tilde XW_k\) n × k scores: the compressed data
  8. \(\hat X\leftarrow\mathbf 1\bar x^\top+ZW_k^\top\) reconstruction
  9. return \(W_k\), \(Z\), explained ratio \(\sum_{j\le k}\lambda_j/\sum_j\lambda_j\)

Step 3: equivalently, minimise reconstruction error

A different goal leads to the same answer. If we keep only the coordinate along \(u\), our best guess for the point is its projection \(uu^\top\tilde x_i\), and the red segment is what we lose:

$$\frac1n\sum_i\big\lVert\tilde x_i-uu^\top\tilde x_i\big\rVert^2=\frac1n\sum_i\lVert\tilde x_i\rVert^2-\frac1n\sum_i(u^\top\tilde x_i)^2$$

By Pythagoras, total = captured + lost: the projection and the red segment are at right angles. The total is fixed by the data, so the direction that captures most also loses least. That is the bar above.

Keeping k components

$$z_i=W_k^\top\tilde x_i\in\mathbb R^k,\qquad \hat x_i=\bar x+W_kz_i,\qquad \text{explained variance}=\frac{\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k}{\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_d}$$

\(W_k\) holds the top \(k\) eigenvectors as columns. \(z_i\) is the short description of point \(i\) (its \(k\) "scores"), and \(\hat x_i\) is the best rebuild from it. The explained-variance ratio tells you what fraction of the spread you kept; a common rule is to keep enough components for 90–95%.

In practice: SVD

With the centred data matrix \(\tilde X=U\Sigma V^\top\), the columns of \(V\) are the principal directions and \(\lambda_j=\sigma_j^2/(n-1)\). SVD is more stable than forming \(S\), because forming \(\tilde X^\top\tilde X\) squares the condition number. This page uses the Jacobi eigenvalue method on \(S\), which is fine for small \(d\).

Going deeper: why the top-k eigenvectors are optimal together (Eckart–Young)

For \(k\) components, maximise \(\operatorname{tr}(W^\top SW)\) over \(d\times k\) matrices with orthonormal columns. Write \(S=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) and \(P=Q^\top W\) (still orthonormal columns). Then \(\operatorname{tr}(W^\top SW)=\sum_j\lambda_j\,c_j\) with \(c_j=\sum_l P_{jl}^2\). Each \(c_j\in[0,1]\) (a row of a matrix with orthonormal columns has norm at most 1) and \(\sum_jc_j=k\). A weighted sum of the \(\lambda_j\) with weights in \([0,1]\) summing to \(k\) is largest when the weights are 1 on the \(k\) largest eigenvalues, which happens when \(W\) spans the top \(k\) eigenvectors. The maximum is \(\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k\).

The same fact in matrix form is the Eckart–Young theorem: the best rank-\(k\) approximation of \(\tilde X\) in Frobenius norm is \(U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top\), with error \(\sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2+\dots+\sigma_r^2}\). PCA and the truncated SVD are the same thing seen from two sides. See the SVD page.

Scale matters. PCA chases variance, so a feature measured in large units dominates. A salary in rupees will swamp an age in years. Standardise first when features have different units (tick the box above with a stretched cloud to see PC1 jump).

5PCA vs LDA: unsupervised vs supervised directions

PCA direction (ignores labels)LDA direction (uses labels)

PCA never looks at the labels. It picks the direction of largest spread, which can be the worst one for classification when the classes are separated along a thin direction. LDA picks the direction that separates the class means relative to the spread (see the LDA page). Top histogram: projections onto PC1. Bottom: onto the LDA direction. Lesson: if you plan to classify, don't blindly throw away low-variance components; they may hold the signal.

6The same thing in Python

from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

pca = PCA(n_components=2).fit(StandardScaler().fit_transform(X))
pca.components_                   # rows = principal directions (eigenvectors of S)
pca.explained_variance_           # eigenvalues lambda_j
pca.explained_variance_ratio_     # scree plot
Z = pca.transform(X); X_hat = pca.inverse_transform(Z)

# by hand
Xc = X - X.mean(0)
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)
eigvals = s**2 / (len(X) - 1); W = Vt[:k].T; Z = Xc @ W
