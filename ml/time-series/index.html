Time series: forecasting without peeking at the future

Sales next month, electricity demand tomorrow, claims next quarter. Forecasting looks like ordinary regression, but the rows are in order, the future is not allowed in, and yesterday is usually the best clue to today. This page builds the classic toolkit on one series you can reshape: decomposition, honest validation, lag features, exponential smoothing, ARIMA and gradient-boosted trees.

What is time-series forecasting, in plain words?

The problem

A time series is a sequence of measurements in time order: \(y_1,y_2,\dots,y_T\). We want \(y_{T+1},\dots,y_{T+h}\), the next \(h\) values, often with a range of likely values. Unlike most datasets the rows are not independent: this month looks a lot like last month and like the same month last year.

The idea

Split the series into parts that each have a simple story, then extend each story forward:

  1. Trend: the slow drift up or down.
  2. Seasonality: a pattern that repeats every \(m\) steps (12 months, 7 days, 24 hours).
  3. Remainder: what is left, often with short-term memory (autocorrelation).

Statistical models (exponential smoothing, ARIMA) write these parts as equations. Machine-learning models (boosted trees, neural networks) learn them from lag features, the past values placed beside each row.

An everyday picture

Guessing how busy a café will be next Saturday. You know it has been getting busier this year (trend), Saturdays beat Tuesdays (season), and last Saturday was unusually busy because of a festival (remainder, which probably will not repeat). A good guess combines all three, and an honest test of your guessing would only ever use what you knew before each Saturday.

Good at
  • Demand, sales and stock planning
  • Energy load, traffic, web visits
  • Claims and reserving patterns
  • Capacity and budget planning
Watch out for
  • Random splits leak the future
  • Features you will not know at forecast time
  • Trees cannot extrapolate a trend
  • Breaks: a pandemic, a price change, a new product

1Trend, season and remainder

Twelve years of monthly data (made on this page). Top: the series and its trend estimate. Bottom: the seasonal pattern (bars, one per month) and the remainder (line). The last two years, shaded, are kept back as the future for every model below.

Write the series as a sum, an additive decomposition:

$$y_t=T_t+S_t+R_t,\qquad S_{t+m}=S_t .$$

The classical recipe estimates each part in turn. The trend is a centred moving average over one full season, which cancels the seasonal pattern; for even \(m=12\) it is a “2×12” average, \(\hat T_t=\tfrac{1}{24}y_{t-6}+\tfrac{1}{12}\sum_{j=-5}^{5}y_{t+j}+\tfrac{1}{24}y_{t+6}\). The seasonal index for each month is the average of \(y_t-\hat T_t\) over all years with that month, shifted to sum to zero. What is left is the remainder.

If the seasonal swings grow with the level (as with airline passengers), use a multiplicative form \(y_t=T_t\times S_t\times R_t\), or take logs first, which turns it into an additive one. Modern decompositions such as STL (Cleveland et al., 1990) use local regression so the season can change slowly.

2Honest splits: why random cross-validation lies

Each row is one validation fold, each cell one month: purple = training, orange = validation, grey = unused. Switch the scheme. The model (3-nearest-neighbour regression on the date and the month) and the data are the same in both.

Ordinary cross-validation shuffles rows into folds. On a time series that means the model is trained on March and May and tested on April: it interpolates between neighbours it has already seen. In real use it must extrapolate past the last date it knows. The random-split error is therefore much too optimistic, and models that are good at interpolation win the comparison for the wrong reason.

Compare the three numbers below the chart: the random 5-fold error, the time-ordered error, and the error on the two held-back years. Only the time-ordered scheme comes close to the truth.

The fix is to keep time order (TimeSeriesSplit in scikit-learn):

  • Expanding window: train on everything up to a cut-off, validate on the next \(h\) steps, move the cut-off forward.
  • Sliding window: same, but train only on the most recent \(w\) steps, useful when old data are no longer representative.
  • Leave a gap between training and validation if features use the recent past that would not be available yet (for example, a 7-day rolling mean published with a delay).

The same rule covers every preprocessing step: scalers, imputers and target encoders must be fitted on past rows only, exactly as on the datasets page.

Algorithm: rolling-origin evaluation (time-series cross-validation)
  1. input series \(y_1..y_T\), first cut-off \(t_0\), horizon \(h\), step \(s\)
  2. for cut-off \(c=t_0,\ t_0+s,\ \dots\) while \(c+h\le T\) do
  3. fit the model (and every preprocessing step) on \(y_1..y_c\) only
  4. forecast \(\hat y_{c+1},\dots,\hat y_{c+h}\)
  5. record the errors \(y_{c+j}-\hat y_{c+j}\) for \(j=1..h\)
  6. return the error averaged over cut-offs, for each horizon \(j\)

3Lags, rolling windows and autocorrelation

Autocorrelation of the training series at lags 1 to 36. Bars outside the dashed band (±1.96/√T) are larger than chance. Differencing removes the trend and the season; watch the slow decay and the spikes at 12, 24 and 36 disappear.

The autocorrelation at lag \(k\) is the correlation between the series and itself shifted by \(k\) steps:

$$r_k=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-k}(y_t-\bar y)(y_{t+k}-\bar y)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t-\bar y)^2}.$$

A trend makes every \(r_k\) large and slowly decaying; a season adds spikes at multiples of \(m\). Most forecasting models want a stationary series, one whose mean, variance and autocorrelations do not change over time. Differencing gets you there: \(\nabla y_t=y_t-y_{t-1}\) removes a linear trend and \(\nabla_{12}y_t=y_t-y_{t-12}\) removes a fixed season.

For a machine-learning model we turn the series into an ordinary table. Each row is a time \(t\); the target is \(y_t\); the features are things known before \(t\):

  • Lags: \(y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, y_{t-12}\)…, chosen where the autocorrelation is large.
  • Rolling statistics of the past: mean, maximum or standard deviation of \(y_{t-1},\dots,y_{t-w}\). The window must end at \(t-1\), never at \(t\).
  • Calendar features: month, day of week, holidays (known in advance, so safe).
  • Known future inputs: planned prices or promotions, but not weather you will only learn tomorrow.

4Exponential smoothing and Holt–Winters

Black: the series. Purple: one-step-ahead fitted values on the training years, then the forecast for the two held-back years with an approximate 95% band. Orange: what actually happened.

The simplest sensible forecast is a weighted average of the past in which recent values count more. Simple exponential smoothing keeps one number, the level \(\ell_t\), and updates it after each observation:

$$\ell_t=\alpha y_t+(1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \ell_t=\sum_{j\ge0}\alpha(1-\alpha)^j\,y_{t-j}.$$

Weights fall off geometrically, hence the name. \(\alpha\) near 1 follows the latest value; near 0 it averages a long history. Holt added a trend \(b_t\), and Winters a season \(s_t\) of period \(m\). The additive Holt–Winters equations:

$$\begin{aligned}\ell_t&=\alpha\,(y_t-s_{t-m})+(1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1})\\ b_t&=\beta\,(\ell_t-\ell_{t-1})+(1-\beta)\,b_{t-1}\\ s_t&=\gamma\,(y_t-\ell_t)+(1-\gamma)\,s_{t-m}\\ \hat y_{t+h\mid t}&=\ell_t+h\,b_t+s_{t+h-m}\end{aligned}$$

Each line is the same idea: blend the newest evidence with the old estimate. The forecast extends the last level along the last trend and adds the matching seasonal value. The smoothing weights are chosen to minimise the squared one-step errors on the training data; press the button to search a grid. In the modern “ETS” view (Hyndman et al., 2002) these are state-space models with a likelihood, which gives proper prediction intervals and lets AIC choose between additive, multiplicative and damped versions.

Algorithm: Holt–Winters (additive) forecasting
  1. input series \(y_1..y_T\), period \(m\), weights \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\), horizon \(h\)
  2. initialise \(\ell_0\) = mean of the first season, \(b_0\) = average change per step, \(s_j=y_j-\ell_0\)
  3. for \(t=1,\dots,T\) do
  4. one-step forecast \(\hat y_t=\ell_{t-1}+b_{t-1}+s_{t-m}\); error \(e_t=y_t-\hat y_t\)
  5. update \(\ell_t,\ b_t,\ s_t\) with the three smoothing equations
  6. for \(j=1,\dots,h\): \(\hat y_{T+j}=\ell_T+j\,b_T+s_{T+j-m}\)
  7. return forecasts; choose \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) to minimise \(\sum e_t^2\)

5ARIMA and seasonal ARIMA

Choose the orders; the model is fitted to the training years and forecasts the last two. Lower AIC is better (only comparable between models with the same differencing).

ARIMA describes the stationary series left after differencing with two kinds of memory. An autoregressive part says the value depends on its own past; a moving-average part says it depends on past surprises (forecast errors):

$$w_t=c+\underbrace{\phi_1w_{t-1}+\dots+\phi_pw_{t-p}}_{\text{AR}(p)}+\underbrace{\theta_1\varepsilon_{t-1}+\dots+\theta_q\varepsilon_{t-q}}_{\text{MA}(q)}+\varepsilon_t,\qquad w_t=\nabla^d y_t .$$

That is ARIMA(\(p,d,q\)): I for “integrated”, because we forecast \(w\) and then add the differences back up. Seasonal ARIMA (\(p,d,q\))(\(P,D,Q\))\(_m\) adds the same three ideas at lag \(m\): \(w_t=\nabla^d\nabla_m^D y_t\) with terms in \(w_{t-m}\) and \(\varepsilon_{t-m}\). The default here, (0,1,1)(0,1,1)\(_{12}\), is Box and Jenkins’ famous “airline model”, and it is a strong baseline for monthly data with trend and season.

How to choose orders: difference until the autocorrelation plot (section 3) dies out quickly; a sharp cut-off in the partial autocorrelation after lag \(p\) suggests AR(\(p\)); a cut-off in the autocorrelation after lag \(q\) suggests MA(\(q\)). In practice, search a small grid and keep the lowest AIC, which is what auto_arima does.

Estimating MA terms needs the past errors, which depend on the coefficients. This page uses the Hannan–Rissanen trick: first fit a long AR model to get stand-in errors, then fit everything by least squares. Libraries maximise the exact Gaussian likelihood, which gives slightly different numbers.

Algorithm: fitting and forecasting ARIMA (Hannan–Rissanen)
  1. input series \(y\), orders \((p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_m\), horizon \(h\)
  2. \(w\leftarrow\) difference \(y\) \(d\) times, then seasonally \(D\) times
  3. fit a long AR model to \(w\) by least squares; \(\hat\varepsilon_t\leftarrow\) its residuals
  4. regress \(w_t\) on \(w_{t-1..p},\ w_{t-m..Pm},\ \hat\varepsilon_{t-1..q},\ \hat\varepsilon_{t-m..Qm}\) least squares
  5. for \(j=1..h\): \(\hat w_{T+j}\leftarrow\) the fitted equation, with future \(\varepsilon=0\)
  6. undo the differencing: add back \(y_{t-m}\) and \(y_{t-1}\) step by step
  7. return \(\hat y_{T+1..T+h}\) and AIC \(=T\log(\text{SSE}/T)+2k\)

6Gradient-boosted trees as forecasters

Boosted trees (100 depth-3 trees) trained on lag features, then forecasting the two held-back years one step at a time, feeding each prediction back in as the next lag. Switch the target to see why trees need help with trends.

Gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost; see bagging and boosting) won most of the large forecasting competitions of the last decade, such as the M5 retail competition (2020), mainly because they combine many related series and many features in one model. The recipe is the lag table from section 3:

  • features at time \(t\): \(y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, y_{t-3}, y_{t-12}\), the mean of the last 12 values and the month;
  • train any regressor on these rows;
  • forecast recursively: predict \(t+1\), put the prediction into the lag columns, predict \(t+2\), and so on (or train one model per horizon, the “direct” strategy).

The trap: a tree predicts the average of a leaf, so it can never output a value larger than anything it saw in training. With a rising trend the level model flattens out at the old maximum. The cure is to model something stationary, here the change from a year ago, \(y_t-y_{t-12}\), and add it back afterwards. Detrending or differencing the target is the single most important step when using trees for forecasting.

Algorithm: recursive forecasting with lag features
  1. input training series, lags \(L=\{1,2,3,12\}\), a regressor \(f\), horizon \(h\)
  2. build rows \((x_t,\ y_t)\) with \(x_t=(y_{t-\ell})_{\ell\in L}\), rolling mean, month
  3. target \(z_t=y_t-y_{t-12}\) stationary target, trees cannot extrapolate
  4. train \(f\) on the rows
  5. for \(j=1..h\) do
  6. build \(x_{T+j}\) from the history plus the forecasts so far
  7. \(\hat y_{T+j}\leftarrow f(x_{T+j})+y_{T+j-12}\); append it to the history
  8. return \(\hat y_{T+1..T+h}\)
Going deeper: global models and deep learning

With thousands of related series (products, stores, sensors), one “global” model trained on all of them usually beats one model per series, because rare patterns are learned from many examples. Deep models (DeepAR, N-BEATS, temporal fusion transformers) and recent pretrained time-series foundation models (TimesFM, Chronos, Moirai) follow the same idea at larger scale. On a single short series, however, ETS and ARIMA remain very hard to beat, as the backtest below usually shows.

7Backtesting: which method wins on this series?

Rolling-origin evaluation: each method is refitted at four cut-offs (years 7 to 10) and forecasts the next 12 months each time. Bars: mean absolute scaled error (MASE) per method; below 1 beats the seasonal-naive forecast “same month last year”.

One train/test split gives one noisy number. A backtest repeats the forecast from several origins, as in section 2, and averages. Report errors on a scale that compares across series:

$$\text{MASE}=\frac{\tfrac1h\sum_{j=1}^{h}|y_{T+j}-\hat y_{T+j}|}{\tfrac{1}{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T}|y_t-y_{t-m}|}.$$

The denominator is the in-sample error of the seasonal-naive forecast, so MASE below 1 means “better than copying last year”. Unlike percentage errors (MAPE), it does not explode when values are near zero and it treats over- and under-forecasts alike.

Always include the naive baselines: on many real series, especially short or noisy ones, they are surprisingly hard to beat. Reshape the series in section 1: with no trend and high noise the simple methods catch up; with a strong trend, the tree on levels falls behind.

Beyond point forecasts, judge prediction intervals too: a 95% interval should contain about 95% of outcomes in the backtest (coverage), and narrower is better only when coverage holds. Quantile loss and CRPS score whole forecast distributions.

8The same thing in Python

import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL
from statsmodels.tsa.holtwinters import ExponentialSmoothing
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX
y = pd.read_csv("sales.csv", index_col="month", parse_dates=True)["sales"].asfreq("MS")
train, test = y[:-24], y[-24:]                                    # split by time, never at random

STL(train, period=12).fit().plot()                                  # trend / season / remainder
hw  = ExponentialSmoothing(train, trend="add", seasonal="add", seasonal_periods=12).fit()
sar = SARIMAX(train, order=(0,1,1), seasonal_order=(0,1,1,12)).fit(disp=False)  # airline model
fc_hw, fc_sar = hw.forecast(24), sar.get_forecast(24).summary_frame()     # mean + intervals
