Linear Regression

Given a few houses with their sizes and prices, how would you predict the price of the next one? Linear regression answers with the simplest reasonable guess, a straight line, and a precise rule for choosing the best line. This page builds that rule from scratch: you can drag the data and watch every quantity update, then walk down the error surface with gradient descent and see how adding curves, ridge and lasso trade bias for variance.

What is linear regression, in plain words?

The idea

Suppose we have the living area and selling price of 47 houses, and we want to predict the price of a house that is about to go on sale. Plot price against area and the points drift upwards in a rough band. A natural first model is a straight line through that band: price \(\approx\beta_0+\beta_1\times\text{area}\). Linear regression is the method for choosing the two numbers \(\beta_0\) (the intercept) and \(\beta_1\) (the slope).

What makes a line "good"? For each house, the line's prediction \(\hat y_i\) misses the true price \(y_i\) by a residual \(e_i=y_i-\hat y_i\). We want the residuals small overall, and the standard way to say "small overall" is: make the sum of their squares as small as possible. That is least squares. Squaring treats over- and under-predictions alike, punishes big misses more than small ones, and, as we will see, makes the best line easy to compute.

The same recipe goes much further than straight lines. Use several inputs (area, bedrooms, age) and the line becomes a flat surface. Add \(x^2, x^3,\dots\) as extra inputs (polynomial features) and it fits curves. Add a penalty on large coefficients and it becomes ridge or the lasso, which resist overfitting.

How it works

  1. Choose the form of the prediction: \(\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\dots+\beta_dx_d\).
  2. Choose how to measure error: the sum (or mean) of squared residuals.
  3. Find the weights that make the error smallest: in one shot with the normal equations, or step by step with gradient descent.
  4. Check the fit with R², the residuals and, most importantly, on new data.

An everyday picture

Predicting sales from TV advertising spend (the classic Advertising dataset in the lab below). Each extra $1,000 of TV spend adds roughly the same number of sales, so a straight line through the scatter tells you both the trend and the typical return per dollar: that return is the slope.

2The maths, with your numbers

Everything below is computed from the points currently on the plot, using a straight line (degree 1). We go in three steps: write down what we are minimising, find where it is smallest, then ask how much to trust the answer.

Step 1: the model and the cost

We have \(n\) examples \((x_i,y_i)\). Our model says each \(y_i\) is a straight-line function of \(x_i\) plus an error \(\varepsilon_i\) we cannot predict. The cost of a choice of \(\beta\) is the residual sum of squares (RSS):

$$y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad \mathrm{RSS}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1x_i\big)^2=\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2$$

The second form is the same sum written with matrices. Stack the inputs into a design matrix \(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times 2}\): a column of ones (so that \(\beta_0\) gets added to every prediction) next to the column of \(x\)'s. Then all \(n\) predictions at once are \(X\beta\), and the RSS is the squared length of the residual vector \(y-X\beta\).

Step 2: where is the cost smallest?

RSS is a bowl-shaped (convex quadratic) function of \(\beta\), so its lowest point is where the slope is zero in every direction:

  1. \(\nabla_\beta\,\mathrm{RSS}=-2X^\top(y-X\beta)\)
    Differentiate the squared length; the chain rule brings down \(X^\top\). (See "Going deeper" for the step-by-step version.)
  2. \(X^\top(y-X\hat\beta)=0\)
    Set it to zero at the best \(\hat\beta\). In words: the residuals are uncorrelated with every column of \(X\).
  3. \(X^\top X\,\hat\beta=X^\top y\;\Rightarrow\;\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\)
    Rearrange. These are the normal equations; here they are with your data:

The inverse exists only if \(X\) has full column rank, meaning no feature is an exact combination of the others. If you include a house's area in square feet and in square metres, one column is a multiple of the other, \(X^\top X\) is singular, and infinitely many \(\beta\) fit equally well (see rank).

Algorithm: ordinary least squares
  1. input inputs \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) (each a vector of \(d\) features), targets \(y_1,\dots,y_n\)
  2. build \(X\): row \(i\) is \((1, x_{i1},\dots,x_{id})\) leading 1 for the intercept
  3. \(G\leftarrow X^\top X\); \(g\leftarrow X^\top y\) (d+1)×(d+1) matrix, (d+1) vector
  4. solve \(G\hat\beta=g\) use a solver, not an explicit inverse
  5. \(\hat y\leftarrow X\hat\beta\); \(e\leftarrow y-\hat y\)
  6. return \(\hat\beta\), \(\hat y\), \(e\)

Geometry: a projection

There is a picture behind step 2. Think of \(y\) as one point in \(n\)-dimensional space. Every choice of \(\beta\) gives predictions \(X\beta\), and these all lie in a flat plane (the column space of \(X\)). The best fit is the point of that plane closest to \(y\): its projection. Formally \(\hat y=X\hat\beta=Hy\) with the "hat matrix" \(H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\). The closest point is the one where the error sticks straight out of the plane, so the residual is orthogonal to every column: \(X^\top e=0\). The column of ones is one of them, which is why residuals always sum to zero when there is an intercept.

Going deeper: the gradient step by step, and why squared error

The gradient. Expand \(\mathrm{RSS}=(y-X\beta)^\top(y-X\beta)=y^\top y-2\beta^\top X^\top y+\beta^\top X^\top X\beta\). Two standard facts, \(\nabla_\beta(\beta^\top a)=a\) and \(\nabla_\beta(\beta^\top A\beta)=2A\beta\) for symmetric \(A\), give \(\nabla\mathrm{RSS}=-2X^\top y+2X^\top X\beta\). The Hessian is \(2X^\top X\), which is positive semi-definite because \(v^\top X^\top Xv=\lVert Xv\rVert^2\ge0\). So the function is convex, and any point with zero gradient is a global minimum.

A probabilistic reason for squares. Assume the errors are independent Gaussians, \(\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\). Then the probability density of seeing \(y_i\) is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\big(-\frac{(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\big)\), and the log-likelihood of all the data is

$$\ell(\beta)=-n\log(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2 .$$

Only the last term depends on \(\beta\), so maximising the likelihood is exactly minimising RSS. Least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise (see maximum likelihood). If the noise has heavier tails, other losses (absolute error, Huber) are more appropriate.

Step 3a: how good is the fit?

Start from the simplest possible model, "always predict the average \(\bar y\)". Its squared error is the total sum of squares (TSS). The line explains part of that variation (MSS) and leaves the rest unexplained (RSS). With an intercept, the two pieces add up exactly:

$$\underbrace{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}_{\mathrm{TSS}}=\underbrace{\sum(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2}_{\mathrm{MSS}}+\underbrace{\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}_{\mathrm{RSS}},\qquad R^2=1-\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}}$$

So \(R^2\) is the fraction of the variation in \(y\) that the model accounts for: 0 means no better than the average, 1 means a perfect fit. The split works because the residual vector is orthogonal to \(\hat y-\bar y\), so Pythagoras applies.

Step 3b: how much should we trust each coefficient?

If we collected a fresh sample of houses, we would get slightly different \(\hat\beta\). How much would they wobble? Estimate the noise level from the residuals (dividing by \(n-(p+1)\), because fitting \(p+1\) coefficients has used up that many degrees of freedom), then propagate it through the formula for \(\hat\beta\):

$$\hat\sigma^2=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-(p+1)},\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)=\hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1},\qquad t_j=\frac{\hat\beta_j}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_j)}$$

The standard error \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_j)\) is the square root of the \(j\)-th diagonal entry. The \(t\)-statistic asks how many standard errors \(\hat\beta_j\) is away from zero; it is a hypothesis test of "this feature has no effect".

A large \(|t|\) (roughly above 2) is evidence that the coefficient is not zero. Try the Advertising dataset to get a real-data regression table, then drag a point far away and watch the standard errors react.

Going deeper: where \(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) comes from

Substitute \(y=X\beta+\varepsilon\) into the normal-equation solution: \(\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\beta+\varepsilon)=\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon\). So \(\hat\beta\) is the truth plus a linear transformation \(A\varepsilon\) of the noise, with \(A=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\). Two consequences: \(\mathbb E[\hat\beta]=\beta\) (unbiased, since \(\mathbb E\varepsilon=0\)), and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\beta)=A\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)A^\top=\sigma^2AA^\top=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\).

Reading this formula tells you how to design data. The variance is small when \(X^\top X\) is "large": many examples, inputs spread widely, and features not strongly correlated with each other. Highly correlated features make \(X^\top X\) nearly singular, so its inverse, and the standard errors, blow up (multicollinearity). The Gauss–Markov theorem says that among all unbiased estimators that are linear in \(y\), least squares has the smallest variance.

3Gradient descent on the loss surface

The normal equations solve the problem in one shot, but they need a \((d+1)\times(d+1)\) linear system, which is expensive when there are millions of features, and they do not exist for most other models. Gradient descent is the general-purpose alternative: start with some guess for \(\beta\), and repeatedly take a small step in the direction that decreases the cost fastest, \(\beta\leftarrow\beta-\eta\,\nabla J\). For \(J(\beta_0,\beta_1)=\tfrac{1}{2n}\mathrm{RSS}\) that step is \(\beta\leftarrow\beta+\tfrac{\eta}{n}X^\top(y-X\beta)\). Click the contour plot to choose a starting point.

Contours are log-spaced levels of \(J\). The star is the closed-form \(\hat\beta\).

Look at the update one example at a time: \(\beta\leftarrow\beta+\eta\,(y_i-\hat y_i)\,x_i\). It has a pleasing logic. If the prediction for example \(i\) is nearly right, the error \(y_i-\hat y_i\) is tiny and \(\beta\) barely moves. If it is far off, \(\beta\) moves a lot, and in the direction that fixes the error. This is the LMS (least mean squares) or Widrow–Hoff rule.

Because the bowl is convex there is only one minimum, so gradient descent always reaches it, as long as the step size \(\eta\) is small enough. Too large and it overshoots and diverges (try a fraction above 1). The bowl's shape matters too: if \(x\) is on a very different scale from the column of ones, the bowl is a long narrow valley and descent zig-zags. Standardising \(x\) makes the contours rounder and descent much faster. The ratio of the bowl's steepest to flattest curvature is the condition number.

Algorithm: batch and stochastic gradient descent for least squares
  1. input \(X\) (\(n\) rows), \(y\), step size \(\eta\), number of passes \(T\)
  2. \(\beta\leftarrow 0\)
  3. for \(t=1\) to \(T\) do batch version: one step per pass over all data
  4. \(\beta\leftarrow\beta+\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)\,x_i\)
  5. stochastic (LMS) version: one step per example
  6. for \(t=1\) to \(T\) do
  7. for each \(i\) in a shuffled order do
  8. \(\beta\leftarrow\beta+\eta\,(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)\,x_i\) correction ∝ error
  9. return \(\beta\)
Going deeper: why the stability limit is \(2/\lambda_{\max}\)

Write \(H=\tfrac1nX^\top X\) (the Hessian of \(J\)) and let \(\delta=\beta-\hat\beta\) be the distance from the optimum. One step gives \(\delta\leftarrow(I-\eta H)\delta\). In the eigenbasis of \(H\) every component is multiplied by \(1-\eta\lambda_k\) per step. It shrinks only if \(|1-\eta\lambda_k|<1\), that is \(0<\eta<2/\lambda_k\). The largest eigenvalue sets the limit. With \(\eta\) at that limit, the slowest component shrinks by a factor \(1-2\lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max}\approx1-2/\kappa\) per step, so the number of steps needed grows with the condition number \(\kappa\). That is the zig-zag you see without standardising. See eigendecomposition for the same argument with powers of a matrix.

4Polynomial features and the bias–variance trade-off

A straight line cannot follow a curve. The fix is cheap: invent new features \(x^2, x^3,\dots\) and fit \(\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2+\dots+\beta_dx^d\). This is still linear regression, because the model is linear in the unknowns \(\beta\), so everything above still applies. But how high should the degree \(d\) go? Raising it always lowers the training error. The test error tells a different story.

training MSEtest MSE (fresh data from the same source)dashed line = your current degree

Imagine repeating the whole experiment many times: draw a new training set, fit the model, and predict at a fixed point \(x\). The average squared error of that prediction splits into three parts:

$$\mathbb E\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big]=\underbrace{\big(f(x)-\mathbb E\hat f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\hat f(x)}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}$$
  • Bias is how far the average fit is from the truth: the error of a model that is too simple to follow the true curve, however much data it gets.
  • Variance is how much the fit jumps around from one training set to the next: the error of a model so flexible that it follows the noise.
  • Noise \(\sigma^2\) is the part no model can remove.
  • Low degree: too rigid. High bias, low variance, so training and test error are both high (underfitting).
  • High degree: the curve chases noise. Low bias, high variance, so training error keeps falling while test error climbs (overfitting).
  • Regularisation (section 5) is the other dial: keep a high degree but shrink the coefficients.
Going deeper: deriving the decomposition

Fix \(x\) and write \(\bar f=\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]\), averaging over training sets. The new observation is \(y=f(x)+\varepsilon\) with noise independent of the training set. Then \(y-\hat f=(f-\bar f)+(\bar f-\hat f)+\varepsilon\). Square and take expectations. The three cross terms vanish: \(\mathbb E[\bar f-\hat f]=0\) by the definition of \(\bar f\), \(\mathbb E\varepsilon=0\), and \(\varepsilon\) is independent of \(\hat f\). What remains is \((f-\bar f)^2+\mathbb E(\bar f-\hat f)^2+\sigma^2\): bias², variance and noise. The derivation needs nothing about polynomials; it holds for any model and any learning algorithm under squared loss. Very large models (deep networks) can show "double descent", where test error falls again past the point where the model can fit the training data exactly.

5Ridge and lasso: shrinkage versus sparsity

An overfit high-degree polynomial usually has huge coefficients that cancel each other out. So a natural cure is to make large coefficients expensive: add a penalty on the size of \(\beta\) to the cost. The features are standardised first so that "size" means the same thing for every feature, and the intercept is not penalised. The shape of the penalty changes what the solution looks like.

$$\text{Ridge: }\min_\beta\ \tfrac1n\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2\ \Rightarrow\ \hat\beta=(X^\top X+n\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$$

Ridge (L2 penalty) keeps the closed form: the same derivation as section 2 just adds \(n\lambda I\) to \(X^\top X\). As a bonus, that matrix is always invertible for \(\lambda>0\), which cures the collinearity problem. Every coefficient is shrunk towards zero, but none becomes exactly zero.

$$\text{Lasso: }\min_\beta\ \tfrac1{2n}\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1\ \Rightarrow\ \beta_j\leftarrow S_\lambda\!\Big(\tfrac1n x_j^\top r_{(j)}\Big),\ S_\lambda(z)=\operatorname{sign}(z)(|z|-\lambda)_+$$

Lasso (L1 penalty) has no closed form, because \(|\beta_j|\) has a kink at zero. It is solved one coefficient at a time by coordinate descent. Hold all other coefficients fixed, compute how strongly feature \(j\) correlates with what is left to explain (the partial residual \(r_{(j)}\)), then apply the soft-thresholding operator \(S_\lambda\). If that correlation is weaker than \(\lambda\), the coefficient is set to exactly zero. The lasso therefore does feature selection automatically.

Algorithm: lasso by coordinate descent
  1. input standardised \(X\) (columns \(x_j\) with \(\tfrac1n\lVert x_j\rVert^2=1\)), centred \(y\), penalty \(\lambda\)
  2. \(\beta\leftarrow 0\); \(r\leftarrow y\) residual
  3. repeat
  4. for \(j=1\) to \(d\) do
  5. \(z\leftarrow\tfrac1nx_j^\top r+\beta_j\) correlation with the partial residual
  6. \(\beta_j^{\text{new}}\leftarrow\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,0)\) soft-threshold
  7. \(r\leftarrow r-x_j(\beta_j^{\text{new}}-\beta_j)\); \(\beta_j\leftarrow\beta_j^{\text{new}}\)
  8. until no coefficient changes by more than a tolerance
  9. return \(\beta\)

Why the diamond gives zeros

There is an equivalent way to state both problems: minimise RSS subject to a budget, \(\lVert\beta\rVert\le t\). The RSS contours are ellipses around the unpenalised (OLS) solution. Grow the ellipse until it first touches the allowed region. For the L2 circle, the first contact is usually at a generic point with no zero coordinates. The L1 diamond has corners sticking out along the axes, and the ellipse usually hits a corner first, where one coordinate is exactly zero. Drag the OLS point (the star) on the right-hand plot to see it.

Going deeper: the penalties as priors

Penalties have a Bayesian reading. Put a prior on the weights and maximise the posterior instead of the likelihood: \(\log p(\beta\mid\text{data})=\log p(\text{data}\mid\beta)+\log p(\beta)+\text{const}\). With Gaussian noise the first term is \(-\mathrm{RSS}/2\sigma^2\). A Gaussian prior \(\beta_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) contributes \(-\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2/2\tau^2\), giving ridge with \(\lambda\propto\sigma^2/\tau^2\). A Laplace prior \(p(\beta_j)\propto e^{-|\beta_j|/b}\) contributes \(-\lVert\beta\rVert_1/b\), giving the lasso. The Laplace density has a sharp peak at zero, which is the probabilistic version of the diamond's corners. More on this on the regularisation page and in Bayesian updating.

6The same thing in Python

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, Ridge, Lasso
from sklearn.preprocessing import PolynomialFeatures, StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

# closed form
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(x)), x])
beta = np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y)          # (X^T X)^{-1} X^T y, without forming the inverse
e = y - X @ beta; RSS = e @ e; R2 = 1 - RSS / np.sum((y - y.mean())**2)
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(RSS / (len(y) - 2) * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)))

# gradient descent (LMS)
b = np.zeros(2)
for _ in range(1000):
    b += 0.01 / len(y) * X.T @ (y - X @ b)

# polynomial + penalty, as on this page
model = make_pipeline(PolynomialFeatures(degree=6, include_bias=False), StandardScaler(),
                      Lasso(alpha=0.1))     # or Ridge(alpha=...), LinearRegression()
model.fit(x.reshape(-1, 1), y)

Note that scikit-learn's Ridge(alpha) uses \(\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2+\alpha\lVert\beta\rVert^2\) (no \(1/n\)), so its \(\alpha\) equals \(n\lambda\) here. Lasso(alpha) uses the same \(\tfrac1{2n}\) scaling as this page.
