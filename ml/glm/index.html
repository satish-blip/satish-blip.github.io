Generalised linear models: how insurers price risk

Linear regression assumes the answer can be any number and the noise is the same everywhere. Claim counts are whole numbers, claim costs are positive and skewed, and a policy on risk for six months should expect half the claims. A generalised linear model keeps the simplicity of a weighted sum of factors but lets you choose the distribution and how the sum maps to the mean. With a log link, every factor becomes a multiplier: the rating table an insurer actually uses.

What is a GLM, in plain words?

The idea

A generalised linear model predicts the average outcome for each row as a function of a weighted sum of its features, exactly like linear regression, but with two extra choices: which probability distribution the outcome follows (Poisson for counts, gamma for costs) and which link function connects the weighted sum to the average.

How it works

  1. Write the linear predictor \(\eta=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\dots\), as in linear regression.
  2. Turn it into the mean with the inverse link, for example \(\mu=e^{\eta}\).
  3. Say how the data scatter around that mean: the family and its variance function \(V(\mu)\).
  4. Find the \(\beta\)s by maximum likelihood, which here is a sequence of weighted least-squares fits.

An everyday picture

A car insurer charges a base rate, then multiplies it by a factor for your age, another for your car, another for where you live. A 19-year-old pays 2.2× the base, a sports car 1.4×, London 1.3×: in total 2.2 × 1.4 × 1.3 = 4.0× the base. That multiplicative tariff is exactly a Poisson or gamma GLM with a log link; the multipliers are \(e^{\beta}\).

Why insurers use them
  • Every factor's effect is a readable multiplier
  • Handles exposure, counts and skewed costs properly
  • Standard errors and tests for every relativity
  • Regulators and underwriters can audit them
Watch out for
  • Correlated factors (one-way views mislead)
  • Thin levels with wild relativities
  • Over-dispersion hiding behind “significant” p-values
  • Effects that drift from year to year

1Three parts: distribution, linear predictor, link

Every GLM is three choices. The random component: \(y_i\) follows a distribution from the exponential family with mean \(\mu_i\) and variance \(\phi\,V(\mu_i)/w_i\). The systematic component: a linear predictor \(\eta_i=\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\) (plus an offset, section 4). The link: \(g(\mu_i)=\eta_i\).

$$\begin{aligned} y_i&\sim\text{ExpFamily}(\mu_i,\ \phi/w_i)\\ g(\mu_i)&=o_i+\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\\ \operatorname{Var}(y_i)&=\frac{\phi\,V(\mu_i)}{w_i}\end{aligned}$$

Linear regression is the special case: normal distribution, identity link, \(V(\mu)=1\). Change any one of the three and you have a different GLM. On the right, the mean curve \(\mu(x)=e^{\beta_0+\beta_1x}\) is the same in every family; what changes is how the data spread around it. Poisson spread grows like \(\sqrt\mu\); gamma spread grows in proportion to \(\mu\) (a constant coefficient of variation); normal spread stays fixed.

Going deeper: the exponential family

All these distributions can be written \(f(y)=\exp\!\big(\frac{w(y\theta-b(\theta))}{\phi}+c(y,\phi)\big)\). The mean is \(\mu=b'(\theta)\) and the variance \(\phi\,b''(\theta)/w\), so \(V(\mu)=b''(\theta)\). The canonical link is the one that makes \(\theta=\eta\): log for Poisson, logit for binomial, identity for normal, \(-1/\mu\) for gamma. Pricing uses the log link even for gamma because a multiplicative tariff matters more than mathematical convenience.

Same mean, different scatter

2Families and their variance functions

The family is mostly a statement about variance: how much the outcome is expected to wobble at a given mean. That matters because the fit gives each row a weight inversely proportional to its variance (section 5).

3The log link turns coefficients into relativities

With \(\log\mu=\beta_0+\sum_j\beta_jx_j\), taking exponentials gives a product:

$$\mu=e^{\beta_0}\times e^{\beta_1x_1}\times e^{\beta_2x_2}\times\cdots$$

For a categorical factor coded with 0/1 dummies, \(e^{\beta_j}\) is the multiplier for that level compared with the base level. Insurers call these multipliers relativities, and \(e^{\beta_0}\) the base rate. The premium is base rate × one relativity per factor × exposure.

Because effects multiply, a 30% loading for young drivers is 30% whether the car is cheap or expensive, which matches how risk behaves far better than adding a fixed £ amount. A coefficient of 0.262 means \(e^{0.262}=1.30\): 30% more.

A 95% interval for a relativity is \(\exp(\hat\beta\pm1.96\,\text{SE})\): asymmetric around the relativity, as it should be for a multiplier.

4Exposure, offsets and weights

Exposure is how long a policy was on risk, in years. A policy that ran for half a year should expect half the claims of an identical full-year policy. The model handles this with an offset: a term added to the linear predictor whose coefficient is fixed at 1.

$$\log\mu_i=\log e_i+\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu_i=e_i\,e^{\mathbf x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta}$$

So the model predicts a rate per year and multiplies by exposure. For a Poisson GLM there is an equivalent form: model the rate \(y_i/e_i\) with prior weight \(w_i=e_i\) and no offset. The coefficients are identical.

An offset can carry any known effect. Pricing teams often use \(\log(\text{current premium})\) as the offset to model only the change to an existing tariff, or fix a factor's relativities from another source and estimate the rest.

Locking a relativity. The same trick answers “if I set this relativity by hand, how do the others change?” Move the level out of the design and put \(\log(\text{new relativity})\) into its rows' offset; refit, and every other coefficient re-estimates around the fixed one. Factors correlated with it move most. Before refitting, a quick estimate comes from the covariance of the fit: \(\Delta\boldsymbol\beta_{\text{others}}\approx\Sigma_{oj}\,\Sigma_{jj}^{-1}\,\Delta\beta_j\). Prior weights \(w_i\) say how much information a row carries: \(\operatorname{Var}(y_i)=\phi V(\mu_i)/w_i\). An average over more claims is less noisy, so it gets more weight.

Going deeper: why cloglog matches Poisson

If claims arrive as a Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\), the chance of at least one claim in time \(e\) is \(1-e^{-\lambda e}\). Then \(\log(-\log(1-p))=\log\lambda+\log e\): the complementary log-log link with a \(\log e\) offset gives the same \(\beta\)s as the Poisson frequency model, read from yes/no data.

5How the fit works: iteratively reweighted least squares

There is no closed form for \(\hat\beta\) except in the normal case. Instead, IRLS linearises the link around the current fit and solves a weighted least-squares problem, again and again. It is Newton's method (Fisher scoring) in disguise, and it usually converges in 4–8 steps.

Algorithm: IRLS for a GLM
  1. input design \(X\), response \(y\), prior weights \(w\), offset \(o\), family \(V\), link \(g\)
  2. start with \(\mu_i\leftarrow(y_i+\bar y)/2\), \(\eta_i\leftarrow g(\mu_i)\)
  3. repeat
  4. working response \(z_i\leftarrow\eta_i-o_i+(y_i-\mu_i)\,g'(\mu_i)\)
  5. working weight \(W_i\leftarrow w_i\big/\big(V(\mu_i)\,g'(\mu_i)^2\big)\) log link: \(W_i=w_i\mu_i^2/V(\mu_i)\)
  6. \(\boldsymbol\beta\leftarrow(X^\top WX)^{-1}X^\top W\mathbf z\) weighted least squares
  7. \(\eta\leftarrow o+X\boldsymbol\beta\), \(\mu\leftarrow g^{-1}(\eta)\), deviance \(D\leftarrow\sum_iw_i\,d(y_i,\mu_i)\)
  8. until \(D\) stops changing
  9. return \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) and \(\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta})=\hat\phi(X^\top WX)^{-1}\)

For Poisson with a log link the working weight is \(W_i=w_i\mu_i\): a policy expected to have more claims carries more information. For gamma with a log link \(W_i=w_i\): on the log scale every claim counts equally, however large.

6Rating factors: base levels, bands, merges and interactions

Dummy coding and the base level

A factor with \(k\) levels gets \(k-1\) columns of 0/1 dummies. The level without a column is the base: its relativity is 1 and the intercept absorbs it. Choosing the level with the most exposure as the base keeps every other level's standard error small, because each is estimated relative to a well-measured reference.

Bands for continuous variables

Age, vehicle age and mileage are usually cut into bands (17–20, 21–24, …) so each band gets its own relativity without forcing a shape. Fewer, wider bands are more stable; narrower bands follow the curve more closely. Alternatives: a straight line on the log scale \(e^{\beta x}\), a power curve \(x^\beta\) (use \(\log x\)), a quadratic, or splines.

Merging levels

Levels with overlapping confidence intervals and similar relativities can be merged into one. That saves parameters and makes the tariff simpler without losing fit.

Aliasing

If one column is an exact combination of others (for example two factors that always move together, or a level that exists only in combination with another factor's level), \(X^\top WX\) is singular and the coefficient cannot be estimated. Software detects this and drops the column: it is aliased. Near-aliasing (high correlation) does not break the fit but inflates standard errors.

Interactions

Main effects assume the age relativity is the same for every car. An interaction age × vehicle group lets young drivers in fast cars get their own extra multiplier. Each pair adds \((k_1-1)(k_2-1)\) parameters, so add only interactions you can justify and that survive a likelihood-ratio test.

Going deeper: why one-hot with all \(k\) levels fails

With an intercept, the \(k\) dummies of one factor sum to the intercept column, so \(X\) does not have full rank and \(X^\top WX\) has no inverse. Dropping one dummy (the base) restores full rank. The same holds for every factor, which is why a model with \(F\) categorical factors has \(1+\sum_f(k_f-1)\) parameters.

7Deviance, dispersion and testing a factor

The deviance measures how far the fitted model is from a “saturated” model that fits every row exactly: \(D=2\big[\ell(\text{saturated})-\ell(\hat\beta)\big]=\sum_iw_i\,d(y_i,\hat\mu_i)\). It plays the role of the residual sum of squares; for the normal family it is the residual sum of squares.

$$\text{Poisson: } d(y,\mu)=2\Big[y\log\frac{y}{\mu}-(y-\mu)\Big]\qquad\text{Gamma: } d(y,\mu)=2\Big[\frac{y-\mu}{\mu}-\log\frac{y}{\mu}\Big]$$

Dispersion. The Pearson estimate is \(\hat\phi=\frac{1}{n-p}\sum_i\frac{w_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2}{V(\hat\mu_i)}\). For Poisson it should be near 1. Values well above 1 mean over-dispersion: real claim counts vary more than Poisson because policies differ in ways the factors do not capture. The relativities are still fine, but every standard error is too small by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\), so factors look more significant than they are. Fix: quasi-Poisson or negative binomial.

Is a factor worth keeping? Fit with and without it. The increase in deviance is a likelihood-ratio statistic:

$$\frac{D_{\text{without}}-D_{\text{with}}}{\phi}\ \sim\ \chi^2_{\Delta p}\qquad\text{or, with }\hat\phi\text{ estimated,}\qquad F=\frac{(D_{\text{without}}-D_{\text{with}})/\Delta p}{\hat\phi}$$

AIC \(=-2\ell+2p\) and BIC \(=-2\ell+p\log n\) compare models on the same data, trading fit for parameters. BIC penalises more and picks smaller models on large portfolios.

Statistical significance is not the only test. A factor should also be stable over time (section 10), make sense to underwriters, and be legal to use.

8Pure premium in one model: Tweedie, and frequency × severity

Most policies have no claims, so cost per year has a big spike at exactly 0 and a long right tail. A Tweedie distribution with \(1<p<2\) is exactly that: a Poisson number of claims, each with a gamma-distributed size.

$$y=\sum_{k=1}^{N}Z_k,\quad N\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda),\quad Z_k\sim\text{Gamma},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(y)=\phi\mu^p,\quad \lambda=\frac{\mu^{2-p}}{\phi(2-p)}$$

As \(p\to1\) it behaves like Poisson (frequency dominates); as \(p\to2\) like gamma (severity dominates). \(p\) is usually chosen by profiling the likelihood; motor pure premium often lands near 1.5–1.7.

The classic alternative is two models: a Poisson frequency model and a gamma severity model, multiplied. That lets each part have its own factors (vehicle value matters for cost, not for how often you crash), at the price of maintaining two models. 9The one-way trap: why a multivariate model at all

A one-way analysis divides claims by exposure for each level of one factor. It is the first thing every pricing analyst looks at, and it misleads whenever factors are correlated. Young drivers rarely have a no-claims discount, so the one-way “no NCD” loading also contains part of the young-driver effect, and vice versa. Both get double-counted.

The GLM fits all factors at once, so each relativity is the effect holding the others fixed. On the right, 20,000 synthetic policies are generated with known relativities (young 1.8×, no NCD 1.4×). Increase the overlap between the two groups and watch the one-way estimates drift away from the truth while the GLM stays on it.

The same idea, in general, is omitted-variable bias. Cramér's V between factors (on the studio's One-way tab) shows where to expect it.

10Validation: lift, Gini, double lift, consistency

Hold out data. Fit on some policies, judge on others. For a tariff that will price next year's business, an out-of-time test (train on 2020–23, test on 2024) is the honest check; it also exposes trends the model does not contain.

Lift chart. Sort test policies by predicted rate, cut into 10 groups of equal exposure, and plot actual against predicted per group. A good model has a steep, well-tracked line.

Gini. Sort from lowest to highest predicted risk and plot the cumulative share of claims against the cumulative share of exposure (the Lorenz curve). Gini \(=1-2\times\)area under it: 0 for random ordering, higher for better ranking. It measures ranking only, so a model can have a good Gini and a bad overall level: check actual ÷ expected too.

Double lift. To compare two models, sort by the ratio of their predictions. Where they disagree most, which one does the actual experience follow?

Actual ÷ expected by factor. For every column, in the model or not, compare actual with predicted. A pattern in an unused column says it should be in the model; a drift across years says the model needs a trend.

Against the current premium. The monograph's double lift sorts policies by sort ratio = new model ÷ current premium and plots actual, model and current premium (each indexed to its average) per bucket; the loss ratio chart uses the same sort and shows the actual loss ratio under today's premium, which should be flat if today's rates were right. Its simple quantile plot is judged on accuracy, monotonicity and vertical distance.

Consistency. Fit factor × year and plot the relativities per year. Lines that cross or wander mean the effect is not stable, and its relativity will not hold next year.

11Thin data: credibility and penalties

A region with 30 policy-years and 5 claims has an observed relativity of, say, 1.6, but that number is mostly noise. Actuaries have long blended such estimates with a prior using credibility: \(\hat r=Z\cdot r_\text{observed}+(1-Z)\cdot r_\text{prior}\), with \(Z=\frac{E}{E+k}\) growing with exposure \(E\).

A GLM with a ridge (L2) penalty does the same thing automatically: it minimises \(-\ell(\beta)/n+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\beta\|^2\), which pulls every coefficient towards 0, so every relativity towards 1. Levels with lots of data barely move; thin levels move a lot. A lasso (L1) penalty can set a level's coefficient exactly to 0, merging it into the base. Both are on the studio's Model tab.

Going deeper: credibility is a Bayesian posterior

Put a normal prior \(\beta\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) on a level's log-relativity. The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of the data's estimate and 0, with weight \(Z=\frac{1/\text{SE}^2}{1/\text{SE}^2+1/\tau^2}\). Since \(\text{SE}^2\propto1/E\), this is \(Z=E/(E+k)\) with \(k\) set by \(\tau\): Bühlmann credibility, and ridge regression with \(\lambda\propto1/\tau^2\). See the Bayesian page.

12The same thing in Python

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, statsmodels.api as sm, statsmodels.formula.api as smf
df = pd.read_csv("motor_portfolio.csv")
df["age_band"] = pd.cut(df.driver_age, [17, 21, 25, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 99], right=False).astype(str)

# frequency: Poisson, log link, log(exposure) offset; base level = most exposure
freq = smf.glm("claim_count ~ C(age_band, Treatment('[40, 50)')) + C(vehicle_group, Treatment('C')) + C(region)",
               data=df, family=sm.families.Poisson(), offset=np.log(df.exposure)).fit()
print(freq.summary()); print(np.exp(freq.params))          # relativities
print("Pearson chi2/df", freq.pearson_chi2 / freq.df_resid)  # over-dispersion check
qp = smf.glm(..., family=sm.families.Poisson(), offset=np.log(df.exposure)).fit(scale="X2")  # quasi-Poisson SEs

# severity: gamma, log link, weight = claim count, policies with claims only
c = df[df.claim_count > 0].assign(sev=lambda d: d.claim_amount / d.claim_count)
sev = smf.glm("sev ~ C(vehicle_group) + C(region)", data=c, family=sm.families.Gamma(sm.families.links.Log()), var_weights=c.claim_count).fit()

# pure premium in one model: Tweedie, weight = exposure
df["pp"] = df.claim_amount / df.exposure
tw = smf.glm("pp ~ C(age_band) + C(vehicle_group) + C(region)", data=df, family=sm.families.Tweedie(var_power=1.6), var_weights=df.exposure).fit()
