From the chain ladder to stochastic reserving

A reserve is the money an insurer sets aside for claims that have happened but are not yet paid. The stages go from the simplest estimate of it to models that say how wrong that estimate could be. Every panel has a </> Code button with the same calculation in Python, and every setting a ? that explains it.

  1. Triangle. Start with the Taylor & Ashe triangle (the book’s example) or a synthetic one, or upload your own. Look at the development curves first: the methods assume every year develops alike.
  2. Development factors. The chain ladder averages each column of link ratios into a factor. Choose the average, click ratios to leave them out, and add a tail for development after the last column.
  3. Deterministic methods. Chain ladder trusts the claims paid so far; the expected loss ratio method trusts the premium; Bornhuetter–Ferguson and Cape Cod blend the two by how developed each year is. Type premiums and prior loss ratios in the table.
  4. Mack. The same chain-ladder reserve with its standard error, split into process error (randomness to come) and estimation error (uncertain factors). A lognormal turns it into percentiles.
  5. ODP & bootstrap. The over-dispersed Poisson GLM gives the chain ladder again, as a statistical model. The bootstrap resamples its residuals thousands of times to get the whole distribution of the reserve, by year and in total.
  6. One-year risk. Merz–Wüthrich: how much the estimate could move in the next 12 months, the horizon Solvency II uses.
  7. Compare & export. Every method side by side with its error and percentiles, the checks against the book, a CSV of the results and the full Python script.

Basic and Advanced

The Basic / Advanced switch keeps the everyday path short. Basic covers the triangle, the chain ladder with exclusions and a tail, BF, Cape Cod and the expected loss ratio method, Mack with a lognormal distribution, the ODP model and its bootstrap, and the one-year view. Advanced adds, each marked with a purple Advanced tag: regression factors, fitting the tail from a chosen period and smoothing single factors with the curve, generalised Cape Cod (decay), Benktander, Mack’s α, the last-σ rule, tail σ and se, the cross-product term, residual plots by fitted value, origin, development and calendar year, the degrees-of-freedom adjustment, non-constant scale, cash flows by calendar year, the Mack bootstrap, Clark’s loglogistic and Weibull growth curves (LDF and Cape Cod, capped or not) and gamma, Gaussian and inverse Gaussian GLMs.

Reading the results

  • Standard error (prediction error) is the typical distance between the reserve and what will really be paid. The CV is that as a share of the reserve: 13% for Mack on Taylor & Ashe.
  • Mack, ODP and the bootstrap give the same reserve but different errors, because they assume different things about the variance. Big differences are worth understanding before choosing.
  • The 99.5th percentile of the reserve over the whole run-off is not the Solvency II capital: that uses the one-year view (stage 6), which is usually about half the variance.
  • Check the residuals (Advanced): a trend by calendar year is inflation the chain ladder does not model, and the bootstrap copies whatever pattern the residuals have.

Adapted from: David Hindley, Claims Reserving in General Insurance, Cambridge University Press (International Series on Actuarial Science), 2017. The methods, worked examples and table numbers on this page follow that book; section numbers beside each panel point to it. Also: Taylor & Ashe (1983) for the data; Mack (1993, 1999); England & Verrall (1999, 2002, 2006); Merz & Wüthrich (2008); Clark (2003).
